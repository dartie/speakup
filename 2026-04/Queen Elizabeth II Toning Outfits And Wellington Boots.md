As Britain marks the centenary of Queen Elizabeth II’s birth on 21 April 1926, public memory [turns not only to](## "rivolgersi a, ricordare") her decades of service but also to the unmistakable visual identity she cultivated. From her earliest years as [heir](## "erede") to the throne to her seven decades as monarch, the [late](## "defunta") Queen projected an image that was both consistent and instantly recognisable. Carefully curated and deeply symbolic, her [wardrobe](## "guardaroba") became part of how the world perceived her and, by association, the United Kingdom and even the Commonwealth of Nations.

## A ROYAL STORY
As part of the centenary celebrations, the King’s Gallery, a public art gallery at Buckingham Palace, is [hosting](## "ospitare, accogliere") the largest exhibition ever dedicated to her fashion sense. Entitled Queen Elizabeth II: Her Life in Style, it includes over two hundred items that, according to the Royal Collection Trust, “[chart](## "tracciare, mostrare") the remarkable story of Britain’s longest-reigning monarch through clothing worn in all ten decades of her life: from birth to adulthood, from princess to queen, and from [off-duty](## "fuori servizio, nel tempo libero") style to diplomatic dressing for the global stage.” 

## ICONIC ITEMS
Among the most iconic items are the [bridesmaid](## "damigella d’onore") dress the young princess wore to her uncle’s wedding in 1934; the wedding dress she wore in 1947; and the Coronation dress she wore in 1953. Also on display are “never-before-seen design sketches, [fabric](## "stoffa") samples, and handwritten correspondence that reveal the behind-the-scenes process of dressing the most famous woman in the world and [shed new light on](## "far luce su") the [late](## "defunta") Queen’s close involvement in the creation of her [wardrobe](## "guardaroba").”
A [bridesmaid](## "damigella d’onore")’s dress by Edward Molyneux worn by Elizabeth II as a child in 1934 when Princess Elizabeth of York.

## GOOD FOR UK FASHION
Commenting on the exhibition, its curator, Caroline de Guitaut, said, “Over the course of Queen Elizabeth II’s remarkably long reign, her distinctive style became instantly recognisable around the world, [bolstering](## "sostenere") the British fashion industry and influencing generations of designers and [couturiers](## "stilisti di alta moda").”

Hazel Clark, Professor of Design Studies and Fashion Studies at Parsons School of Design in New York City, reflects on the [late](## "defunta") Queen’s uniquely British style and her enduring fashion [legacy](## "eredità, lascito").
**Hazel Clark:** Obviously, the Queen was very [long-lived](## "longeva"), and obviously she came to the throne when she was very young. But over time, she just developed a style which I always felt she seemed very comfortable in the way she looked, be it for a gala event or when she was out in Scotland in the countryside of Balmoral. I mean, she was famous for certain items, like the famous [headscarf](## "foulard, fazzoletto per la testa"), which she [fastened](## "legare, allacciare") under the [chin](## "mento"), and as well as wearing Barbour jackets, et cetera. But I think something like the [headscarf](## "foulard, fazzoletto per la testa") from the 1950s, she carried it on way beyond it being a fashion. She became known for being what would be called ‘very [put-together](## "curata, elegante")’. She tended to favour, in [late](## "defunta")r life, wearing distinctly mat[chin](## "mento")g outfits, coats and dresses, which were made in the same colour, same [fabric](## "stoffa"), distinctive hats, always carried her handbag, and of course, those very bright colours that she’s very famous for wearing. 
Queen Elizabeth in 1956 wearing an evening gown designed by Norman Hartnell.

## STRIKING CONTRAST
There was always a [striking](## "evidente, notevole") contrast between the Queen’s formal [wardrobe](## "guardaroba") and what she wore in the countryside. Clark says her [off-duty](## "fuori servizio, nel tempo libero") style reflected the life she loved away from the palace.
**Hazel Clark:** She was a country woman. Obviously, the family had country estates. She [rode horses](## "andare a cavallo"), she enjoyed walking. So she was really wearing functional country wear, which was very classic in England and Scotland: Wellington boots, as they’re called in the UK, [rubber](## "di gomma") boots and [tweeds](## "capi in tweed"). So it was functional clothes to keep you warm and dry.

## STARK AND COORDINATED
Some of the Queen’s most recognisable looks consisted of carefully-coordinated stark colours. As Hazel Clark explains, everything from her coat to her umbrella was chosen to create a consistent, instantly identifiable style.
**Hazel Clark:**I think one thing that did [stand out](## "spiccare, risaltare") with the Queen was these [toning outfits](## "completi in tonalità coordinate"), which are obviously made specially for her. So the dress and the coat in the same colour, then the mat[chin](## "mento")g hat, always wore the handbag, and even the mat[chin](## "mento")g umbrella, the clear umbrella she carried, which had a mat[chin](## "mento")g colour around the [rim](## "bordo"), which would match, you know, yellow for the yellow outfit, blue for the blue, et cetera. So that sense of everything being very together, that sort of sense of coherence and consistency. And also colours that, of course, stood out because she was a [petite](## "minuta, di bassa statura") woman and she wanted to be seen in the [crowd](## "folla"), or from the [crowd](## "folla"). But I think with the country items, it was very much following the classic British upper-class country clothes that they would wear for the weekend when they were hunting or shooting or fishing in the country.

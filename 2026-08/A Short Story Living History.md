# A Short Story: Living History   [B1]

Grazie a un insolito compito scolastico e a un misterioso libro donatole dalla sua insegnante, Orla capirà quanto passato e presente siano molto più simili di quanto immagini, nonostante i cambiamenti tecnologici.

“Disco Saturday?” Orla texted her friend Maeve.
Students were not permitted to use their phones in class. But this was a history class, the most boring class of all, and Maeve needed a distraction.
“[Ya](## "sì")… Aidan,” responded Maeve, followed by a emoji. Aidan was a boy Maeve had met at last week’s disco and was hoping to see again.
Orla responded with a emoji, then [realised](## "rendersi conto") the teacher was saying her name.
“Huh?” said Orla, [shoving her phone back under](## "infilare sotto") her history book.
On the class projector was an old photo of her school, Newtown School in Waterford, which was one of the oldest schools in Ireland, founded in 1798. The photo was almost a hundred years old and showed black-and-white people in a black-and-white world.
“What do you think their lives were like?” the teacher, Mrs. Hartnett, asked Orla.
“Erm, I don’t know…”
Mrs. Hartnett was a new teacher at the school, and she reminded Orla of an Irish Mary Poppins, with her long, elegant dresses, patient nature and gentle expression. And at least she was trying to [engage](## "coinvolgere") the students, with photos of a place they knew. Still, Orla couldn’t identify with the students in the black-and-white photo, whose world was nothing like hers, with its internet and smartphones, and electric cars.
“Different,” she said, trying to answer Mrs. Hartnett’s question. “[Dull](## "noioso"), boring, antiquated…”
“I see,” said Mrs. Hartnett. “Does everyone agree with Orla’s evaluation?”
"Just like Orla and her friends, the students concluded that 21st-century students were boring and antiquated, and nothing like them."
The other students agreed.
“Imagine not even having a phone?!” said one of them. “They must have been like [grannies](## "nonne") at our age.”
“Yeah,” said another, laughing. “Teenage [grannies](## "nonne").”
“Let’s explore that,” said Mrs. Hartnett. “For your homework, I want you to interview someone who grew up before the internet. Ask them about their lives as teenagers.”
The students [groaned](## "lamentarsi"), and Mrs. Hartnett laughed.
“It won’t be that bad, I promise,” she said.
Orla wasn’t so sure. It sounded like another boring history project to her.

As the students were leaving the class, Mrs. Hartnett called Orla’s name again.
“Could I speak with you, please?” she asked.
“Oh no,” thought Orla, [certain](## "sicura") that Mrs. Hartnett had seen her using her phone and was going to [punish](## "punire") her for it.
“I know you find history boring…” said Mrs. Hartnett.
“But that’s no excuse to use your phone in class,” Orla expected her to say next.
Instead she said, “… And I want to make it more interesting for you, to bring it to life. I have a book that can do that. So, I’m giving you the option of [either doing the homework or](## "o… oppure…") reading this book.”
 “Well…”. Orla didn’t want to read a long history book. That would be even more boring than doing the homework. But to her surprise, Mrs. Hartnett offered her a book that was only a few pages long.
After school, Orla decided to read Mrs. Hartnett’s book while her mother drove her to her grandmother’s house.
On the first page were two images — one was the old photo of Newtown School that she’d seen in class and the other was an illustration of the school in 2150, with flying cars and humanoid robots.
As Orla stared at the page, her eyes started to close, and she was soon transported into the old photo, which transformed from black-and-white to colour as it came to life.
“Hey Mary, Rob’s [staring](## "fissare") at you again,” said one of the students to another.
“No, he’s not,” said Mary [blushing](## "arrossire"), then [glanced](## "gettare uno sguardo") at an attractive boy who was, indeed, [staring](## "fissare") at her.
Orla observed the other students, all talking about the things that she and her friends talked about — boys, girls, fashion, sport…
When she had first seen the photo of them, all she’d seen was how different they were to her, with their traditional uniforms and [dated](## "antiquati") hairstyles. But now, inside this strange dream, she [realised](## "rendersi conto") how similar they were, that the teenage experience was universal, and not defined by time.
As she arrived at this conclusion, the scene around her [faded](## "svanire") and was replaced by another. This time, she was inside the futuristic photo, where she followed the students into a classroom, marvelling at their strangeness, at the technology incorporated into their clothes and bodies.
In the classroom, instead of a teacher there was a robot, who was projecting a video of Newtown School in the early 21st century. Just like Mrs. Hartnett, the robot asked the students to imagine the lives of students at that time. And just like Orla and her friends, the students concluded that 21st-century students were boring and antiquated, and nothing like them.
“But you’re wrong,” thought Orla, waking up from her strange dream.
When she arrived at her grandmother’s house, she decided to do her homework by interviewing her grandmother. She wrote up her grandmother’s account of her teenage life and concluded her project with the words: “We are all the same, just separated by time.”
From then on, thanks to Mrs. Hartnett and her strange, dream-inducing book, the people and places of history became as alive to Orla as the people and places of her time.
When Orla grew up and became a history teacher, she too used Mrs. Harnett’s book to bring history alive for the next generation of students.

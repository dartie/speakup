Chimps at War! The Ngogo Conflict [B1]

Gli scimpanzé del Parco Nazionale di Kibale, in Uganda, sono coinvolti in una violenta e letale guerra civile che dura ormai da oltre dieci anni.

Many studies have claimed that animals have similar characteristics to humans. This is particularly true for chimpanzees, who share almost 99 per cent of our DNA. Just like us, chimpanzees have complex social structures, profound emotional bonds and distinct personalities. And just like us, they engage in conflict sometimes, and even in something researchers are calling ‘civil wars’. A new study has revealed that since 2015, chimpanzees in Kibale National Park in Uganda have been engaging in the deadliest chimpanzee civil war ever recorded: the Ngogo conflict.

CHIMP WAR ONE

Experts say chimpanzee civil wars are very rare, occurring only around every five hundred years. They are not normally caused by resource shortages, but by a breakdown in social cohesion and internal dynamics. The first chimpanzee civil war ever recorded was the Gombe Chimpanzee War (1974-1978), which the late primatologist Jane Goodall observed while studying chimpanzees in Gombe Stream National Park in Tanzania. The war began when a chimpanzee community divided into two rival groups. It ended four years later, after the larger group, including eight male adults, had killed all six adult males in the smaller group. Places

EXTREME VIOLENCE

Before the Ngogo conflict, Kibale National Park had the world’s largest known chimpanzee community. It had around two hundred chimpanzees, who all lived in harmony for twenty years. But then several of the adult males died, causing a change in the dominance hierarchy and in how the chimpanzees interacted with each other. Consequently, the community divided into two factions, now known as the Western and Central groups. The Ngogo conflict has resulted in extreme violence and death. Experts who have been observing it say the Western group is more aggressive than the Central group. Between 2018 and 2024, the Western group conducted around twenty-four coordinated attacks on the Central group, killing at least twenty-four of its chimpanzees: seven male adults and seventeen infants. An additional fourteen chimpanzees from the Central group are missing, and may also have been killed in coordinated attacks.

HEROISM AND FRIENDSHIP

Aaron Sandel is an associate professor of anthropology at the University of Texas at Austin and the co-director of the Ngogo Chimpanzee Project, which has been studying the conflict. He told CNN, “It’s definitely sad to see these chimps kill one another.” However, he also said that while studying the violence, he and his colleagues had observed acts of heroism and friendship: “I feel like we’re tapping into something really at the heart of what it means to be a chimp,” he said.

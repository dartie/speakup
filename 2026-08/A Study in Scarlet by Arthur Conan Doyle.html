“A Study in Scarlet” by Arthur Conan Doyle [B2]

È in questo romanzo che fa il suo debutto il detective più famoso di tutti i tempi: Sherlock Holmes. Con il fidato Watson, risolverà un caso apparentemente inspiegabile.

Sherlock Holmes is such a beloved character, and his methods and quirks so profoundly etched in the public mind, that fans of crime fiction often enjoy his stories without even knowing whether they sprang from the pen of Arthur Conan Doyle or from any of the countless reinterpretations, spin-offs, or appropriations of the archetypal analytical detective.

A CATALOGUE OF CRIME

The canonical Sherlock Holmes corpus consists of sixty cases — four novels and fifty-six short stories — published between 1887 and 1927, many of which first appeared in popular periodicals of the time. A non-exhaustive list of Victorian crime within these pages reveals over thirty murders, attempted murders, or manslaughters, often entangled with or stemming from other offenses, such as a dozen thefts and robberies, about ten cases of blackmail, espionage, fraud or forgery, half a dozen kidnappings and a variety of domestic intrigues. Yet the enduring allure of the Holmes stories lies less in the crimes themselves than in the brilliant method he uses to solve them. A LESS EXPENSIVE DOMICILE

A Study in Scarlet marks the beginning of the Sherlock Holmes universe. Its appeal lies not merely in the resolution of a double murder, but in the careful establishment of Holmes’ character and “science of deduction”, as well as the enduring partnership between the detective and Dr. John H. Watson. It is Watson, an army surgeon recently returned from Afghanistan with a shoulder injury, who recounts Holmes’ exploits. Like many young bachelors of his (and our) time, what brings the two young men together is the high cost of living in a big city: “I naturally gravitated to London, that great cesspool into which all the loungers of the Empire are irresistibly drained […] leading a comfortless, meaningless existence, and spending such money as I had, considerably more freely than I ought. […] I soon realised […] I must make a complete alteration in my style of living […] and to take up my quarters in some less pretentious and less expensive domicile.” Culture

LIMITED KNOWLEDGE

The narrator and sidekick, Watson becomes a proxy for the readers, a privileged audience to Holmes’ reasoning. Through his eyes, we meet a man gifted with extraordinary powers for observation and a brilliant analytical mind. Yet what strikes Watson most is not Holmes’ brilliance, but the particular architecture of his knowledge. Watson lists its limits thus: “profound” in chemistry, and “accurate but unsystematic” in anatomy, but “nil” in subjects such as philosophy and astronomy, and “feeble” in politics. “Yet his zeal for certain studies was remarkable, and within eccentric limits his knowledge was so extraordinarily ample and minute that his observations have fairly astounded me. Surely no man would work so hard or attain such precise information unless he had some definite end in view. […] No man burdens his mind with small matters unless he has some very good reason for doing so.” “Eppure, il suo zelo per certi studi era straordinario, e il suo sapere, entro certi limiti, era talmente vasto e profondo che spesso egli mi sbalordiva con le sue osservazioni. Non era possibile che un uomo lavorasse tanto assiduamente e si procurasse nozioni così minute senza avere in vista una mèta ben definita. […] E nessuno si rompe il cervello con particolari precisissimi, a meno che non abbia ottimi motivi per farlo.” Nigel Bruce as Dr. John Watson and Basil Rathbone as Sherlock Holmes. The iconic cinematic duo starred in fourteen films from 1939 to 1946 and voiced the characters in over two hundred radio episodes.

SCIENCE OF DEDUCTION

Even before witnessing Holmes in action, Watson encounters an article outlining the principles of what its author — Holmes himself, he soon discovers — calls the “science of deduction”. His initial reaction is one of scepticism, as might be expected from a reasonably educated but conventional mind: “It struck me as being a remarkable mixture of shrewdness and of absurdity. The reasoning was close and intense, but the deductions appeared to me to be far-fetched and exaggerated. […]. Deceit, according to him, was an impossibility in the case of one trained to observation and analysis. […] So startling would his results appear to the uninitiated that until they learned the processes by which he had arrived at them they might well consider him as a necromancer.” “Gli parve di leggere un singolare miscuglio di ingegno e assurdità. Il ragionamento era conciso e serrato, ma le deduzioni mi parevano eccessive e campate per aria.[…] La simulazione, secondo lui, era una cosa impossibile nei confronti di una persona abituata a osservare e analizzare con acume. […] I risultati da lui esposti sarebbero apparsi così sconcertanti al profano, che chiunque, prima d’aver afferrato i processi attraverso i quali l’autore vi era giunto, l’avrebbe facilmente considerato uno stregone.”

BLOOD LETTERS

Watson is soon given the opportunity to see the science at work when Inspectors Gregson and Lestrade of Scotland Yard call upon 221B Baker Street to hire Holmes’ consultancy services in a baffling case: the discovery of a corpse in an abandoned house, with no apparent cause of death and a single enigmatic word — “RACHE”, the German word for ‘revenge’ Holmes will reveal later — written in blood on the wall. Soon, another man is stabbed to death in his hotel; again, “RACHE” is written on the wall. What follows is less a conventional police investigation than a demonstration of method. Holmes observes what others overlook and reconstructs a narrative invisible to everyone else, including the reader. After a brief investigation, he announces the name of the culprit, to everyone’s astonishment.

VENGEANCE AND CONFESSION

At this point, the narrative takes a sharp turn. The story shifts from Watson’s first-person account to an omniscient voice that transports the reader across the Atlantic to the Great Alkali Plain of Utah, in the American West. It is a long and seemingly digressive tale of survival, persecution and vengeance set within a Mormon community depicted as a fanatical and violent cult.  This sudden detour pauses the detective narrative at its most thrilling moment, replacing it with what reads as a frontier melodrama. The murderer, it turns out, is no conventional villain, but a man scarred by injustice, loss and a decades-long obsession with retribution. “There is no satisfaction in vengeance unless the offender has time to realise who it is that strikes him, and why retribution has come upon him. I had my plans arranged by which I should have the opportunity of making the man who had wronged me understand that his old sin had found him out.” “Non vi è gioia nella vendetta, se il nemico non ha il tempo di capire chi lo colpisce e perché è scoccata la sua ultima ora. Avevo già escogitato un piano per far sí che il mio nemico si accorgesse che stava pagando il suo sanguinoso peccato.”

TO REASON BACKWARDS

Finally comes the most anticipated moment: Holmes’ explanation of the method by which he solved the mystery. In a characteristic speech, the detective guides his audience through his chain of reasoning, offering what amounts to a manifesto for analytical thinking: “From a drop of water […] a logician could infer the possibility of an Atlantic or a Niagara without having seen or heard of one or the other. So all life is a great chain, the nature of which is known whenever we are shown a single link of it. Like all other arts, the Science of Deduction and Analysis is one which can only be acquired by long and patient study […] In solving a problem of this sort, the grand thing is to be able to reason backwards. That is a very useful accomplishment, and a very easy one, but people do not practise it much.” “Da una goccia d’acqua un ragionatore logico potrebbe dedurre la possibile esistenza dell’Atlantico o delle cascate del Niagara, senza averli visti e senza aver mai sentito parlare né dell’uno né delle altre. Così, tutta la vita è una grande catena la cui natura si rivela a chiunque ne osservi un solo anello. Come tutte le altre arti, la scienza della deduzione e dell’analisi può essere acquisita soltanto attraverso uno studio lungo e paziente […] Nel risolvere un problema di questo genere, l’essenziale è saper ragionare all’indietro. É una tattica utile e saggia, ma pochi se ne servono”.

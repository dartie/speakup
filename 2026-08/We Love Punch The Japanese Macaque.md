Eerybody needs comfort [now and then](## "ogni tanto"), and monkeys are no exception. In July 2025, a Japanese macaque was born at the Ichikawa City Zoo. He was named [Punch](## "pugno"), after manga artist Monkey [Punch](## "pugno"). Shortly after his birth, his mother abandoned him.

## NOT ALONE
Maybe the [heat wave](## "ondata di calore") during which [Punch](## "pugno") was born played a role, causing his mother enough stress to prioritise her own health. It’s also possible that living in captivity affected her ability to care for her first baby, as Japanese macaques [rarely](## "raramente") abandon their [offspring](## "prole, cuccioli") in the wild. 
[Whatever](## "qualsiasi sia") the reason, [Punch](## "pugno") found himself [motherless](## "orfano di madre").
Thankfully, [Punch](## "pugno") was not completely alone. [Zookeepers](## "guardiani dello zoo") took care of him from the start, ensuring his health and safety. But there was one problem: baby monkeys need to [grip](## "aggrapparsi") onto someone or something immediately after birth to [build up](## "sviluppare") their muscles, but also to feel a sense of comfort. Usually that someone is their mother, but in [Punch](## "pugno")’s case, that was impossible.

## IKEA FRIEND
“We tried several options to give him something to hold,” Kosuke Shikano, [Punch](## "pugno")’s keeper, told The Guardian. “We rolled towels into various [thicknesses](## "spessori") to find something easy to [grasp](## "afferrare") and let him try holding different [stuffed toys](## "peluche").” In the end, [Punch](## "pugno") found something he was happy with: a stuffed monkey from Ikea.
In February, videos of [Punch](## "pugno") [clinging](## "aggrapparsi") to his stuffed monkey went viral. After facing aggression from other monkeys at the zoo, [Punch](## "pugno") would always go back to his ‘friend’ and carry it around with him.

## STRICT HIERARCHY
These videos are [cute](## "adorabili"), [touching](## "commoventi"), and a little bit sad, in part because they show a lack of socialisation between [Punch](## "pugno") and his [fellow](## "simili") monkeys. Japanese macaques have a complex social structure. [Troops](## "branchi") (the collective noun for a group of animals, especially primates) of several dozen individuals, mostly females and young monkeys, live according to a strict hierarchy. To maintain this hierarchy and the social [bonds](## "legami") that hold the troop together, there are a series of [behaviours](## "comportamenti") that clarify who is doing what and why. [Social grooming](## "toelettatura") plays a big part in this, where one monkey will groom another. Other [behaviours](## "comportamenti") show that an individual is playing or angry, for example.

## LEARNED BEHAVIOUR
These [behaviours](## "comportamenti") are learned, just as they are in humans. For [Punch](## "pugno"), however, who was [raised](## "crescere") by zookeepers and a stuffed monkey, learning the social [cues](## "segnali") was difficult. This may explain why the troop was initially antagonistic towards him; if he was trying to play but ended up communicating “I’m angry,” he wouldn’t have immediately become everyone’s best friend.
The good news is that [Punch](## "pugno") is depending less and less on his stuffed friend and is gradually being incorporated into the rest of the group as he learns the right way to behave.
Let’s just hope the stuffed monkey doesn’t [feel left behind](## "sentirsi escluso").

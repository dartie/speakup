Greyfriars Bobby: Edinburgh’s Most Loyal Dog [B2]

La statua di questo cagnolino è tra i luoghi più visitati e fotografati di Edimburgo. Ma chi era Bobby? E perché è diventato la mascotte della capitale scozzese?

The loyalty of dogs has inspired countless moving stories. Perhaps the best known is that of Hachikō, the famous Japanese Akita who kept returning to a Tokyo train station for nearly ten years, waiting in vain for his owner who had passed away. But did you know that Scotland had its equivalent — albeit a smaller breed, arguably no less devoted? His name was Bobby. A Skye terrier, or possibly a Dandie Dinmount — accounts differ —, Bobby belonged to a night watchman named John Gray. When Gray died in 1858 and was buried in Greyfriars Kirkyard, Bobby refused to leave. For fourteen years he kept vigil at the graveside, becoming so well-known that the people of Edinburgh took it upon themselves to feed and look after him. 

BOBBY’S FOUNTAIN

In 1867, when a law was introduced requiring stray dogs to be licensed or else be put down, the city’s Lord Provost, Sir William Chambers — who also happened to be the director of the Scottish Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals — paid to take ownership of Bobby, allowing him to continue living and returning to the cemetery. Alex Phillips, Sarah Davison When Bobby died in 1872, he was not buried directly beside his owner in consecrated ground, but he was laid to rest nearby, at the cemetery entrance, not far from John Gray’s grave. A year later, the English philanthropist Lady Burdett-Coutts, moved by the story, funded a small drinking fountain topped with a statue of Bobby. It stands at the corner of George IV Bridge and Candlemaker Row, just a short distance from its burial place and in front of Greyfriars Bobby’s Bar, a historic pub. BUM

But Edinburgh is not just Bobby’s kennel: the terrier shares the city with another dog, perhaps a little less famous but equally worthy of being remembered. In 1978, the Scottish capital was twinned with San Diego, California. And it so happens that San Diego has its own canine mascot, too: Bum, a cross between a St. Bernard and a spaniel. Although Bum was taken in by a man named Ah Wo Sue, he spent much of his life roaming the streets, taking part in parades, playing with children, and even riding public transport. In fact, ‘bum’ is a colloquial term commonly used to refer to a homeless person. Thanks to his gentle and sociable nature, Bum became one of the city’s most beloved symbols, and San Diego eventually honoured him with a statue in the Gaslamp Quarter.

A WORTHY LIFE

In 2007, a statue in honour of Bobby was erected in the same Californian neighbourhood. To return the gesture, in 2008 the Scots dedicated a statue to Bum in Princes Street Gardens, at the entrance to St. Cuthbert’s Churchyard. Visitors still leave sticks in front of it so that Bobby’s ‘brother dog’ can keep playing.

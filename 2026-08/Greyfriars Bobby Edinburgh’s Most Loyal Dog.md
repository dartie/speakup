The loyalty of dogs has inspired [countless](## "innumerevoli") moving stories. Perhaps the best known is that of Hachikō, the famous Japanese Akita who kept returning to a Tokyo train station for nearly ten years, waiting in vain for his owner who had [passed away](## "morire").
But did you know that Scotland had its equivalent — [albeit](## "sebbene") a smaller [breed](## "razza"), [arguably](## "indubbiamente") no less devoted? His name was Bobby. A Skye terrier, or possibly a Dandie Dinmount — [accounts differ](## "le fonti differiscono") —, Bobby [belonged](## "appartenere") to a night watchman named John Gray. When Gray died in 1858 and was [buried](## "seppellire") in Greyfriars Kirkyard, Bobby refused to leave. For fourteen years he kept vigil at the [graveside](## "accanto alla tomba"), becoming so well-known that the people of Edinburgh [took it upon themselves](## "farsene carico") to feed and look after him. 

## BOBBY’S FOUNTAIN
In 1867, when a law was introduced requiring [stray dogs](## "cani randagi") to be [licensed](## "registrare") or else be [put down](## "sopprimere"), the city’s Lord Provost, Sir William Chambers — who also [happened to be](## "risultare essere") the director of the Scottish Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals — paid to [take ownership](## "diventare padrone") of Bobby, allowing him to continue living and returning to the cemetery.

When Bobby died in 1872, he was not [buried](## "seppellire") directly beside his owner in [consecrated ground](## "terra consacrata"), but he was [laid to rest](## "dare sepoltura") nearby, at the cemetery entrance, not far from John Gray’s grave. A year later, the English philanthropist Lady Burdett-Coutts, moved by the story, [funded](## "finanziare") a small drinking fountain [topped](## "coronare, completare") with a statue of Bobby. It stands at the corner of George IV Bridge and Candlemaker Row, just a short distance from its burial place and in front of Greyfriars Bobby’s Bar, a historic pub.

## BUM
But Edinburgh is not just Bobby’s [kennel](## "cuccia"): the terrier shares the city with another dog, perhaps a little less famous but equally worthy of being remembered. In 1978, the Scottish capital was [twinned](## "gemellare") with San Diego, California. And it so happens that San Diego has its own canine mascot, too: Bum, a cross between a St. Bernard and a spaniel.
Although Bum was [taken in](## "accogliere") by a man named Ah Wo Sue, he spent much of his life [roaming](## "vagare") the streets, taking part in [parades](## "sfilate"), playing with children, and even riding public transport. In fact, ‘[bum](## "vagabondo")’ is a colloquial term commonly used to refer to a homeless person. Thanks to his gentle and sociable nature, Bum became one of the city’s most beloved symbols, and San Diego eventually honoured him with a statue in the Gaslamp Quarter.

## A WORTHY LIFE
In 2007, a statue in honour of Bobby was erected in the same Californian neighbourhood. To return the gesture, in 2008 the Scots dedicated a statue to Bum in Princes Street Gardens, at the entrance to St. Cuthbert’s Churchyard. Visitors still leave sticks in front of it so that Bobby’s ‘brother dog’ can keep playing.

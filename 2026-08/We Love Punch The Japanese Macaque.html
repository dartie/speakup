We Love Punch: The Japanese Macaque [B1]

Quest’adorabile scimmietta senza mamma ha commosso il mondo intero. Tutti abbiamo seguito la sua storia sui social.

Eerybody needs comfort now and then, and monkeys are no exception. In July 2025, a Japanese macaque was born at the Ichikawa City Zoo. He was named Punch, after manga artist Monkey Punch. Shortly after his birth, his mother abandoned him.

NOT ALONE

Maybe the heat wave during which Punch was born played a role, causing his mother enough stress to prioritise her own health. It’s also possible that living in captivity affected her ability to care for her first baby, as Japanese macaques rarely abandon their offspring in the wild.  Whatever the reason, Punch found himself motherless. Thankfully, Punch was not completely alone. Zookeepers took care of him from the start, ensuring his health and safety. But there was one problem: baby monkeys need to grip onto someone or something immediately after birth to build up their muscles, but also to feel a sense of comfort. Usually that someone is their mother, but in Punch’s case, that was impossible. Culture

IKEA FRIEND

“We tried several options to give him something to hold,” Kosuke Shikano, Punch’s keeper, told The Guardian. “We rolled towels into various thicknesses to find something easy to grasp and let him try holding different stuffed toys.” In the end, Punch found something he was happy with: a stuffed monkey from Ikea. In February, videos of Punch clinging to his stuffed monkey went viral. After facing aggression from other monkeys at the zoo, Punch would always go back to his ‘friend’ and carry it around with him.

Fare il buffone. To monkey around

STRICT HIERARCHY

These videos are cute, touching, and a little bit sad, in part because they show a lack of socialisation between Punch and his fellow monkeys. Japanese macaques have a complex social structure. Troops (the collective noun for a group of animals, especially primates) of several dozen individuals, mostly females and young monkeys, live according to a strict hierarchy. To maintain this hierarchy and the social bonds that hold the troop together, there are a series of behaviours that clarify who is doing what and why. Social grooming plays a big part in this, where one monkey will groom another. Other behaviours show that an individual is playing or angry, for example.

LEARNED BEHAVIOUR

These behaviours are learned, just as they are in humans. For Punch, however, who was raised by zookeepers and a stuffed monkey, learning the social cues was difficult. This may explain why the troop was initially antagonistic towards him; if he was trying to play but ended up communicating “I’m angry,” he wouldn’t have immediately become everyone’s best friend. The good news is that Punch is depending less and less on his stuffed friend and is gradually being incorporated into the rest of the group as he learns the right way to behave. Let’s just hope the stuffed monkey doesn’t feel left behind.

What makes a song memorable? As part of a project exploring musical memory and its links to conditions such as dementia, researchers collaborating with Manchester’s Museum of Science and Industry launched an experiment to find the most recognisable songs of all time. They produced four interactive games where listeners played against the clock to guess song titles, and then gathered data from over twelve thousand participants, whose responses took an average of five seconds. Hoping to find the shared characteristics that make a song an earworm, Dutch musicologist Ashley Burgoyne, who designed the game, concluded “very strong melodic hooks seem to be the most memorable for people” and is continuing to study the therapeutic benefits.  The museum compiled a list of the catchiest songs of all time — including the average time participants took to recognise each tune —, making them ideal material for improving your English. 

1. WANNABE, SPICE GIRLS  (1996, 2.29 SECONDS)

The best-selling single worldwide by an all-female group, it took listeners just 2.29 seconds to recognise English group Spice Girls’ debut single Wannabe. A global Number One, its lyrics are about the importance of friendship and feature contributions from all five members. It opens with Geri Halliwell’s infectious laugh and the call-and-response “Tell me what you want”, and also uses mixed conditionals and imperatives as well as the nonsense exclamation “zigazig ah”. The band claimed this is “whatever you want it to be.” If you wanna be my lover, you gotta get with my friends Make it last forever, friendship never ends If you wanna be my lover, you have got to give Taking is too easy, but that’s the way it is Entertainment

2. MAMBO NO. 5 (A LITTLE BIT), LOU BEGA (1999, 2.48 SECONDS)

When Ugandan-Italian singer Lou Bega sampled Cuban musician Pérez Prado’s 1950 Mambo No.5, he kept the title but added his own lyrics to the original instrumental. It was a huge summer hit, selling millions and spending a record twenty weeks at Number One in France. The song uses the imperative to outline dance movements but is primarily a list of women’s names Bega said were from his past. His favourite? “Sandra, that’s why she was the one in the sun.”  Jump up and down and move it all around Shake your head to the sound Put your hand on the groundTake one step left and one step right

3. EYE OF THE TIGER, SURVIVOR (1982, 2.62 SECONDS)

The theme song from the boxing blockbuster Rocky III, Eye of the Tiger’s iconic dead guitar riff was written to perfectly synch with the punches thrown in the movie’s opening sequence. The film’s director and protagonist Sylvester Stallone was unable to secure publishing rights for Queen’s Another One Bites the Dust, so commissioned this track from American rock band Survivor. Drawing heavily on the film’s storyline, the lyrics follow a boxer’s intense training sessions and the song has become a global sporting anthem. Risin’ up, back on the street Did my time, took my chances Went the distance Now I’m back on my feet Just a man and his will to survive

4. JUST DANCE, LADY GAGA (2008, 2.66 SECONDS)

American superstar Lady Gaga’s debut single was written in a Los Angeles studio in just ten minutes, following a farewell party in New York the night before. Feeling a little under the weather, she recalled the previous evening’s energy to produce “a happy record” about losing your inhibitions and forgetting your worries on the dance floor, with lyrics mostly in the present simple. It is one of the best-selling digital singles of all time. What’s going on on the floor? I love this record baby but I can’t see straight anymore Keep it cool, what’s the name of this club? I can’t remember but it’s alright, a-alright

5. S.O.S., ABBA (1975, 2.73 SECONDS)

Swedish pop group ABBA struggled to follow their global success with 1974’s Eurovision-winning Waterloo, releasing a slew of unsuccessful singles. S.O.S. marked the beginning of a hugely successful run of hits where they became one of the defining bands of pop music. About a relationship falling apart, the lyrics use the past simple to evoke happier times with question forms to reflect the anguish of breaking up. Many consider it the band’s best song and its opening piano intro even influenced the Sex Pistols’ punk-classic Pretty Vacant. Uniquely in pop music, both the song title and artist are palindromes. So when you’re near me, darling, Can’t you hear me S.O.S.? The love you gave me, Nothing else can save me, S.O.S. When you’re gone, How can I even try to go on? When you’re gone, though I try, How can I carry on?

6.OH, PRETTY WOMAN, ROY ORBISON (1964, 2.73 SECONDS)

American singer-songwriter Roy Orbison’s signature hit was inspired by a throwaway comment from his songwriting partner Bill Dees: when Orbison asked his wife if she had enough cash for shopping, Dees said “a pretty woman never needs any money.” Lyrically the song describes the singer’s admiration for a beautiful woman, using the present simple and the modals ‘would’ and ‘could’. A huge hit in the 60s, it was given a new lease of life in 1990 when it was used as the theme song of the movie of the same name. Pretty woman walkin’ down the street Pretty woman, the kind I’d like to meet Pretty woman, I don’t believe you, You’re not the truth No one could look as good as youMercy

7. BEAT IT, MICHAEL JACKSON (1982, 2.80 SECONDS)

When producer Quincy Jones suggested to Michael Jackson that he write a song like The Knack’s My Sharona, the King of Pop came up with “the type of rock song that I would buy.” He then enlisted rock legend Eddie Van Halen for one of the 1980s’ best-known guitar solos and produced an iconic choreographed music video, making Beat It a massive worldwide hit. The lyrics describe gangland rivalry and are almost entirely in the present simple.  They told him, “Don’t you ever come around here” “Don’t wanna see your face, You better disappear” The fire’s in their eyes And their words are really clear So beat it, just beat it

8. I WILL ALWAYS LOVE YOU, WHITNEY HOUSTON (1992, 2.83 SECONDS)

The best-selling single ever by a female artist, this was recorded for Whitney Houston’s film debut The Bodyguard. A cover of a 1974 Dolly Parton song, it was originally written as a farewell to the singer’s manager, but in Whitney’s version becomes a powerful ode to devotion. Opening with an example of the first conditional with ‘should’, the song largely uses the future ‘will’, with initially restrained a cappella vocals slowly giving way to the pop-diva’s soaring dramatic high notes. A huge global hit, it spent a then-record fourteen weeks at the top of the US charts. If I should stay I would only be in your way So I’ll go, but I know I’ll think of you every step of the way

9. DON’T YOU WANT ME, THE HUMAN LEAGUE (1981, 2.83 SECONDS)

English band The Human League were pioneers in electronic sound and this is considered the first hit song to use a drum machine. Although the band were reluctant to release it, feeling it was too mainstream, it became a huge hit in the UK and, thanks to heavy rotation on the newly-launched MTV, all over the world. Inspired by a story in a teen’s magazine, singer Phil Oakey claimed the song is about a couple’s power politics. Opening with the past simple, the song also uses the past continuous and present perfect.  You were working as a waitress in a cocktail bar When I met you I picked you out, I shook you up And turned you around Turned you into someone new

10. I DON’T WANT TO MISS A THING, AEROSMITH (1998, 2.84 SECONDS)

This is the theme from blockbuster disaster movie Armageddon. Veteran songwriter Dianne Warren originally envisaged this power ballad being performed by Celine Dion, but producers first approached U2, who declined, and then the film’s star Liv Tyler’s father’s band Aerosmith. It became their best-known song and only US Number One. Inspired by a quote from Barbra Streisand’s husband, who said he missed his wife when they were asleep, the song uses the slang ‘wanna’ (‘want to’) and the conditional ‘would’ clause.

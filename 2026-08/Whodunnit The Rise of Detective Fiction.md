The usual [plot](## "trama") involves a crime, most often a murder, at the start of the story, and then it is the detective’s job to work out who committed the crime, or ‘whodunnit’, as this kind of fiction is commonly called. It is satisfying for readers to [pick up](## "raccogliere") [clues](## "indizi") [scattered](## "disseminare") by the author and try to guess the murderer’s identity. Extra interest can be generated in the form of sudden, unpredictable changes in the fictional events, or ‘[[plot](## "trama") twists](## "colpi di scena")’, or if the author throws in some [misleading](## "fuorviante") false [clues](## "indizi"), known as ‘red herrings’. Best of all is when the identity of the killer remains a mystery until the very end, and the culprit [turns out to be](## "rivelarsi") the least suspected character.

## THE BIRTH OF CRIME FICTION
It is generally understood that the birth of the genre [coin](## "coniare")cided with the early 1800s and the Industrial Revolution. As people moved from the countryside to the cities in search of work, they were forced to live close together in [squalid](## "squallide") conditions of poverty, where crime was [rife](## "diffuso"). It was at this time that the first police forces were established, in London in 1829, and in New York City in 1845. Reason and logic were gaining traction as positive ideals, as trust in science began to [overtake](## "superare") faith in religion. [Steam-powered](## "a vapore") [printing presses](## "macchine da stampa, tipografie") meant cheaper newspapers and books, which contributed to a rise in [literacy](## "alfabetizzazione"). Factory processes became gradually more mechanised and there were more people earning regular [wages](## "salari"). For the first time in history, a lot of people had money and time as well as the inclination to read.

## THE TEN COMMANDMENTS FOR DETECTIVE FICTION
Ronald Knox was an English Catholic priest, radio [broadcaster](## "conduttore") and mystery writer, who codified the rules for detective fiction in his “Ten Commandments for detective fiction”. Written in 1929, they reflect the language and ideology of the time. Knox believed that the reader should be able to participate [fairly](## "equamente") in the murder mystery by following the [clues](## "indizi") and attempting to find a solution perhaps even before the fictional detective.
After the first railway opened in Britain in 1830, people began to travel to work. They often wanted something to read on the train and so W. H. Smith & Sons opened their first [bookstall](## "bancarella di libri") at Euston Station in London in 1848. By the end of the century they had over one thousand station [bookstall](## "bancarella di libri")s on railway [concourses](## "spazi, atri"). Crime fiction was particularly popular with travellers, as the suspense made for engaging reading and helped to pass the time.

## THE FIRST DETECTIVE
Edgar Allan Poe was the first writer to [coin](## "coniare") the term ‘detective’ when he introduced his private investigator, C. Auguste Dupin, in Murders in the Rue Morgue. This story is also considered to be the first [locked-room mystery](## "mistero della stanza chiusa"), that is, a story where it appears impossible for the perpetrator to have entered the crime scene, committed the crime and left undetected. The story was highly influential, establishing many tropes that would become common to detective stories, such as a brilliant though eccentric detective, a less than competent police force, and a first-person narrator who is a friend and admirer of the detective.

## THE GOLDEN AGE
The 1920s and 1930s were a golden age of detective fiction in the UK. The period was defined by Sir Arthur Conan Doyle with his stories about Sherlock Holmes, as well as the four Queens of Crime: Agatha Christie, Dorothy L. Sayers, Margery Allingham, and Ngaio Marsh. The majority of the novels were whodunnits and these writers excelled at [keeping their readers on tenterhooks](## "tenere con il fiato sospeso") until the very end of the book. The crimes almost always took place among the upper-class inhabitants of English country houses. Certain [plot](## "trama") conventions were also established to prevent the writers from playing tricks on the reader by introducing new evidence right at the end.

## HARD-BOILED
The [light-hearted](## "spensierato"), [straightforward](## "semplice") whodunnit of the Golden Age began to decline at the start of the Second World War. In fact, American writers had already been reinventing what they saw as an unrealistic, [puzzle-solving](## "risoluzione di enigmi") genre about the upper classes.
1940s America saw the introduction of a new type of crime novel known as ‘hard boiled’ or ‘noir fiction’, where struggling detectives [waged war](## "muovere guerra") on organised crime and corrupt cops in dirty and dangerous cities. The genre took off in the US between the 1930s and 1950s with examples such as Raymond Chandler’s detective Philip Marlowe.

## FOR ALL INTERESTS
From the 50s and 60s, crime fiction transitioned in all directions. It began to focus less on the [plot](## "trama") and more on their characters’ psychological motivations. The novels could be set in the distant past or distant future and the detectives could have a variety of ethnic or social backgrounds. Today, with crime fiction as popular as ever, there is room for a vast number of detective genres to satisfy any reader’s tastes and interests.

## WHODUNNIT TIMELINE
Timeline of the early years of detective fiction:
The first English edition of A Thousand and One Nights (original English title The Arabian Nights) is printed. In this collection of [Middle Eastern](## "del Medio Oriente") [folk tales](## "racconti popolari") from the Islamic Golden Age, many stories present [striking](## "sorprendenti") characteristics of crime and specifically detective fiction.
E.T.A. Hoffmann becomes a possible [claimant](## "pretendente") to the title of first crime writer with his story Mademoiselle Scuderi. The eponymous protagonist solves a crime using logic and intuition.
Edgar Allan Poe publishes Murders in the Rue Morgue. He is generally considered to be the first author to write a true detective story.
Wilkie Collins publishes The Moonstone, first serialised in a magazine and then released as a novel in the same year. Generally considered to be the first true detective novel, the book features Sergeant Cuff, whom Wilkie Collins based on the real life detective Jonathan Whicher. T.S. Eliot described it as “the first, the longest and the best of modern English detective novels.”
Perhaps the most influential writer of the genre, Arthur Conan Doyle, introduces his detective Sherlock Holmes to the world in the story A Study in Scarlet. Although likely influenced by Edgar Allan Poe’s Dupin, Holmes’s character is also said to be based on the surgeon Dr. Joseph Bell. Conan Doyle met Bell in 1877 and spent hours observing Bell’s celebrated deductive abilities.
A fan of Conan Doyle, Agatha Christie uses her experience with Belgian refugees and soldiers that she met while working as a volunteer nurse during the First World War to create her detective Hercule Poirot in the story The Mysterious Affair at Styles. Her elderly [spinster](## "zitella") detective, Miss Jane Marple, who uses her knowledge of people and relationships in an English village to solve crimes, was introduced later, in 1927.
Raymond Chandler introduces his hard-boiled detective Philip Marlowe in The Big Sleep.

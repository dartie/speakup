The Isle of Skye: Wild and Wonderful [B2]

La seconda isola più grande della Scozia offre una costa spettacolare, paesaggi mozzafiato e una storia antica. Il luogo perfetto per immergersi in una natura allo stato selvaggio.

With its other-worldly landscape, unique culture and picture-perfect coastline, the Isle of Skye is the most-visited of the nine hundred or so islands that comprise the Hebrides. Skye juts out from the west coast of mainland Scotland like a rugged lobster claw or winged creature (its Gaelic name An t-Eilean Sgitheanach translates as The Winged Isle). Named after the Norse word for ‘cloud’ (skuy), the Isle of Skye is often wrapped in low-hanging mist or sea fog (known as haar), adding to its sense of mystery.

ARRIVALS

Along with Mull, Islay, Jura, and other smaller isles, Skye forms part of the Inner Hebrides archipelago. To the west, further out in the wild Atlantic Ocean, the Outer Hebrides include Lewis and Harris, Barra and Vatersay, and the uninhabited beauty of St. Kilda. Skye was settled at least 11,500 years ago, with evidence of hunting camps, stone tools and chambered tombs. In the first century BC, Gaelic-speaking Irish settlers arrived, followed by Vikings from the 9th century AD onwards. Today, the island is home to a population of 13,000 people — and more than one hundred thousand sheep. A view over Old Man of Storr, a fifty-metre-high petrified lava pinnacle with a cliff behind it that overlooks the town of Portree.

COLOURFUL CAPITAL

Around 650,000 visitors travel to Skye each year, looking for wildness, wonder, and the difference that Skye offers in abundance. Since 1995, the island has been attached to the Scottish mainland by the Skye Road Bridge, making it an easy day trip for tourist coaches. But many people still prefer to travel the slow way, by ferry, crossing in half an hour from Mallaig on the mainland to Armadale in the southwest of Skye. A shorter crossing from Glenelg uses a rare turntable ferry. Rotating manually, so that cars can drive on without needing to reverse off, the MV Glenachulish is the only one of its kind in the world. An hour’s drive north brings visitors to Portree, capital of Skye, where colourful houses line the harbour alongside active fishing boats. Its shops, cafés, restaurants, and places to stay are about as busy as life gets on Skye. Places

WILDNESS

Despite the large number of visitors, Skye contains many peaceful, hidden corners. The island is 80 kilometres long (115 kilometres by road) but its coastline is so heavily indented that wildness, and the sea, are never far away. In the northwest, the lighthouse at Neist Point features views of towering sea cliffs and a chance to spot sea eagles, whales, and dolphins. In the south, the peninsula of Elgol and Camasunary Bay offer access to remote beaches and panoramic views of the Cuillin mountains. Though popular with walkers and climbers, the iconic Black Cuillin ridge is not for the inexperienced, with razor-edged paths, challenging scrambles and steep falls on all sides. The nearby Red Cuillin are tough, but less intimidating.

PREHISTORIC

Located in the northeast of Skye, the Trotternish peninsula is home to some of the island’s more breathtaking and unusual scenery. Most famous is the Old Man of Storr, an eroded volcanic sentinel looking out to sea. On the edge of Staffin Bay, the Quiraing is an unreal landscape of dramatic cliffs, grassy plateaus, and towering rock pinnacles offering panoramic views across the island. If it all looks a bit prehistoric, that’s no surprise: dinosaur fossils and footprints have been found all around this area. A fossilised dinosaur footprint on the rocky shore of An Corran Beach in Staffin, Isle of Skye.

GEOLOGIST’S DREAM

With nearly three billion years of ancient rock record on show, the Isle of Skye is a geologist’s dream. It offers a unique combination of dramatic volcanic landscapes, including the gabbro and granite Cuillin mountains, layered basaltic plateau, and Jurassic fossil beds. Skye is an epicentre of volcanic activity and the Cuillin are the eroded remains of a sixty-million-year-old magma chamber. In the southeast, the Lewisian Gneiss is almost three billion years old, while the north shows the iconic basalt columns of Kilt Rock and massive landslips of the Quiraing, still moving today. Like a visible geological map, its sedimentary, metamorphic, and igneous rocks layered together, Skye reveals the forces that have shaped our planet.

CONTRASTS

Skye is a place of contrasts, and not just in its geology. Looking more like the Caribbean than Scotland, Coral Beach near Dunvegan Castle has a turquoise sea and a sun-bleached white beach made of crushed, calcified seaweed. Further south, close to the implacable Black Cuillin, Glen Brittle provides access to the mythical Fairy Pools: crystal-clear, cold blue waterfalls and water pools on the River Brittle. In 1601, this was the site of a battle between the island’s two dominant clans: the MacLeods and the MacDonalds. The MacDonalds were said to have won, but after such a bloody encounter there were few victors.

THE SKYE BOAT SONG

“Speed, bonnie boat, like a bird on the wing / Onward, the sailors cry! / Carry the lad that’s born to be King / Over the sea to Skye.” Combining history, heroism and myth, the Skye Boat Song derives from an ancient Gaelic rowing song. Its lyrics, from the late 19th century, describe how Charles Edward Stuart, ‘Bonnie Prince Charlie’, escaped in a small boat after the terrible defeat by the English at the Battle of Culloden in 1746. Disguised as a serving maid, he was hidden by the heroic young Flora MacDonald. The song has been rewritten many times through the years; it was used as a theme song for the TV series Outlander, and played by bagpipers at the funeral of the late Queen Elizabeth II.

GAELIC CULTURE

Skye is a heartland of Gaelic-speaking culture through language, storytelling, music and tradition. The southern peninsula of Sleat is home to Sabhal Mòr Ostaig, the National Centre for Gaelic Language and Culture. This is the world’s only higher education institution providing learning programmes entirely in Gaelic. Skye retains a strong community culture built on connections to the land, especially through crofting. For any visitor, it’s worth taking time to connect with the resident Skye community — and stopping to breathe in the sea air, the raw beauty and the untamed spirit of this unique and magical island.

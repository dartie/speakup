Chloe Dalton: “Raising Hare” [C1]

Un incontro inaspettato può farci cambiare completamente il modo in cui guardiamo il mondo che ci circonda. È ciò che è successo a questa scrittrice.

On a snowy January day during the Covid lockdown, Chloe Dalton, a high-level political advisor, found a leveret (a baby hare) abandoned near her home in the countryside of northern England. The animal was tiny, probably no more than a day old. Dalton was unsure what to do. Should she leave the leveret and hope its mother returned? Should she take the leveret home and try to look after it? Was it right to take a wild animal into a domestic space? Eventually, Dalton did decide to take the tiny animal home with her. It was a decision that would change her life profoundly.

THROUGH A HARE’S EYES

For Dalton, sharing her home with a growing hare, as she prepared the animal to return to the wild, involved adjusting her routines and travel plans, and rearranging her house and garden. But not only did practical things change; Dalton found her whole relationship with nature was shifting. She began to look at the natural world through a hare’s eyes, noticing things that had been invisible before. A new awareness of the wild plants and animals around her brought an unexpected joy. And, when lockdown ended, Dalton’s commitment to the hare forced her to question her priorities in a way that took her by surprise. RESEARCH

Dalton has spent most of her career working with high-level British politicians as an advisor and speech writer. This often involves travelling to conflict zones around the world. She acknowledges that for most of her life she has been a city person, spending more time in London and in airports than in the countryside where she grew up. So she didn’t feel at all prepared for the unexpected challenge of looking after a wild animal in her home. The responsibility worried, even obsessed her. In response, she used her research skills to find out all she could about hares — in books and online.

A RICH MYTHOLOGY

As well as practical information about what kind of food and shelter the hare needed, and how to deal with paw injuries, Dalton discovered a rich and complex mythology surrounding hares. For example, hares have been associated with witchcraft; in the Middle Ages, it was believed that witches would shape-shift into hares. In other traditions, meanwhile, hares symbolised innocence and renewal: seeing a hare was thought to be lucky. Dalton is intrigued at why the hare occupies such a prominent place in our cultural imagination even though most of us know very little about them in practice. Raising Hare, Dalton’s first book, is a deeply personal account of how she raised the hare to adulthood and what happened next. In it she describes the challenges, both practical and emotional, of caring for a wild animal in her home. And she explores how her own relationship with the nature around her has changed, giving a detailed hare’s-eye-view of the plants and animals of the English countryside.

RAISING HARE

The title Raising Hare is a play on the expression ‘hair-raising’, which means scary or awe-inspiring. Although ‘hare’ and ‘hair’ are spelled differently they are pronounced in exactly the same way — they are homophones. For Dalton, the experience of raising a hare was often hair-raising.

BACK TO BASICS

Chloe Dalton met with Speak Up to talk about her experiences of looking after the hare, engaging with nature in a new way, and about recording those experiences in her book. To write Raising Hare, Dalton had to research things that were completely new to her and she needed to find a whole new vocabulary to express them. Chloe Dalton (English accent): My understanding completely changed because I didn’t know anything about hares. I never thought about the difference between a rabbit and a hare, I didn’t understand the different species, that their lives are completely different, that their relationship to humans is completely different. Part of the effect of this experience was for the first time I needed words to describe nature and I realized I didn’t even have vocabulary for colour, that the words to describe what I was observing, even to describe the fur of the hare… and I loved the process of trying to learn about colour and nature and ask the stupid questions that you think you can never ask, like, “What is grass? And what is a rainbow?” And you know, the things that if you turned around and said to a friend in a bar, they would just laugh at you. And I had this opportunity to almost rediscover that innocence of learning about nature, admitting I didn’t know very much, trying to imagine the world through the eyes of a hare and in the process seeing the world completely differently.

LIFE-CHANGING

When Dalton first brought the baby hare home, it lived in her office. She changed her habits to avoid making a noise or doing anything to disturb it. But even when the hare eventually moved outside, she realised her habits had been changed forever. Chloe Dalton: I’ve acquired some strange habits. One is that I still don’t turn the lights on at night very often, and so if I have friends come to stay with me, they find it very confusing that I’ve got into this habit of just not automatically turning the lights on, because for four years I wouldn’t put the lights on in order not to interfere with the night vision of the hare. So that’s just a humorous side, but some of my habits have definitely changed, even if they’re slightly unusual habits. And I still mark the passage of the hours by the movement of the hares. When I’m at home, there are certain times of day where I leave the outside to the hares. I don’t disturb them. And then when I know they’ve gone to rest, then I go out and about. And again, it’s slightly eccentric, but it’s a small adjustment and it makes me happy. Like the early morning hours are the hours where the hares are very active and I watch them from a distance. So yes, there’s a mixture of emotional change and also practical changes in my habits. Culture

A NEW VOICE

Dalton already has plenty of experience writing political speeches for other people. It is high-stakes, challenging work. But in an extraordinary way it is the silent hare who has helped her find a new voice as a writer in her own right. Chloe Dalton: I could never have imagined this because I never saw myself as someone who could write a book. I made my way in the world writing for other people who had really big, important jobs. And I thought it was an incredible privilege, which it was, to write for them, you know, to write for the British Foreign Secretary at the time, to have things that I had written, spoken, you know, at the State Department or the UN Security Council or by other big international figures… [it] felt like all I could possibly ever dream of achieving. But I write in the book about how I realised at some point in this experience that I’d lived my life a little bit like a hare. I’d been a bit camouflaged, a bit too hidden. And this is a story in part about how an utterly silent creature helped me find my own voice. And I think lots of different people at different stages in their life can relate to that feeling, that you don’t realise perhaps there’s a change that you need.

ADVOCATING FOR NATURE

Despite the new direction as a writer, Dalton does not want to abandon politics completely, as she believes it has a role in addressing urgent issues, including the biodiversity crisis. Chloe Dalton: Part of me is still very strongly drawn towards politics. It’s hard when you care about certain issues to completely step away when you see, you know, for instance, some of the issues that I worked on in government have been undone, quite apart from the new things that have happened in the world that we’re seeing at the moment. And it’s just that reminder, you know, progress isn’t linear, you can’t just take it for granted. So I feel a bit torn, I definitely want to write more and follow my interest in animals… I think of it as almost the hare left me these tracks in the landscape and I’m now following them. But sometimes I think at some point I might put down my pen and go back and be a bit more active in politics because there’s a lot to fight for.

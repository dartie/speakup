Crooked Houses: Britain’s Wonky Homes [B2]

Non vi preoccupate, non è la vostra vista a ingannarvi: queste case sono proprio storte! Una caratteristica piuttosto insolita che le ha rese curiose attrazioni.

The Spanish-American philosopher George Santayana once described England as being a “paradise of individuality, eccentricity, heresy, anomalies, hobbies, and humours.” It’s no surprise then, that the isle is home to an impressive collection of houses that look like they’ve had one too many at the local pub! Across the country, you’ll find houses that tilt, lean and sag in ways that would give any surveyor a headache. Here’s a tour of some of the best.

SUFFOLK SWAYS

Suffolk is particularly well-furnished in wonky houses. The culprit? Medieval ambition. A boom in the wool trade meant houses had to go up fast, so builders used green, unseasoned timber that hadn’t been properly dried. Over the centuries the wood warped, and the buildings warped with it. The town of Lavenham is full of these houses, and like most others in Suffolk, they are coloured a joyful rosy hue known as ‘Suffolk pink’. One of them stands out for being orange and particularly welcoming: built in 1395 as part of a grand house for a wealthy wool merchant, past visitors include Queen Elizabeth I and John Lennon. You can book a tour to learn all about its six hundred-year old history. More crooked houses in the medieval village of Lavenham. Robert Louis Stevenson was so fascinated by the colourful contortions in Lavenham that he thought the houses “ought to be in a novel.” One of them even ended up in Godric’s Hollow, the magical village in the Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows film.

WONKY BUILDINGS

Until recently, Staffordshire’s Crooked House was Britain’s most famous wonky building. The pub was so badly affected by subsidence that its leaning walls gave rise to optical illusions, such as bottles appearing to roll upwards along tables, which is either a great party trick or a sign you should probably drink somewhere else! It burnt down in suspicious circumstances in 2023 and was then demolished, but South Staffordshire Council has demanded the owner rebuild it exactly as it was. That legal fight is still ongoing. Ci sono diverse leggende urbane… Justin Ratcliffe Windsor has its own 17th-century crooked house. It sits on the corner of Market Street and has a very confident lean. Like the Suffolk houses, the cause is unseasoned green oak.

A LITTLE TOO TIPSY

The House of the Golden Key in Tewkesbury has a different problem: it tips forward over the street at an angle that makes passers-by slightly nervous. The tilt happened in the 17th century when an attempt to add a storey caused the ridge piece to slip. It has stayed that way ever since.

HOW DO YOU SAY…?

Perdere le staffe. To hit the roof. And then there’s Little Moreton Hall in Cheshire, a grand Tudor manor house that is slowly collapsing on its south side, having been built with virtually no foundations. Iron rods were inserted in the 19th century to slow things down. The National Trust now notes that the fireplace in the Long Gallery may be the only truly vertical thing in the entire building.

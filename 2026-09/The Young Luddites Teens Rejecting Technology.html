The Young Luddites: Teens Rejecting Technology [C1]

C’è una rivoluzione in atto: mentre molti adolescenti sono dipendenti dagli smartphone e dai social media, altri hanno scelto di dire no alla tecnologia per riscoprire valori e passatempi di altri tempi.

Multiple studies have found that smartphones and social media have a negative impact on young people, often contributing to anxiety, depression, sleep disturbances, reduced social skills, and impaired cognitive functioning. It was during the COVID-19 pandemic that teenager Logan Lane realised that her excessive use of smartphones and social media was having a negative impact on her life. When she returned to school at Edward R. Murrow High School in Brooklyn, New York, she decided to do something about it. She helped to start the Luddite Club, a safe and  accepting group for “young people to connect with each other and to conquer Big Tech’s addictive agendas.”

19TH-CENTURY ORIGINS

The Luddite Club is named after the historical Luddites, a group of 19th-century English textile workers who protested against the introduction of new machinery, which they perceived as a threat to their livelihoods. Today, the club has more than twenty-five chapters across the United States, as well as one in Australia and another in Stockholm, Sweden, mostly made up of students and other young people, and encourages members to prioritise in-person interactions over online experiences. It organises regular meet-ups at which they engage in things like reading, hiking and simply enjoying each other’s company, and it publishes a quarterly newsletter, The Luddite Dispatch, issued exclusively as a physical, hardcopy, publication.

TECHNO-CRITICAL

Twenty-year-old Lane describes herself as “a techno critic and an activist” and, like most members of the Luddite Club, has a flip phone — also called a ‘dumb phone’ — instead of a smartphone and isn’t on social media. By limiting her use of technology, she has rediscovered her love of reading, and she has recently committed to writing at least one letter a day to her many techno-critical pen pals around the world. The Luddite Dispatch is a print-only newsletter published by The Luddite Club.

CREATING A COMMUNITY

Speak Up chatted with Lane to find out more about the process that led her to give up smartphones and step away from social media. Logan Lane (American accent): It was definetely gradual decision. I think the question that was over my head constantly when I was in seventh, eight and ninth grade was just, “How do you want to spend your time?” And how I was doing that was online, and I didn’t want to be consuming as much junk content as I was. And there was nothing in the way between me and my devices, and I could just kind of really overindulge. And through that overindulgence, I kind of just came to a breaking point where I decided I wanted to see how long I could go without my smartphone. And I ended up sticking with it because of how positive an experience I had. After COVID, I had a good eight or nine months where I had no phone at all. And then I went back to high school with a flip phone, and I just sort of felt really isolated by my decision. I knew I was doing what was best for me, but I felt like I was sectioned off from my peers because I wasn’t on social media anymore and I was sort of hard to reach. And so I wanted to create a community where we could make it easier for each other. But I also was just increasingly disgusted by the environment that the school was when I came back after COVID. Things certainly got worse: people increasingly anxious, depressed, unable to talk to each other, unable to pay attention.  Current Affairs

ATTENTION, AUTONOMY, CHOICE

Lane outlines the many cognitive and emotional effects of smartphone use and explains how giving them up led her back to reading. Logan Lane: I think the main effects have to do with time, attention, autonomy, and choice. You know, you think that you have choice when you open your smartphone, but really you’re kind of drawn to these applications you might not even want to open, but you’re just driven by impulse and habit. And we’re just, as a generation, way less socially well-off than any other before. On social media I experienced a comparative-driven egotism. Just constantly thinking about yourself because you’re comparing yourself to other people. You’re seeing people who are so much more brilliant than you, so much more attractive, you know, skinnier, clearer skin, stuff like that. I think intellectually as well, I mean, none of us are reading anymore. And for me, I really started reading when I got rid of my phone. 

IN-PERSON, NOT ONLINE

More and more young people are getting rid of their smartphones and joining the club, Lane explains. In her view, disconnecting from social media can bring clear social benefits, even if it sometimes means feeling isolated at first. Logan Lane:I don’t know about the people who join the club outside of my own experience and my three clubs that I’ve been part of. But people who want to start clubs are typically college students, some high schoolers, an occasional adult, a lot of college kids. And some of them have a flip phone and they’re just, like, isolated and heard about us and thought, “Oh, this would be a great way to find other people.” ‘Cause it’s a really wonderful thing for your socialization to get rid of your smartphone. However, when you do it in an environment where everyone else has a smartphone, it can be increasingly isolating. I didn’t mention this earlier, but I think the big detrimental effect of the smartphones is a decrease in community. If we have community online, we think we don’t need community in-person. But realistically, it’s actually like, two totally separate things, and we can’t be rejecting in-person community the way that we are.

WEEKLY ACTIVITIES

Although each chapter of the Club operates independently, Lane gives a sense of the kinds of activities that bring participants together. Logan Lane: All the clubs have weekly meetings, and then what we do in them really depends on the club, like, what chapter it is. In my clubs, we’ve done a lot of, like, readings. We’ll go on walks, we’ll go on hikes, we will design posters, we’ll do, like, letter writing… Things like that. 

MUTUAL ACCOUNTABILITY

For those who feel trapped by their smartphone or social media habits, breaking away can seem next to impossible. Lane shares some practical advice based on her own experience and the community she has helped build. Logan Lane: The most important tip I would say is, it is so much easier to accomplish things when you’re doing them in community. I don’t know a single person in my life who doesn’t resent a tiny bit their relationship to their technology, how much they use it. So find a friend and try and build this mutual accountability system where you both try and use social media less or take a break. I find it’s very hard to immediately downgrade it to use it less. So I think, like, getting away from it and not using it for a while is very helpful. Find a friend to do it with, and because you want them to accomplish their goal, you’ll sort of stick to what you promised as well.

LOVE OF LEARNING

Stepping away from screens has also opened up new ways of spending time. Reflecting on her own habits, Lane talks about the activities she has rediscovered. Logan Lane: I’m committed to writing a letter every single day. I’ve got, like, all of these pen pals. I’m really into running, writing in general, journaling, creative writing… I consider myself a life learner. I love learning new things and reading seems to be the main way to independently do that. Oh, I don’t know how much of a hobby this is, but I’m learning Russian. So I try and do that every day.

HUNDREDS OF LETTERS

What once seemed like an eccentric choice is beginning to feel less unusual. From her perspective, Lane observes a growing backlash against digital overload and explains what is driving it. Logan Lane: Four years ago when I got a flip phone, everyone thought I was crazy. It didn’t feel like anyone really got what I was doing, aside from just going against the grain because it was fun. Now it feels like every other person I meet knows somebody who has a flip phone, which is progress. It’s definitely growing. Now it feels like people really get it. I mean, I get so many letters. Every time I come back from a break from school, our PO box is like hundreds of letters. Yeah, it’s just incredible. There’s so many people around the country rejecting tech. It’s been years and years of oversaturation and the algorithms, they’re only getting better at pinning us down and figuring out what’s gonna hold our attention the most and the longest. And, you know, people are fed up with that.

FIGHT WORRY WITH HOPE

Given the increasingly pervasive nature of technology, the question of what lies ahead may seem disheartening. But rather than letting herself be discouraged, Lane chooses optimism. Logan Lane: I really try not to worry because this is my number one cause. I care about this so much and I’ve been thinking about this for years and years and years and it’s just like not productive for me to be worrying so much. I just try and battle the worry with hope. As much as the tech is so scary, I also think this resistance movement is also very strong and I really believe in it. You know, I believe in it enough to try to spread the gospel. I really deeply care about this and think that we’re actively pursuing a solution and I have faith that more and more people will continue to keep getting involved in the movement.   www.theludditeclub.org

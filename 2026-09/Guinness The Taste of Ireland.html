Guinness: The Taste of Ireland [C1]

250 anni e non sentirli! L’iconico marchio irlandese rimane un mito anche per la Generazione Z, al centro di trend virali e protagonista di una serie Netflix.

Go into an Irish pub anywhere in the world and you’ll almost certainly see the bartender pouring a pint of the Irish dry stout Guinness. Founded by Arthur Guinness in 1759, Guinness has evolved into an iconic brand and global symbol of Irish identity. It is now experiencing an international boom, driven by strategic rebranding, viral social media trends, and the international popularity of the Netflix series House of Guinness.

PINT WITH VIEWS

Guinness is made from just four simple ingredients: water, barley, hops, and yeast. It was first brewed at St. James’s Gate Brewery in Dublin, an historic site that is still a functioning brewery today. The brewery is also home to the Guinness Storehouse, Ireland’s most-visited tourist attraction, with over 1.5 million visitors a year. The Storehouse celebrated its 25th anniversary last year and, since opening in 2000, has welcomed more than twenty-eight million visitors from around the world. Here, visitors can explore the brand’s history through a seven-storey immersive experience, before heading to the Gravity Bar on the top floor which boasts 360° views of the city. Every pint still undergoes its distinctive settling process as nitrogen bubbles create Guinness’s famous creamy head and velvety texture.

THE BRAND

Today, the Guinness brand is owned by Diageo, the British multinational beverage company behind a wide portfolio of global spirits and beer brands. Catherine Toolan is Managing Director of Diageo Irish Brand Homes. She spoke to Speak Up about what makes the Guinness Storehouse such a popular destination for visitors from around the world, and the culture surrounding the stout beer. Catherine Toolan (Irish accent): I think the Guinness Storehouse is a really special place because visitors come here to discover more than the story of Guinness. They come here to discover a piece of Irish history, a bit of culture. We’re very known for warm hospitality and our role is to bring that story of Guinness and a little bit of Ireland to life for a global audience. We’ve welcomed people from two hundred countries and they’ve come here to celebrate a little bit of the heritage of Guinness. Over the seven floors of the Guinness Storehouse we get to bring the amazing story of Guinness to life, past, present, and future. We’re a brand that is about celebrating, it’s about breaking bread, it’s about communion. The Guinness Storehouse itself is an old fermentation plant, but it’s actually built in the shape of a pint glass. So by the time you get to the top of the experience, you’re really at the top of one of the tallest buildings in Dublin. It was actually built as one of the skyscrapers in Dublin. Culture

POURING METHOD

In 1759, Arthur Guinness signed the now-legendary nine-thousand-year lease on a disused brewery at St. James’s Gate, a bold decision that still defines the brand. That same blend of patience and care survives today in the ritual of the pint itself, most famously in the two-part pour, consisting of a first pour up to the logo of the harp in the glass, then a pause of exactly 119.5 seconds to let it settle, and a final top-up. Catherine Toolan: Guinness has a remarkable history that stretches back to 1759 when Arthur Guinness took that bold leap of faith and he signed the famous nine-thousand-year lease on a disused beer brewery here in St. James’s Gate in Dublin. We’re only kind of 260 years into that nine-thousand-year lease. This part of Dublin where we’re based was always known for brewing, but it was a real leap of faith for Arthur Guinness to sign that lease. And it starts with that very inspiring story of innovation, which actually still inspires us today, and also the ambition and the craft that he put behind [it]. And it’s incredible that more than 260 years later, Guinness is still brewed and shipped all around the world at the same historic site. And it’s extraordinary when you think about the pace of change around the world. And I think what makes Guinness iconic is not just simply its longevity, it’s [that] from the very beginning, Arthur had that pioneering spirit, that commitment to quality. We talk about the seven steps of pouring the perfect pint of Guinness, the four ingredients that make Guinness, and that craftsmanship and innovation continues today. Throughout its history, Guinness has pushed the boundaries in brewing. When we started advertising, we pushed the boundaries in advertising. The thing that we do every day and the brand still does so well is that sense of storytelling and creating a brand that’s recognised and admired and shared around the world. Its roots [are] firmly in Dublin and in Ireland, but today Guinness is enjoyed in almost every corner of the world. And that global success, it’s down to its very distinctive character as a beer. It’s instantly recognisable through the taste, through the two-part pour, which is iconic. It takes 119.5 seconds for a pint of Guinness to settle and, you know, creates a really great story if you live around the world.  Today, the Guinness Storehouse is one of Dublin’s most recognisable landmarks.

VISITOR EXPERIENCES

For generations, Guinness has been inseparable from Dublin and from Irish social life. It’s been part of celebrations, especially present in music, sport and everyday events, and one of the first associations many visitors make with the country. In recent years, however, the brand has transformed its image to become a Gen Z status symbol, attracting celebrity endorsements from the likes of Kim Kardashian and Olivia Rodrigo. It has repositioned itself for a younger audience with the global rollout of Guinness 0.0, now the world’s most popular non-alcoholic stout, and new flagship spaces such as the Open Gate Brewery in Covent Garden, a £73 million facility with a microbrewery, restaurant, and immersive visitor experiences. It has recently announced a €100 million investment in St. James’s Gate Brewery, to accelerate the site to net-zero carbon emissions by 2030. All of this while also staying true to its origins, Toolan notes, balancing a strong sense of heritage with a constant drive to evolve. Catherine Toolan: It’s still part of the fabric of our national story and it represents that [those] generations of craftsmanship. Here in the Guinness Storehouse, for example, where we welcome almost two million visitors a year, things like pouring your own pint, where people get to pour their own pint and learn about that craft of the two-part pour, is really special. And so we talk about that sense of the Guinness Storehouse moving from maybe what was, in Guinness, maybe a 2D brand that maybe wasn’t for everyone, to people feeling part of the brand. And we talk in Guinness about the power and goodness of communion. And in the past, maybe Guinness was associated with rugby and, you know, various things like that. But now the power of Guinness is about those sharing moments when you get to have a pint with somebody, friends, whether it be in London or LA or right here in the Guinness Storehouse in Dublin. It’s really some of those iconic moments where people feel that sense of celebration. 

THE GUINNESS HARP

The Guinness harp is one of the most recognisable logos in the world. It is also closely linked to Ireland’s own national symbol, although, as Toolan notes, Guinness registered the harp as a trademark before it was adopted by the Irish state. Catherine Toolan: I suppose the other thing with Guinness is, it actually had registered the trademark, it was one of the first registered trademarks in Ireland. But actually the Irish harp, it’s faced the other way around because Guinness had registered the harp as their trademark, the Brian Boru’s Harp, actually before the Irish government registered it when the Irish state was [had] been formed. But they had to turn it around the opposite way.

NATIONAL IDENTITY

Few brands are so closely tied to national identity. Born in Dublin, Guinness has become one of the most recognisable symbols of Ireland. It is a familiar presence that, wherever it is found, carries with it a sense of place and belonging, which, as Toolan points out, is carefully preserved in the Guinness Archive, one of the largest commercial archives in the world. Catherine Toolan: I think the Guinness family has had a huge impact on society. The brewery was one of Ireland’s largest employers for many families. Securing a job at Guinness, you know, was considered a job for life. And long before some of these practices became commonplace, Guinness employees benefited from things like pension and healthcare and housing support and welfare programmes. The sense of giving back and philanthropy is huge and it’s dotted right over the city of Dublin. Everywhere you go, you can see the fingerprints of Guinness and the Guinness family. And I think that progressive spirit helped shape the company’s reputation and it created this lasting connection between Guinness and the people of Dublin. When we look at the Guinness Archive — it is actually located here in the Guinness Storehouse — it’s just a fascinating record of that legacy, it’s one of the largest commercial archives in the world and it contains generations of stories and photographs and employment records that help not only tell the story of Guinness, but actually also the story of Dublin and the people whose lives were connected here to the brewery. It’s one of the things I absolutely love is, when a visitor comes to the archive and we’re able to trace their family connection, and they get to learn about the role that Guinness played in shaping the city, but maybe the role their family member played in that, too. So it feels really special that Guinness is not just about a brand, but it belongs to Ireland’s past and its present.

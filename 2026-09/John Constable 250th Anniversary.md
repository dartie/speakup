Two hundred and fifty years after his birth, John Constable remains one of Britain’s most celebrated artists. Born in Suffolk in 1776, Constable found inspiration in the countryside around his home. He created works that transformed landscape painting by focusing on the places he knew and [loved best](## "amare di più").

## FROM SUFFOLK TO THE WORLD
Constable grew up in the village of East Bergholt, near the River Stour, where his father owned [mills](## "mulini") and [traded](## "commerciare") boats. The fields, rivers and villages of Suffolk became his favourite subjects and remained central to his work throughout his life.
Throughout 2026, Suffolk has been marking the anniversary of Constable’s birth with a programme of exhibitions, events and creative projects called Constable 250. One of the highlights has been [The Hay Wain](## ""Il carro di fieno""): Walking Constable’s Landscape, which brought the famous painting back to Suffolk for the first time, reuniting it with [sketches](## "bozzetti"), drawings and related works from major collections from the UK.
Constable’s painting [The Hay Wain](## ""Il carro di fieno"") from 1821. While the painting seems conservative today, it was revolutionary for the time, forcing viewers to slow down and become immersed in the emotion of natural detail.

## AN ARTIST IN THE MAKING
To find out more, Speak Up spoke to Emma Roodhouse, Art Collections & Learning Curator at Colchester and Ipswich Museums and lead curator of Constable 250. As Roodhouse explained, although Constable is now [regarded](## "considerare") as one of Britain’s greatest painters, his artistic ambitions were not always encouraged, as his family expected him to work in the milling business. But his passion [lay elsewhere](## "risiedere altrove").
**Emma Roodhouse (English accent):** His family, his father in particular, wanted his young son to enter into the family business of milling and looking after the boats that [sail](## "navigare") on the River Stour. But Constable had no desire to [follow in his father’s footsteps](## "seguire le orme di qualcuno"). He really was passionate about drawing and sketching and art. So he was the first artist of the family, and he had to prove to them that he could become an artist and make a career. We have in our collection a wonderful example of his interest in art, but having to do the things that his parents really wanted him to do. So at the age of sixteen, he had to go and work in a mill called Pitt’s Mill in East Bergholt. And while he was there, he [carved](## "intagliare") into one of the [wooden timbers](## "wooden timbers") in the mill a picture of the mill, and he signed his name and [dated](## "datare") it. Because really, he was more interested in drawing the mill than actually working there. And that piece of wood, the carving, is in our collections. So we’re able to show a really early artwork by the artist from when he was a teenager, and probably shouldn’t have been carving on wood at the time.

## THE HAY WAIN
Constable’s most famous painting, [The Hay Wain](## ""Il carro di fieno""), was completed in 1821 and depicts a [horse-drawn cart](## "carro trainato da cavalli") crossing the [millpond](## "laghetto del mulino") at Flatford. Although the scene is now recognised around the world, it was not an immediate success in Britain. Its reputation grew after it was exhibited in Paris in 1824, where the freshness and originality of Constable’s approach earned a gold medal from King Charles X of France. Today, [The Hay Wain](## ""Il carro di fieno"") is one of the most reproduced images in British art. Yet behind its popularity lies a deeply personal connection to the Suffolk landscape.
**Emma Roodhouse: **[The Hay Wain](## ""Il carro di fieno"") is part of a series of large-scale paintings. They were six foot wide or high [180cm], and they were painted from 1819 to 1825. There’s a house with a white colouring, which is known as Willy Lott’s house, and you see Willy Lott’s house in the distance, and the [millpond](## "laghetto del mulino") with Flatford Mill behind, and the cart in the water. There’s a [restless](## "irrequieto") boy in the back of the cart, and the cart is known as the ‘wain’, and it’s carrying hay. And the painting was [noticed](## "notare") by lots of people, but it became more famous when it went to Paris in 1824, and people absolutely couldn’t believe this painting was so fresh and wonderful, that the king, Charles, actually gave the painting a gold medal. And the medal is in the National Gallery’s collection, because [The Hay Wain](## ""Il carro di fieno"") would eventually be donated to the National Gallery in 1886. And after that, it starts to become reproduced in posters and postcards. You could find it, at one point, in the British passport. So it’s a very well-reproduced image, but it still speaks to us of the countryside, and the warmth in nature, and the connection that we can all feel with our own landscapes.
The painting Boat building Near Flatford Mill (1814-1815).

## A LANDSCAPE THAT SURVIVES
The countryside around East Bergholt, Flatford and the River Stour lies on the border between the counties of Suffolk and Essex, within the Dedham Vale National Landscape. National Landscapes are protected areas recognised for their exceptional natural beauty. Until 2023 they were officially known as Areas of Outstanding Natural Beauty (AONBs), a designation first given to Dedham Vale in 1970. Many of the views painted by Constable remain recognisable today and are protected for future generations. Visitors can still walk through much of the landscape that Constable knew and painted, including the area around Willy Lott’s House, the [cottage](## "casa di campagna") depicted in [The Hay Wain](## ""Il carro di fieno""), which has been carefully preserved and remains largely unchanged.
**Emma Roodhouse:**We’re very fortunate that Flatford has been preserved, and Willy Lott’s house is still to be seen today. [Even though](## "anche se") in 1920 the house was in [disarray](## "degrado"), it was falling down, and it didn’t look quite so good as it does at the moment. And that’s because after the First World War, people had left it to [go to rack and ruin](## "andare in rovina"). But there was an Ipswich man, Thomas Parkington, and he bought the house and decided to preserve it, and eventually it would then become part of the National Trust’s property portfolio. And the surrounding area is designated a National Landscape, which means that it’s conserved and protected,  from the trees to the wildlife in the water. So that means when you walk around Flatford, you can walk down to Flatford Mill, and if you were standing looking at where the painting was produced, the mill would be behind you, and you would look out across the water towards Willy Lott’s house. So you can really immerse yourself into the world that Constable knew and the world that he really loved to paint.
Willy Lott’s Cottage appears in several paintings by Constable. The earliest part of the building is sixteenth century. It was restored in the 1920s after a revival of interest in Constable’s paintings.

## WALKING THE LANDSCAPE
Walking is one of the central themes of the 250th anniversary celebrations. Visitors are encouraged to explore the same [paths](## "sentieri"), rivers and fields that inspired Constable more than two centuries ago. According to Roodhouse, walking in nature was fundamental to the artist’s way of seeing the world.
**Emma Roodhouse:** Walking was really important to Constable as a very young boy. If you ever get the chance to visit East Bergholt and Flatford, and the local area, you start to understand that as a young boy, when he goes to school, he has to walk at least a good couple of miles from his house in East Bergholt down to Flatford, across the river and the [meadows](## "prati"), [all the way to](## "fino a") Dedham, ‘cause that’s where his school was. And when you do the repetition of a walk, those walks became so [imbued with](## "impregnate di") meaning, and you would start to observe the changes  in the seasons, and the colours of the leaves as they change, and the skies, and the different clouds. And he really [built up](## "accumulare") that knowledge of the local area through constant walking.

## PAINTING AND FEELING
Constable once wrote that “painting is but another word for feeling.” More than two centuries later, that idea remains at the heart of the anniversary celebrations.
**Emma Roodhouse:** I really hope people see the difference in Constable as a radical artist of the time who really was doing something different. Now, I think we see these paintings in their big [gilt frames](## "cornici dorate"), and they seem very traditional, but he was painting a landscape that wasn’t familiar to the London audiences, and it wasn’t the traditional way of painting landscape, to go outside and paint what you [loved best](## "amare di più"). He took that and really focused in on it. So I hope people get that understanding of what he was trying to do with the paintings of landscapes, and how he really felt. He famously writes that, “Painting is but another word for feeling.” And he writes that to his friend, because for him, all of these landscapes have really deep emotions and feelings attached to them. And I would love it if people would leave the exhibitions, perhaps they [go off](## "andarsene") and decide to paint their own landscapes, or write a poem, or take a picture of something that they love and we all like to keep those precious images that we have together. So perhaps it just inspires somebody to [go off](## "andarsene") and do those little acts of creativity. And that would be great.

## CONSTABLE AND NATURE TODAY
Although Constable painted the countryside more than two hundred years ago, his work continues to inspire conversations about our relationship with nature and the environment.
**Emma Roodhouse:** I really hope that the exhibitions do [ignite](## "accendere, suscitare") that connection with nature. And seeing the landscapes and the trees in these paintings and, and wanting to perhaps go on those walks and just really immerse yourself in the environment, helps you perhaps even promote small changes that you might do within your own gardens or spaces that you have. In the village of East Bergholt, they are planting 250 trees this year with a particular type of tree, the [black poplar](## "pioppo nero"), which Constable enjoyed painting, and was much more part of the sort of environment. But unfortunately, over the years, there’s not as many of them. So in the village they’re [giving away](## "regalare") [seedlings](## "piantine") and getting people to plant trees wherever they can. And I think that’s a really nice part of this project, and I hope that it inspires people to do more of that.

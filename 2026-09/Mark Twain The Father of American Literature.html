Mark Twain: The Father of American Literature [B2]

Considerato il padre della letteratura americana, seppe cogliere lo spirito contraddittorio degli Stati Uniti dopo la Guerra Civile con la sua pungente satira sociale e l’uso dei dialetti regionali.

Mark Twain was born Samuel Langhorne Clemens on 30 November 1835. Considered the father of American literature, his masterful use of vernacular speech, biting wit, ferocious social satire, and deep understanding of human nature revolutionised the world of fiction in the US. His was the critical voice of a rapidly-changing nation in a period he himself called The Gilded Age. 

MISSISSIPPI AS A SYMBOL

Born in Missouri in the village of Florida, Twain grew up in the bustling town of Hannibal on the Mississippi River. Steamboats, a slave-owning society, and the adventures of childhood along the riverbanks would colour his imagination indelibly. Twain hated school and dreamt of becoming a riverboat pilot. His first job, however, was as a printer’s apprentice and typesetter, which “helped him figure out how words go together.” Finally, after a two-year training period, he became a Mississippi River pilot. The Mississippi was the central symbol of his literary work. The main artery of the slavery business: one million men, women and children were transported down the river for sale on steamboats from 1820 to 1860.  Mark Twain in 1910.

ACCLAIM AS A WRITER

When the Civil War broke out in 1861, Twain served briefly as a Confederate soldier. He then headed west in the 1861 Gold Rush, before working as a miner and finally a journalist. THE GREAT AMERICAN NOVEL

Twain then published the two novels which would make him famous. In The Adventures of Tom Sawyer (1876), a coming-of-age tale, he captured the nostalgic, romantic, light-hearted spirit, and imagination of his boyhood. Tom is a mischievous but good-hearted orphan who shares adventures with his best friend, Huckleberry Finn. Its sequel, Adventures of Huckleberry Finn (1884) cemented Twain’s place in literary history. A runaway boy and an escaped slave travel down the Mississippi, looking for freedom, in a novel tackling the harsh reality of America’s original sin, slavery.  

TRAGEDIES

Twain regularly invested in new technology. Disaster struck in 1894, when a failed investment in the Paige Compositor, a complex typesetting machine, left him bankrupt. Suffering from depression and anxiety, he decided to pay off his debts with a worldwide lecture tour in 1895-96. Delighting audiences with his quick wit and eloquence, he became an international celebrity. He returned to the US with his wealth and public reputation restored.

Always do right. This will gratify some people, and astonish the rest.

In his private life, however, Twain suffered a series of family tragedies. He and his wife Livy lost their son, Langdon, at just nineteen months, in 1872. Susy, his favourite daughter, died aged twenty-four in 1896 from spinal meningitis. His wife died in 1904, followed by their youngest daughter Jean, an epileptic, in 1909. Only Clara, his middle daughter, outlived him.

AN IMMEASURABLE IMPACT

These terrible events, together with disillusionment regarding human nature, imperialism, religion, and institutional greed, filled the writer with bitter anger. His final works were dark and cynical. He died just four months after Jean. Twain’s impact on American literature and culture is immeasurable. Ernest Hemingway famously said that “all modern American literature comes from Huckleberry Finn.” Twain was the first major American author to successfully use vernacular language. He democratised American literature by demonstrating that regional voices could describe and confront the country’s most serious moral issues. He also entertained millions, using his wonderful sense of humour as a weapon against injustice.

HUCKLEBERRY FINN

Before this novel, American authors had written in formal English, copying European styles. Breaking the mould, Twain’s story is narrated through the voice, faithfully reproduced, of an uneducated backwoods boy. Social outcast Huckleberry Finn is living with the wealthy Widow Douglas, who is trying to civilise him, against his wishes. Kidnapped by his abusive, alcoholic father, Huckleberry manages to escape to an island on the Mississippi River, where he meets Jim, a runaway slave. The outcast’s conscience is divided, as society says that helping a runaway slave is a sin, but he wants to help. In a famous line, Huckleberry says, “All right, then, I’ll go to hell!”, as he decides to help Jim escape down the river to freedom. Our anti-hero grows into a hero, choosing individual morality and human loyalty over the hypocrisy of a corrupt society. Confronting America’s original sin, slavery and systemic racism, Twain forces Americans to confront themselves. Cover from the 1885 original edition of Mark Twain’s Adventures of Huckleberry Finn with illustrations by E. W. Kemble; Eddie Hodges played Huckleberry Finn in the 1960 series. Huckleberry Finn is one of the most criticised, banned books in history. During the time of the Civil Rights movement in the US, in the 1950s and 1960s, concerns were raised about the book, especially its language. The racial slur ‘nigger’ appears 219 times. Scholars say Twain used it to portray the reality of the pre-Civil War American South, but others say the book is simply racist, with the slur used even by the story’s hero.

A FIGHTER AGAINST INJUSTICE

Mark Twain fought against slavery all his life. He was shocked when his own parents ordered the whipping of Jennie, the black slave who had nursed him and his siblings, for insubordination. The “woman was almost like one of the family”, Twain wrote. Jennie then demanded to be sold.  Twain’s attitude towards American Indians changed from racist prejudice during his time in the West to later empathy and anti-imperialist anger as he recognised the terrible impact of European and American colonisation. Twain’s friend William Dean Howells described the writer as having “an indignant sense of right and wrong, a scorn of all affectation and pretence, an ardent hate of meanness and injustice.” The writer loved modern technology and inventions. He was one of the first Americans to have a telephone at home. The inventor Thomas Edison visited Twain at his Connecticut home in 1909 and made the only known film of the writer, dressed in his iconic white suit, with his daughters Clara and Jean. He adored his family, and the deaths of his wife and three of his children almost destroyed him. His wife Livy (Olivia), the daughter of a New York coal baron, was fundamental to his success, brilliantly editing his work. The Mark Twain House and Museum in Hartford, Connecticut. The author’s outsized personality helped to make him famous. He made his unruly hair an important part of his public persona, laughing at it at times. He delighted in publicity and was a friend to presidents, artists, industrialists and European royalty. But he was also a friend to his neighbours, especially the local farmers. Twain was born just after the appearance of Halley’s Comet in 1835. He died one day after the comet was at its closest to the sun in 1910. Predicting this coincidence, Twain himself wrote: “The Almighty has said, no doubt: ‘Now here are these two unaccountable freaks; they came in together, they must go out together.’”

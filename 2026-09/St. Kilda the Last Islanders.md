The majestic and isolated archipelago of St. Kilda, whose [cliffs](## "scogliere") famously [tower over](## "ergersi sopra, dominare") Atlantic waters some forty miles off Scotland’s [western edge](## "confine occidentale"), has captivated imaginations for over two hundred years. 
Cut off from the [mainland](## "continente") by [treacherous](## "insidiosi") seas and [relentless](## "implacabili") storms, St. Kilda became a sanctuary for Britain’s largest seabird colony. For four thousand years, St. Kildans lived a life of [remarkable](## "notevole") self-sufficiency by exploiting the dense colonies of [gannets](## "sule"), [fulmars](## "fulmari") and [puffins](## "pulcinelle di mare") for food, [feathers](## "piume"), and oil.

## THE IMPACT OF TOURISM
From the 18th century, tourists started to [flock](## "affluire") to the isles, fascinated by St. Kildans’ ability to survive in such a [harsh](## "duro, inospitale") landscape. Yet, as outsiders brought [goods](## "merci"), they also carried disease — tetanus and other illnesses started to erode the fragile community. Locals were [cheated out of their possessions](## "spogliare dei propri beni con l’inganno"), and when introduced to the [conveniences](## "comodità") of [tinned food](## "cibo in scatola"), started to lose their survival skills. Other guests [compelled](## "obbligare") them to attend church daily, which meant they had less time to take care of their [crops](## "coltivazioni") and [seabird harvest](## "caccia di uccelli marini").
An archive image from 1927 that shows the men of St. Kilda meeting to discuss their plans for the day at The Village Parliament.

## FINAL EXODUS
When World War One broke out, a [detachment](## "distaccamento") of British Soldiers was set up on St. Kilda, and the population rose — locals were employed and kept very busy. In the [aftermath](## "conseguenze") of the war, when all of the work disappeared, St. Kildans were left [destitute](## "in miseria") and were [compelled](## "obbligare") to find jobs on the [mainland](## "continente"). In 1930, the thirty-six remaining islanders, [lacking](## "mancare di") the support of their community, had no other choice but to abandon their ancestral home, leaving behind a [hauntingly](## "in modo inquietante") beautiful landscape.

## A PEOPLE’S HISTORY
To find out more about St. Kilda’s history, Speak Up spoke to Roger Hutchinson, author of St. Kilda: A People’s History. We started by asking him to explain what makes St. Kilda so unique. 
**Roger Hutchinson (Scottish accent):** The significance of the St. Kilda Islands in Scottish history is [chiefly](## "principalmente") connected to their distance from anywhere else. There’s such a very long way away. The archipelago of St. Kilda, the three main islands, are eighty kilometres from the western coast of the Western Isles, which are themselves another eighty kilometres away from the Scottish [mainland](## "continente"). So St. Kilda is an incredibly long way away from anywhere. And the fact that it supported for millennia an indigenous group of Hebridean people, who spoke the language of the Hebrides Gaelic, Scottish Gaelic, that is not Irish Gaelic, and who were clearly part of Hebridean civilization, Hebridean culture — the fact that they survived on these islands, self-sufficient, was a source of fascination to people on the [mainland](## "continente") almost as soon as we got to hear about them. Our fascination with the St. Kildans began a few hundred years ago when intellectuals and people in the cities realized that just off the coast of the British Isles there was a kind of Hawaii, a sort of South Sea Islanders group of undiscovered people leading an [enclosed](## "chiusa, isolata") and integral life among themselves with [hardly](## "appena") any connection [whatsoever](## "qualsiasi") with the outside world. And as there were never more than a hundred people living there; it was [remarkable](## "notevole") that they managed to [get by](## "tirare avanti, cavarsela") without support from anywhere else. They were a sort of tabula rasa as well. They were a [blank slate](## "pagina bianca") upon which people could project and write their own fantasies of the evolution of Western civilizations.

## TOURIST TRADE
Once word of this remote utopia [spread](## "diffondersi"), curiosity grew steadily. What began as [a handful of](## "una manciata di") adventurous travellers — those [willing](## "disposti") to make the journey by small boat — gradually [drew](## "attirare") larger numbers. By the mid-19th century, St Kilda had turned into a tourist attraction.
**Roger Hutchinson:**People started going there to investigate. First of all, individual travellers would go. You could get a boat from the Western Isles, like you can today. It takes a few hours across the open Atlantic Ocean, but it’s [doable](## "fattibile, realizzabile"). And so a few people did go out there to see them. And then in the 19th century, as more boats became available, people started going out until in the end, by the middle of the 19th century, there were actually hired cruise vessels going out from [mainland](## "continente") ports in Britain out to St. Kilda. People from Lancashire and the northeast of England would [anchor](## "ancorare, gettare l’ancora") in the bay, [go ashore](## "sbarcare") and look at the locals who would sell them [bits and bobs](## "cianfrusaglie") of [rubbish](## "robaccia"), really, tourist [trinkets](## "ninnoli") and things like these. 

## MYTH OF COMMUNITY
The fascination for St. Kilda reached new levels when people started to mythologise the community, projecting onto its simple communal way of life the elaborate structures of their own imaginations.
**Roger Hutchinson:** People invented for them a king and a queen, which they didn’t have, and a parliament, which in any realistic sense they didn’t have, although the men would get together to discuss what work they would do that day, and everything basically other than an army. They were an ideal fantasy group of happy savages. On the one hand, they were sort of uncorrupted and able to lead perfect, [blissful](## "idilliache"), innocent lives in the Garden of Eden. And on the other, they were sort of easily corrupted by visiting diseases or bad habits such as alcohol, et cetera, et cetera. In fact, they were just Hebridean islanders who were a bit further away from the rest of everybody else than most of the other Hebrides. Today, there are still cruise ships which [dock](## "attraccare") in Village Bay in St. Kilda. Not every day, but in the summer, [in any given week](## "in una settimana qualsiasi"), you’d expect one or two cruise ships to go in there and see the place.

## MODERNITY
Part of our modern fascination with St. Kilda is tied to the tragic ending to life on the isles, brought about by outsiders and modernity. Centuries of self-sufficiency were eroded by disease and outside influence, and a fragile [reprieve](## "tregua") during World War One made its [aftermath](## "conseguenze") even harder. St. Kildans were left [stripped of](## "privare di") work and hope. On 29 August 1930, the thirty-six remaining islanders decided to evacuate their ancestral home. 
**Roger Hutchinson:** They had absolutely no choice. You see, there was a kind of Indian summer in St. Kilda around the time of the First World War, when infant mortality was reduced massively. It had have been a serious problem. They died from typhus infections, [chiefly](## "principalmente") infants. Most children died before they were a week old. They didn’t get [christened](## "battezzare") until they were a month old. They were expected to die. So infant mortality was a huge problem. [Inoculations](## "vaccinazioni") and the arrival of nurses from the [mainland](## "continente") took care of that. They were still a long way away from the early 20th century [mainland](## "continente") until the First World War came along when the first military base was established in St Kilda. It was actually a sort of [spotting range](## "area di osservazione"). A [detachment](## "distaccamento") of British soldiers was put up there in a quickly built [barracks](## "baracche, alloggi"). They employed locals. Women were employed in the [mess](## "mensa") to cook for them and men were employed as [spotters](## "vedette") from the [hilltops](## "cime delle colline") to look out into the Atlantic at the western approaches to see enemy ships coming, to monitor the waters and things like these. And of course, they [got on fine](## "andare d’accordo"). By that time, they tended to have a bit of English, the St. Kildans. And so communication with soldiers from the [mainland](## "continente") of Scotland and the UK wasn’t so much of a problem. The younger St. Kildans tended to speak some English by early in the 20th century. They had a school and a school teacher, you see. And so the population actually rose. It went up between 1911 and 1921, during the First World War. People had no reason to go away. They were making money. They were provisions being [shipped in](## "trasportare") by the army. They could [hitch a lift](## "farsi dare un passaggio") on boats, military boats, going back to the [mainland](## "continente") quite regularly. So throughout the First World War, life in St. Kilda was really quite busy and interesting for them. And then the war ended, of course, in 1918, and all these things disappeared [overnight](## "da un giorno all’altro"). They no longer had the jobs, they no longer had the money, they no longer had the provisions being imported. There were no longer a [garrison](## "guarnigione") of young men among them. So everything [fell away](## "scomparire gradualmente") very quickly.
St. Kilda cemetery.

## YOUTH EXODUS
St. Kilda started [emptying](## "spopolarsi"). The island’s younger generation, drawn by the promise of [steady](## "stabile") employment and urban opportunities, migrated en masse to industrial centres like the Clyde [shipyards](## "cantieri navali") and Glasgow’s [tenements](## "condomini popolari").
**Roger Hutchinson:** Lots of the younger St. Kildans left in the 1920s, went to the [mainland](## "continente"), mainly got jobs in industrial central Scotland and [elsewhere](## "altrove"). They left en masse in the 1920s. Until by the late 1920s, there were just a few kids and old ladies and young mothers and a couple of elderly men left there. Life may have been sustainable on St. Kilda, but it was only sustainable with [a good deal of](## "una grande quantità di") effort, mainly communal effort, to cut [peats](## "torba") for fuel. And the peat banks were quite a long way away from Village Bay where they lived, three miles away over a really [steep](## "scoscesa") hill. They couldn’t carry the [peats](## "torba") back, they couldn’t heat their houses or cook without [peats](## "torba"). Looking after the [livestock](## "bestiame") was difficult, and of course nobody was able to go after the birds.

## ENVIRONMENTAL INTEREST
In 1957, the military set up a radio station in order to monitor a missile testing range. That same year, St. Kilda became part of the National Trust for Scotland, a conservation charity dedicated to protecting Scotland’s most significant historical, cultural, and natural sites. The Trust’s role includes preventing environmental degradation and promoting education about the island’s extraordinary history. This transition marked a [turning point](## "punto di svolta") for St. Kilda, shifting its legacy from abandonment to protected heritage — becoming a UNESCO site in 1986. We asked Roger Hutchinson if it could ever be inhabited again.
**Roger Hutchinson: ** You couldn’t live there under current circumstances. The military base that’s there has helicopters coming in and out, as well as boat contact. There’s quite a few people on the rocket testing range there, but you couldn’t really live there. I mean, there’s no shops. You need provisions delivering. There’s no ferry to the [mainland](## "continente"), which is a requisite, really. There’s no way of getting off. There’s no hospital. It doesn’t have any of the 20th-century [facilities](## "infrastrutture") that people everywhere, including in the Hebrides, now expect. The houses that they left behind are still there. In fact, they’re looked after by the National Trust now. It’s a sort of national reserve. It’s a UNESCO World Heritage Site now, both for its natural environment and for its human history.

## STONES, BIRDS, AND EXTREME HARDSHIP
There are no trees in St. Kilda but a great amount of stone. St. Kildans became expert [stonemasons](## "scalpellini, tagliapietre") and built their villages, their houses and gargantuan walls to set up their [livestock](## "bestiame"). They had peat banks for fuel, to use for their fires. St. Kildans cultivated [crops](## "coltivazioni") and farmed sheep, [cattle](## "bestiame bovino") and ponies — but as no trees or [shrubs](## "arbusti") grew on the island, [crops](## "coltivazioni") were vulnerable to extreme weather. Wood was [scarce](## "scarso"), so making fishing boats was near-impossible. Birds were therefore of great importance for St. Kildans. Hebrideans were always good at scaling [cliffs](## "scogliere") for birds, and in St. Kilda, this was adapted into an art form, walking up and down vertical [slopes](## "pendii"). They would risk their lives to hunt birds by scaling the precipitous [cliffs](## "scogliere"), capturing them not by shooting them, but using [nooses](## "cappi scorrevoli") on the end of sticks, and searching for eggs. The birds’ meat was [dried](## "seccare") and stored and the eggs were eaten. Their oil provided fuel for lamps, and they started selling their [feathers](## "piume") in the 19th century to use for beds and [mattresses](## "materassi"), until the arrival of artificial [upholstery](## "tappezzeria") took over the market. St. Kildans had to be very economical, and so every part of the bird was used: the [beaks](## "becchi") became [brooch-pins](## "spille"), the bones were fashioned into [needles](## "aghi"), the skins of [gannets](## "sule") were turned into shoes.
[Trade](## "commercio") with the outside world was rare, [hindered](## "ostacolare") by the [harsh](## "duro, inospitale") Atlantic winters and violent storms that often isolated the islanders for months. In times of extreme [hardship](## "difficoltà") — such as the threat of [starvation](## "fame") — locals relied on a unique method of communication known as the St. Kilda Mail: they would place their [mess](## "mensa")ages in floating containers, attached to [buoyant](## "galleggianti") [sheep bladders](## "vesciche di pecora"), and release them into the sea, leaving their [pleas](## "suppliche") [to the mercy of](## "in balia di") the waves.

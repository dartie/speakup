About twenty-five years ago, Adeo Ressi, a [former](## "ex") college roommate of Elon Musk’s, [begged](## "implorare") the tech entrepreneur not to start a rocket company.
Musk had just [reaped](## "raccogliere, ottenere") millions of dollars from the sale of PayPal, which he helped create, to eBay. He and Ressi were looking at ways to send terrestrial plant life to Mars. They had a $50 million budget, but soon found it was not enough to cover the cost of a rocket. So Musk told his friend that he was going to build his own.
Ressi said he had organized a panel of space experts one day in a conference room of a hotel in Santa Monica, California, and brought Musk in to hear why the idea was a [fool’s errand](## "impresa inutile, senza speranza"). Private spaceflight was expensive, and the economics made little sense, the dozen or so people there said. But Musk did not [heed](## "dare ascolto") them.
“I really want to congratulate him for the incredible perseverance,” Ressi said in an interview. “For making it happen.”
Musk’s rocket company, Space X, has come a long way since Ressi’s failed intervention. On 12 June, Space X debuted on the [stock market](## "mercato azionario, borsa") as the world’s largest [initial public offering](## "offerta pubblica iniziale, lancio in borsa") (IPO), a $2 trillion [juggernaut](## "colosso") that has launched hundreds of rockets into space; operates a dominant satellite internet service, Starlink; and [oversees](## "supervisionare") Musk’s artificial intelligence efforts and social network, X. The company’s IPO was so huge that Musk became the world’s first trillionaire.
Yet Space X’s success was never a sure thing. Musk and his team of executives and engineers [overcame](## "superare") numerous [hurdles](## "ostacoli"), solving problems as varied as the physics of rockets and the connection of multiple satellites in orbit.
Musk [acknowledged](## "riconoscere") how [far-fetched](## "inverosimili") Space X’s [achievements](## "risultati") were. At the company’s Starbase headquarters in Texas for the IPO, the fifty-four-year-old said he had given Space X less than a 10 per cent chance of succeeding.
Concept rendering of Space X’s Red Dragon spacecraft, which was intended to land on Mars before the project was cancelled in 2017.
“I told people this: ‘It’s probably going to fail. But we should give it a try,’” he said.
Musk’s space dreams were initially modest, and centered on sending some [seeds](## "semi") to Mars in a small [greenhouse](## "serra") through a [venture](## "impresa, attività imprenditoriale") he had started with Ressi, Life to Mars. He thought that if people on Earth saw green [sprouts](## "germogli") [pushing up](## "spuntare") on Mars, they would be excited enough to push Congress to give more money to NASA.
So Musk travelled to Russia, hoping to find an old intercontinental ballistic missile that could cheaply launch the [greenhouse](## "serra") into space. The Russians [spurned](## "rifiutare, respingere") him. On the flight back to the United States, Musk turned to Jim Cantrell, a space industry veteran, and a few other colleagues and said he would build one himself.
In Space X’s early years, it alternated between [eye-catching](## "vistose") failures and [resounding](## "clamorosi") successes. The company’s first rocket, the Falcon 1, was small. Its first two launches, from the Marshall Islands in the Pacific Ocean, failed. Space X was starting to [run out](## "esaurire, finire") of money.

At that point, Musk said he would try one more time to make the rocket work, [recalled](## "ricordare") Robert Zubrin, an aerospace engineer and a [former](## "ex") friend of Musk’s.
The third try also failed. But Musk then said he would try again. If it failed a fourth time, he might have given up entirely, Zubrin said.
“That would have been the end of Space X,” he said.
In September 2008, the fourth Falcon 1 launch made it to orbit. So did a fifth nearly a year later.
By then, Space X had won a key contract from NASA to develop the much larger Falcon 9 rocket and the accompanying Dragon capsule to take cargo to the International Space Station. The first Falcon 9 launch, in 2010, reached orbit, as did the second six months later. In 2014, Space X won another major NASA contract to take astronauts, not just cargo, to the space station.
By [charging](## "far pagare") less to launch satellites, Space X grew to dominate the rocket business. Space X also used its ability to launch quickly and cheaply to [deploy](## "impiegare") Starlink internet satellites starting in 2019. The satellites communicate with terminals on Earth to [beam](## "irradiare, emettere") high-speed internet to nearly every corner of the planet. That idea went back a couple of decades, but Starlink, now Space X’s biggest business, was the first to not end in [bankruptcy](## "bancarotta").
Musk, who simultaneously [runs](## "amministrare, dirigere") electric carmaker Tesla, also turned his attention to getting people to Mars with a spaceship large enough to carry colonists. (Sending [seeds](## "semi") was no longer the main goal.)
In 2016, Musk revealed the initial plans for Space X’s biggest rocket, now known as Starship, at a meeting of the International Astronautical Congress in Guadalajara, Mexico. Earlier that year, he said at a different conference that the company would launch a mission to Mars by 2025.
Since then, Space X’s test flights have included failures and hopeful progress, although almost never as quickly as Musk promises. In March 2024, he said Starship would arrive on Mars “within five years.” A year later, he said the spaceship would depart for Mars “at the end of next year.” NASA has said the best-case scenario would be humans reaching Mars in the mid-2030s.
Even if there are future failures, Musk has [stoked](## "alimentare, stimolare") a generation of space enthusiasts, especially with Space X’s stratospheric IPO.
“What Space X is doing, really, is building the infrastructure that can support and sustain this brilliant new promise of a new space economy,” said Royden D’Souza, an independent journalist and podcast host in Doha, Qatar, who has focused on the cosmos for nearly three decades. “When you build a highway to space, when you build a highway to a new place, you have the potential for whole new economies to come up.”

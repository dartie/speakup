St. Kilda: the Last Islanders [C1]

Questo remoto arcipelago scozzese, la cui bellezza e isolamento hanno da sempre affascinato viaggiatori e studiosi, custodisce una storia complessa che ha portato alla completa disabitazione delle sue isole.

The majestic and isolated archipelago of St. Kilda, whose cliffs famously tower over Atlantic waters some forty miles off Scotland’s western edge, has captivated imaginations for over two hundred years.  Cut off from the mainland by treacherous seas and relentless storms, St. Kilda became a sanctuary for Britain’s largest seabird colony. For four thousand years, St. Kildans lived a life of remarkable self-sufficiency by exploiting the dense colonies of gannets, fulmars and puffins for food, feathers, and oil.

THE IMPACT OF TOURISM

From the 18th century, tourists started to flock to the isles, fascinated by St. Kildans’ ability to survive in such a harsh landscape. Yet, as outsiders brought goods, they also carried disease — tetanus and other illnesses started to erode the fragile community. Locals were cheated out of their possessions, and when introduced to the conveniences of tinned food, started to lose their survival skills. Other guests compelled them to attend church daily, which meant they had less time to take care of their crops and seabird harvest. An archive image from 1927 that shows the men of St. Kilda meeting to discuss their plans for the day at The Village Parliament.

FINAL EXODUS

When World War One broke out, a detachment of British Soldiers was set up on St. Kilda, and the population rose — locals were employed and kept very busy. In the aftermath of the war, when all of the work disappeared, St. Kildans were left destitute and were compelled to find jobs on the mainland. In 1930, the thirty-six remaining islanders, lacking the support of their community, had no other choice but to abandon their ancestral home, leaving behind a hauntingly beautiful landscape. Places

A PEOPLE’S HISTORY

To find out more about St. Kilda’s history, Speak Up spoke to Roger Hutchinson, author of St. Kilda: A People’s History. We started by asking him to explain what makes St. Kilda so unique.  Roger Hutchinson (Scottish accent): The significance of the St. Kilda Islands in Scottish history is chiefly connected to their distance from anywhere else. There’s such a very long way away. The archipelago of St. Kilda, the three main islands, are eighty kilometres from the western coast of the Western Isles, which are themselves another eighty kilometres away from the Scottish mainland. So St. Kilda is an incredibly long way away from anywhere. And the fact that it supported for millennia an indigenous group of Hebridean people, who spoke the language of the Hebrides Gaelic, Scottish Gaelic, that is not Irish Gaelic, and who were clearly part of Hebridean civilization, Hebridean culture — the fact that they survived on these islands, self-sufficient, was a source of fascination to people on the mainland almost as soon as we got to hear about them. Our fascination with the St. Kildans began a few hundred years ago when intellectuals and people in the cities realized that just off the coast of the British Isles there was a kind of Hawaii, a sort of South Sea Islanders group of undiscovered people leading an enclosed and integral life among themselves with hardly any connection whatsoever with the outside world. And as there were never more than a hundred people living there; it was remarkable that they managed to get by without support from anywhere else. They were a sort of tabula rasa as well. They were a blank slate upon which people could project and write their own fantasies of the evolution of Western civilizations.

TOURIST TRADE

Once word of this remote utopia spread, curiosity grew steadily. What began as a handful of adventurous travellers — those willing to make the journey by small boat — gradually drew larger numbers. By the mid-19th century, St Kilda had turned into a tourist attraction. Roger Hutchinson:People started going there to investigate. First of all, individual travellers would go. You could get a boat from the Western Isles, like you can today. It takes a few hours across the open Atlantic Ocean, but it’s doable. And so a few people did go out there to see them. And then in the 19th century, as more boats became available, people started going out until in the end, by the middle of the 19th century, there were actually hired cruise vessels going out from mainland ports in Britain out to St. Kilda. People from Lancashire and the northeast of England would anchor in the bay, go ashore and look at the locals who would sell them bits and bobs of rubbish, really, tourist trinkets and things like these. 

MYTH OF COMMUNITY

The fascination for St. Kilda reached new levels when people started to mythologise the community, projecting onto its simple communal way of life the elaborate structures of their own imaginations. Roger Hutchinson: People invented for them a king and a queen, which they didn’t have, and a parliament, which in any realistic sense they didn’t have, although the men would get together to discuss what work they would do that day, and everything basically other than an army. They were an ideal fantasy group of happy savages. On the one hand, they were sort of uncorrupted and able to lead perfect, blissful, innocent lives in the Garden of Eden. And on the other, they were sort of easily corrupted by visiting diseases or bad habits such as alcohol, et cetera, et cetera. In fact, they were just Hebridean islanders who were a bit further away from the rest of everybody else than most of the other Hebrides. Today, there are still cruise ships which dock in Village Bay in St. Kilda. Not every day, but in the summer, in any given week, you’d expect one or two cruise ships to go in there and see the place.

MODERNITY

Part of our modern fascination with St. Kilda is tied to the tragic ending to life on the isles, brought about by outsiders and modernity. Centuries of self-sufficiency were eroded by disease and outside influence, and a fragile reprieve during World War One made its aftermath even harder. St. Kildans were left stripped of work and hope. On 29 August 1930, the thirty-six remaining islanders decided to evacuate their ancestral home.  Roger Hutchinson: They had absolutely no choice. You see, there was a kind of Indian summer in St. Kilda around the time of the First World War, when infant mortality was reduced massively. It had have been a serious problem. They died from typhus infections, chiefly infants. Most children died before they were a week old. They didn’t get christened until they were a month old. They were expected to die. So infant mortality was a huge problem. Inoculations and the arrival of nurses from the mainland took care of that. They were still a long way away from the early 20th century mainland until the First World War came along when the first military base was established in St Kilda. It was actually a sort of spotting range. A detachment of British soldiers was put up there in a quickly built barracks. They employed locals. Women were employed in the mess to cook for them and men were employed as spotters from the hilltops to look out into the Atlantic at the western approaches to see enemy ships coming, to monitor the waters and things like these. And of course, they got on fine. By that time, they tended to have a bit of English, the St. Kildans. And so communication with soldiers from the mainland of Scotland and the UK wasn’t so much of a problem. The younger St. Kildans tended to speak some English by early in the 20th century. They had a school and a school teacher, you see. And so the population actually rose. It went up between 1911 and 1921, during the First World War. People had no reason to go away. They were making money. They were provisions being shipped in by the army. They could hitch a lift on boats, military boats, going back to the mainland quite regularly. So throughout the First World War, life in St. Kilda was really quite busy and interesting for them. And then the war ended, of course, in 1918, and all these things disappeared overnight. They no longer had the jobs, they no longer had the money, they no longer had the provisions being imported. There were no longer a garrison of young men among them. So everything fell away very quickly. St. Kilda cemetery.

YOUTH EXODUS

St. Kilda started emptying. The island’s younger generation, drawn by the promise of steady employment and urban opportunities, migrated en masse to industrial centres like the Clyde shipyards and Glasgow’s tenements. Roger Hutchinson: Lots of the younger St. Kildans left in the 1920s, went to the mainland, mainly got jobs in industrial central Scotland and elsewhere. They left en masse in the 1920s. Until by the late 1920s, there were just a few kids and old ladies and young mothers and a couple of elderly men left there. Life may have been sustainable on St. Kilda, but it was only sustainable with a good deal of effort, mainly communal effort, to cut peats for fuel. And the peat banks were quite a long way away from Village Bay where they lived, three miles away over a really steep hill. They couldn’t carry the peats back, they couldn’t heat their houses or cook without peats. Looking after the livestock was difficult, and of course nobody was able to go after the birds.

ENVIRONMENTAL INTEREST

In 1957, the military set up a radio station in order to monitor a missile testing range. That same year, St. Kilda became part of the National Trust for Scotland, a conservation charity dedicated to protecting Scotland’s most significant historical, cultural, and natural sites. The Trust’s role includes preventing environmental degradation and promoting education about the island’s extraordinary history. This transition marked a turning point for St. Kilda, shifting its legacy from abandonment to protected heritage — becoming a UNESCO site in 1986. We asked Roger Hutchinson if it could ever be inhabited again. Roger Hutchinson:  You couldn’t live there under current circumstances. The military base that’s there has helicopters coming in and out, as well as boat contact. There’s quite a few people on the rocket testing range there, but you couldn’t really live there. I mean, there’s no shops. You need provisions delivering. There’s no ferry to the mainland, which is a requisite, really. There’s no way of getting off. There’s no hospital. It doesn’t have any of the 20th-century facilities that people everywhere, including in the Hebrides, now expect. The houses that they left behind are still there. In fact, they’re looked after by the National Trust now. It’s a sort of national reserve. It’s a UNESCO World Heritage Site now, both for its natural environment and for its human history.

STONES, BIRDS, AND EXTREME HARDSHIP

There are no trees in St. Kilda but a great amount of stone. St. Kildans became expert stonemasons and built their villages, their houses and gargantuan walls to set up their livestock. They had peat banks for fuel, to use for their fires. St. Kildans cultivated crops and farmed sheep, cattle and ponies — but as no trees or shrubs grew on the island, crops were vulnerable to extreme weather. Wood was scarce, so making fishing boats was near-impossible. Birds were therefore of great importance for St. Kildans. Hebrideans were always good at scaling cliffs for birds, and in St. Kilda, this was adapted into an art form, walking up and down vertical slopes. They would risk their lives to hunt birds by scaling the precipitous cliffs, capturing them not by shooting them, but using nooses on the end of sticks, and searching for eggs. The birds’ meat was dried and stored and the eggs were eaten. Their oil provided fuel for lamps, and they started selling their feathers in the 19th century to use for beds and mattresses, until the arrival of artificial upholstery took over the market. St. Kildans had to be very economical, and so every part of the bird was used: the beaks became brooch-pins, the bones were fashioned into needles, the skins of gannets were turned into shoes. Trade with the outside world was rare, hindered by the harsh Atlantic winters and violent storms that often isolated the islanders for months. In times of extreme hardship — such as the threat of starvation — locals relied on a unique method of communication known as the St. Kilda Mail: they would place their messages in floating containers, attached to buoyant sheep bladders, and release them into the sea, leaving their pleas to the mercy of the waves.

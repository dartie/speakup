The Bayeux Tapestry: Wonder of the Medieval World [B2]

Il British Museum ospita l’Arazzo di Bayeux, il capolavoro medievale che raffigura la conquista normanna dell’Inghilterra del 1066. Sarà esposto fino a luglio 2027.

The Bayeux Tapestry, one of the most famous and recognisable historical documents in the world, is now on show for millions to visit in the British Museum in London while its usual home in the town of Bayeux in Normandy is closed for renovations. Not seen in Britain since it was created in the 11th century, the document commemorates the most famous date in English history, 1066, the last time England was successfully invaded, and the moment from which the British monarchy dates its origins. Seventy metres long and fifty centimetres high, the Tapestry is actually misnamed, as it is really a wool-on-linen embroidery. The document represents, in a visual, comic book-style, the Norman invasion of England in 1066, which culminated in William, the Duke of Normandy, defeating the last crowned Anglo-Saxon king of England, Harold, at the Battle of Hastings. After the Norman conquest, William became king and was known as William the Conqueror. He founded the current British monarchy. Culture

DEPICTING THE BATTLE

The Tapestry depicts both the famous battle and the events leading up to it. The first scene shows the English noble Harold Godwinson with the English king, Edward, whom he hopes to succeed. Later, while travelling in France, Harold is taken hostage but then rescued by William. He swears loyalty to William, supporting his claim to the English crown. When Harold breaks his oath and takes the English throne for himself, a furious William invades England. Harold dies in battle, with an arrow in his eye, graphically reproduced in the Tapestry.

A BLOODY DOCUMENT

Crowding the document are 623 humans, more than seven hundred animals, thirty-seven buildings, forty-one vessels, and ninety-three or ninety-four male genitalia of horse and men — depending on who is counting. “History’s most devastating document of war” is filled with bloodstained images of wounded horses and chain-mailed riders surrounded by corpses and severed heads.

A TROUBLED HISTORY

The Bayeux Tapestry has come close to disaster several times. In the French Revolution, there were plans to cut up the artefact to make wagon covers. A few years later, in the winter of 1803-1804, Napoleon was worried about a possible English invasion during the Napoleonic Wars, so he decided to transfer the Tapestry to Paris. Then, during the German occupation of France in the Second World War, the Nazis moved it to the Louvre as the Allied troops advanced after D-Day. The Nazis were obsessed with the artefact, considering it proof of early Aryan dominance, as the Normans were descended from the Vikings. They took fragments of the cloth to study in Germany. The French Resistance prevented the Nazis from taking it to Germany. The 19th-century replica of the Bayeux Tapestry which is on permanent display in the Reading Museum.

BRITISH COPY

In 1885, thirty-five women from the Victorian Leek Embroidery Society made a full-scale replica of the tapestry. They copied figures from censored photos made by male staff at the South Kensington Museum, who had covered all the penises. The copy was sent on international tours until World War Two, and it can now be seen in the Reading Museum in Berkshire.

A PLETHORA OF PENISES

The Bayeux Tapestry contains ninety-three depictions of penises, which have been variously interpreted as examples of intentional political commentary, symbols of power or expressions of medieval humour. The eighty-eight equine penises are symbols of status and virility. In those times, a powerful, well-endowed stallion was the ultimate symbol of success, vitality and physical dominance. The size of the horse’s member corresponded to the political status of the rider. The five human penises, all erect, are considered to be a subversive comment on betrayal and/or added for comic relief. The Victorians were shocked by the nudity. In the full-scale English replica, made in 1885, they either added clothes or omitted the penises entirely. The equine members were shrunk.

UNESCO STATUS

The Bayeux Tapestry holds a protected status on the UNESCO Memory of the World Register as a documentary treasure of global significance. It gives historians a unique window into the Middle Ages, offering unmatched data on 11th century shipbuilding, agriculture, social hierarchies, and culinary habits. It also has the first known depiction of Halley’s Comet. The origins of the Tapestry, however, are a mystery. Many historians think it was commissioned by William’s half-brother, Bishop Odo of Bayeux, in the 1070s, to decorate the city’s cathedral. It was probably sewn by Englishwomen in Canterbury. And, to add another mystery, the last three metres are missing!

MILLIONS OF VISITORS

The British Museum expects over seven million visitors for what will be the most important exhibition in the UK since the legendary Tutankhamen blockbuster of 1972. Considered to be one of the wonders of the medieval world, it is a miracle that this fragile work of art, containing around ten thousand holes, has survived for almost a thousand years after the famous events it depicts.   Until 11 July 2027 www.britishmuseum.org

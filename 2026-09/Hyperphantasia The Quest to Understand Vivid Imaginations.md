William Blake’s imagination is thought to have burned with such intensity that, when creating his great artworks, he needed little reference to the physical world. While drawing historical or mythical figures, for instance, he would wait until the “spirit” appeared in his mind’s eye. The visions were apparently so detailed that Blake could [sketch](## "fare uno schizzo") as if a real person were sitting before him.
Like human models, these imaginary figures could sometimes act temperamentally. According to Blake biographer John Higgs, the artist could become frust[rate](## "valutare")d when the object of his [inner gaze](## "sguardo interiore") casually changed posture or left the scene entirely. “I can’t go on, it is gone! I must wait till it returns,” Blake would [declaim](## "esclamare, declamare").
Such intense and detailed imaginations are thought to reflect a condition known as hyperphantasia, and it may not be nearly as rare as we once thought, with as many as one in thirty people reporting incredibly vivid mind’s eyes.
Just consider the experiences of Mats Holm, a Norwegian hyperphantasic living in Stockholm. “I can essentially zoom out and see the entire city around me, and I can fly around inside that map of it,” Holm tells me. “I have a second space in my mind where I can create any location.”
This once-neglected form of neurodiversity is now a topic of scientific study, which could lead to [insights](## "comprensione profonda") into everything from creative inspiration to mental illnesses such as post-traumatic stress disorder and psychosis.
Francis Galton — better known as a racist and the father of eugenics — was the first scientist to recognise the enormous variation in people’s visual imagery. In 1880, he asked participants to [rate](## "valutare") the “illumination, definition and colouring of your breakfast table as you sat down to it this morning.” Some people reported being completely unable to produce an image in the mind’s eye, while others — including his cousin Charles Darwin — could picture it extraordinarily clearly.

“Some objects quite defined. A [slice](## "fetta") of cold beef, some grapes and a pear, the state of my plate when I had finished and a few other objects are as distinct as if I had photos before me,” Darwin wrote to Galton.
Unfortunately, Galton’s [findings](## "risultati") failed to [fire the imagination](## "stimolare l’immaginazione") of scientists at the time. “The psychology of visual imagery was a very big topic, but the existence of people at the extremes somehow disappeared from view,” says Professor Adam Zeman at Exeter University. It would take more than a century for psychologists such as Zeman to [take up](## "riprendere") where Galton left off.
Even then, much of the initial research focused on the poorer end of the spectrum — people with aphantasia, who claim to [lack](## "essere privo di") a mind’s eye. Within the past five years, however, interest in hyperphantasia has started to grow, and it is now a [thriving](## "fiorente") area of research.
To identify where people [lie](## "trovarsi") on the spectrum, researchers often use the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire (VVIQ), which asks participants to visualise a series of sixteen scenarios, such as “the sun rising above the horizon into a [hazy](## "fosco, nebbioso") sky” and then report on the level of detail that they see in a five-point scale. You can try it for yourself. When you picture that sunrise, which of the following statements best describes your experience?
The final score is the sum of all sixteen responses, with a maximum of 80 points. In large surveys, most people score around 55 to 60. Around 1 per cent score just 16; they are considered to have extreme aphantasia; 3 per cent, meanwhile, achieve a perfect score of 80, which is extreme hyperphantasia.
The VVIQ is a relatively [blunt](## "poco preciso") tool, but Reshanne Reeder, a lecturer at Liverpool University, has now conducted a series of in-depth interviews with hyperphantasic people — research that helps to delineate the peculiarities of their inner lives. “As you talk to them, you start to realise that this is a very different experience from most people’s experience,” she says. “It’s extremely immersive, and their imagery affects them very emotionally.”
Some people with hyperphantasia are able to [merge](## "fondere") their mental imagery with their view of the world around them. Reeder asked participants to [hold out](## "porgere") a hand and then imagine an apple sitting in their palm. Most people feel that the scene in front of their eyes is distinct from that inside their heads. “But a lot of people with hyperphantasia — about 75 per cent — can actually see an apple in the hand in front of them. And they can even feel its weight.”
As you might expect, these visual abilities can influence career choices. “Aphantasia does seem to [bias](## "influenzare") people to work in sciences, maths or [IT](## "informatica (Information Technologies)") whereas hyperphantasia [nudges](## "indirizzare") people to work in what are traditionally called creative professions,” says Zeman. “Though there are many exceptions.”
Reeder [recalls](## "ricordare") one participant who uses her hyperphantasia to [fuel](## "alimentare") her writing. “She said she doesn’t even have to think about the stories that she’s writing, because she can see the characters right in front of her, acting out their parts,” Reeder [recalls](## "ricordare").
Hyperphantasia can also affect people’s consumption of art. Novels, for example, become a cinematic experience. “For me, the story is like a film in my mind,” says Geraldine van Heemstra, an artist based in London. Holm offers the same description. “When I listen to an audiobook, I’m [running](## "riprodurre") a movie in my head.”
Zeman’s research suggests that people with hyperphantasia enjoy especially rich autobiographical memories. This certainly [rings true](## "sembrare convincente") for Van Heemstra. When thinking of trips in the countryside, she can recall every step of her walks, including [seemingly](## "apparentemente") inconsequential details. “I can picture even little things, like if I [dropped](## "far cadere") something and picked it up,” she says.
Exactly where these abilities come from is unknown. Aphantasia is known to run in fami[lie](## "trovarsi")s, so it’s reasonable to expect that hyperphantasia may be the same. Like many other psychological [traits](## "caratteristiche"), our imaginative abilities probably come from a combination of [nature and nurture](## "natura e cultura"), which will together shape the brain’s development from infancy to old age.
Zeman has taken the first steps to investigate the neurological differences that [underpin](## "sostenere") the [striking](## "notevole") variation in the mind’s eye. Using [fMRI](## "risonanza magnetica funzionale (Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging)") to scan the brains of people at rest, he has found that hyperphantasic people have greater connectivity between the prefrontal cortex, which is involved in higher-order thinking such as planning and decision-making, and the areas responsible for visual processing, which [lie](## "trovarsi") towards the back of the [skull](## "cranio").
“My guess is that if you say ‘apple’ to somebody with hyperphantasia, the linguistic representation of ‘apple’ in the brain immediately transmits the information to the visual system,” says Zeman. “For someone with aphantasia, the word and concept of ‘apple’ ope[rate](## "valutare") independently of the visual system, because those connections are [weaker](## "più deboli").”
Many mysteries remain. A large survey by Professor Ilona Kovács, at Eötvös Loránd University in Hungary, suggests that hyperphantasia is far more common among children, and [fades](## "attenuarsi") across adolescence and into adulthood. She suspects that this may reflect differences in how the brain encodes information. In infancy, our brains store more sensory details, which are slowly [replaced](## "sostituire") by more abstract ideas. “The child’s memories offer a more concrete appreciation of the world,” she says — and it seems that only a small percentage of people can maintain this into later life.
Reeder, meanwhile, is interested in studying the consequences of hyperphantasia for mental health. It is easy to imagine how vivid memories of [upsetting](## "perturbanti") events could [worsen](## "peggiorare") the symptoms of anxiety or post-traumatic stress disorder, for example.
Reeder is also investigating the ways that people’s mental imagery may influence the symptoms of illnesses such as schizophrenia. She suspects that, if someone is already at risk of psychosis, then hyperphantasia may lead them to experience vivid hallucinations, while aphantasia may increase the risk of non-sensory [delusions](## "deliri"), such as fears of persecution.
For the moment, this remains an intriguing hypothesis, but Reeder has shown that people with more vivid imagery in daily life are also more susceptible to seeing [harmless](## "innocue") pseudo-hallucinations in the laboratory. She asked participants to sit in a darkened room while watching a [flickering](## "tremolante") light on a screen — a [set-up](## "configurazione, allestimento") that gently stimulates the brain’s visual system. After a few minutes, many people will start to see simple illusions, such as geometric shapes. People with higher VVIQ scores, however, tended to see far more complex scenes — such as a stormy beach, a medieval castle or a volcano.
Reeder emphasises that the participants in her study were perfectly able to recognise that these pseudo-hallucinations were [figments](## "invenzioni") of their imagination. “If someone never has reality discrimination issues, then I don’t think they’re going to be more [prone](## "predisposti") to psychosis.” For those with mental illness, however, a better understanding of the mind’s eye could offer [insights](## "comprensione profonda") into the patient’s experiences.

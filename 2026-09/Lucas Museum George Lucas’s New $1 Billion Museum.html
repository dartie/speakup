Lucas Museum: George Lucas’s New $1 Billion Museum [A2]

Il creatore di “Star Wars” è anche un appassionato collezionista d’arte. A settembre 2026, la sua straordinaria collezione sarà aperta al pubblico nel nuovo museo che inaugurerà a Los Angeles.

George Lucas has given the world some of the greatest film franchises of all time: Star Wars and Indiana Jones. Now the iconic filmmaker is giving the world a $1 billion museum, the Lucas Museum of Narrative Art. Announced in 2017, the museum in Los Angeles, California, is opening on 22 September, with a promise to make art more accessible to everyone.

40,000 WORKS OF ART SAVINGS

Founded by George Lucas and his wife, Mellody Hobson, the museum was originally conceived as a space to present Lucas’s incredible art collection to the world. Lucas has been collecting art since he was a college student in the 1960s. He now has over forty thousand works of art and film memorabilia. They include paintings, illustrations, photography, sculpture, comic art, and of course storyboards, props, and costumes from Star Wars and Indiana Jones. George Lucas and Mellody Hobson with two Norman Rockwell works, The Problem We All Live With (1963) and Shuffleton’s Barbershop (1950).

OVER $1 BILLION

Lucas and his wife planned to open the museum first in San Francisco and then Chicago. But because of legal and logistic difficulties, they abandoned these plans and decided to open it in Los Angeles instead. The museum took eight years to construct and is estimated to have cost over $1 billion. Places

Molly Malcolm The site of the museum occupies 4,5 hectares of Exposition Park, an urban park and cultural destination that also has other popular museums and attractions. Its five-storey, 27,900-square-metre building resembles a giant space cruiser. It has thirty-five galleries, two theatres, a library, a retail store, a restaurant, a café, and event spaces.

A TEMPLE TO THE PEOPLE’S ART

The museum houses Lucas’s entire collection, organised in galleries that are curated around thematic experiences such as family, love, work and adventure. According to the museum’s official website, the museum “celebrates the power of visual storytelling” and “explores how images move us and shape our collective memory. From Frida Kahlo’s self-portrait to Norman Rockwell’s scenes of everyday life to Charles White’s portraits of dignity. Each invites reflection. Each tells a story.”

THE COLLECTION

The museum’s 40,000-piece collection treats all visual storytelling equally, bringing together so-called high art and popular culture. It rests on three pillars: narrative art history (traditional painting, sculpture, and illustration); the art of cinema (storyboards, costume designs, and visual effects archives); and digital/graphic art (comics, manga, and animation). Among some of its more prominent works are: Mexican Stand-Off (2006) by Rafael Navarro; a polychrome relief depicting the afterlife of an elite official (around 2200BC-2000BC) by an unknown artist; The Critic’s Corner (2007) by Ernie Barnes; George Washington Carver Crossing the Delaware: Page from an American History Textbook (1975) by Robert Colescott; Migrant Mother, Nipomo, California (1936) by Dorothea Lange; American Historical Epic, Second Chapter: Axes (Clearing the Land) (between 1924 and 1927) by Thomas Hart Benton; The Seasons (1897) by Alphonse Mucha; Las Meninas, after Velázquez (1897) by John Singer Sargent; Podrace Crash (1995-99) by Doug Chiang. Speaking at Comic-Con International in San Diego last year, Lucas said the museum is “sort of a temple to the people’s art… Society cannot exist without a common belief system. And that’s where illustration is vital, to show you what that means in everyday life.”  www.lucasmuseum.org

Glasgow’s Most Iconic Statue: The Cone-Headed Duke [B2]

Gli scozzesi hanno quest’abitudine di mettere dei coni stradali in testa alle statue. Lo fanno per sdrammatizzare e per ricordare che non vale la pena prendersi troppo sul serio!

Glasgow, Scotland’s largest city, is well known for its citizens’ irreverent, sarcastic, sharp, and sometimes surreal sense of humour. The story you are about to read is a perfect example of this attitude.

THE DUKE OF WELLINGTON

If you’ve ever wandered around Glasgow’s city centre, past the Gallery of Modern Art at Royal Exchange Square, you may have noticed a curious statue: a man on horseback wearing a traffic cone on his head. It is a monument that never fails to catch the eye. So much so that in 2011, Lonely Planet included it in its list of the Top 10 Most Bizarre Monuments on Earth. The man depicted is Arthur Wellesley (1769-1852), 1st Duke of Wellington, a British Army commander who also served twice as Prime Minister of the United Kingdom. He is shown riding Copenhagen, his favourite horse. The bronze statue commemorates the defeat of Napoleon in the Battle of Waterloo (1815), in which Wellesley played a key role. It was erected in 1844, almost a hundred years before traffic cones were even invented.  The painting The Battle of Waterloo by Jan Willem Pieneman from 1824. Wellington, commander of the Anglo-Dutch troops, is the central figure.

DON’T MESS WITH THE CONE

The traffic cone was first placed on the Duke’s head as a joke in the early 1980s. Since then, every time the city council removes it, some prank‑loving citizen puts it back.  Who came up with the idea remained a mystery for decades. Legend had it that it was a drunk student, until December 2025, when art director Jason Orr claimed that he had placed a cone on the statue in 1979 at the age of ten. Julian Earwaker, Justin Ratcliffe According to the Glasgow City Council, the cost of removing the cone amounted to around £10,000 annually. In 2013, the council proposed raising the statue on a pedestal to deter cone placements, but the plan was abandoned due to strong public opposition. In fact, a campaign called “Keep the Cone” gained tens of thousands of supporters in less than 24 hours. The City Council and the police had to surrender to the will (and stubbornness) of the citizens of Glasgow, and the Duke has never again been deprived of his orange hat. 

THE SCOTTISH BANKSY

Sometimes, locals and artists change the cone’s colour to mark special events or causes: it became gold to celebrate Olympic success, and different colours have been used for political and social statements. A few years ago, graffiti artist Banksy reportedly described the statue as his “favourite work of art in the UK.” In 2025, the artist The Rebel Bear, also known as the Scottish Banksy, placed a small sculpture of a pigeon reading a newspaper — and wearing a cone, of course — on the Duke’s head. In an Instagram post, the artist simply stated: “The dignified and undignified of beasts.”

HUME, TOO

The habit of putting cones on statues’ heads has spread to other Scottish cities. In Edinburgh, for example, it is not unusual to see the statue of the philosopher David Hume on the Royal Mile also wearing a traffic cone. As if the thousands of tourists rubbing his toe for luck weren’t enough already!

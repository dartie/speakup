Lent: What Are You Giving Up? [B1]

Nei quaranta giorni che precedono la Pasqua, l’usanza è quella di rinunciare a qualcosa: nel 2026, è l’occasione giusta per disintossicarsi dal cellulare.

During the forty days of Lent (Sundays are excluded,) many people across the UK will be giving something up: alcohol, cigarettes, carbs… Abstinence during Lent was originally a Catholic tradition, and England, Wales, and Scotland haven’t officially been Catholic countries since the 1500s. In recent years, however, there seems to be a renewed interest in doing a springtime detox.

A NEW START

The word ‘Lent’ comes from the Old German word for springtime and has the same etymological roots as the word ‘lengthen’. It is the time of year when the days are getting longer. In the Christian calendar, the day before Lent starts is called Shrove Tuesday. It was the day when Catholics would confess their sins before Lent began. Rich foods were forbidden during Lent, so households would use up eggs, butter and milk by making pancakes on this day. It’s similar to the way that in Catholic countries Mardi Gras and carnival are an opportunity for a final indulgence before the restrictions of Lent. In recent times, across the UK, Ireland, Australia, Canada, and New Zealand, many people eat pancakes on Shrove Tuesday, which is now celebrated as the secular festival of Pancake Day. SELF-DISCIPLINE

Historians generally agree that observing Lent became a shared Christian practice in 325 CE. Early on, Lent centred on fasting, often in the form of the Black Fast. This extreme fasting allowed only one small meal after sundown and excluded meat, dairy, alcohol, and sexual relations. The aim was to practice self-discipline and reflect on how Jesus spent forty days and nights in the desert before the crucifixion. But during the Protestant Reformation, which began in Germany in 1517 and spread across Northern Europe, many of the Catholic Church’s practices, including fasting for Lent, were criticised or even banned. Though some Protestants continued to observe Lent, it was in a more moderate form.

A MODERN LENT

Today, Lent often survives less as a religious obligation than as a secular personal challenge. People might give up sweets, meat, alcohol, or carbohydrates. Others try digital fasting, sometimes called a ‘digital detox’, giving up or at least reducing social media, screen time and phone use. Wellness culture has embraced Lent as an opportunity for detox or reset, with apps and clinics promoting it as a time to lose weight or build healthier habits. Lent, whether religious or secular, is still a season when less is more.

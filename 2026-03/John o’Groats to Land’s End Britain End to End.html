One fascinating way to experience the many different landscapes of the UK is to travel the whole length of the country from John o’Groats at the north-eastern tip of Scotland to Land’s End at the south-western tip of England. You can do the route by car, bike, or on foot. Some extreme athletes even run the whole way, averaging 80km a day for a little over two weeks. 

DRAMATIC DIFFERENCE

John o’Groats is a small village on the north coast of Scotland, facing towards Norway across the North Sea. The weather is cold and windy, perfect for the seals and puffins that live around its rocky coast. Land’s End, meanwhile, is a point at the end of the south-west tip of England, on Cornwall’s stunning Lizard Peninsula. The weather here is much milder, there are lots of wildflowers, and the dramatic cliffs look over the English Channel and the Celtic Sea. Although neither John o’Groats nor Land’s End is the absolute extreme point of Britain, both are fixed as such in tradition. John o’Groats to Land’s End

DISTANCE TRAVELLED

The distance between the two points is approximately 960km as the crow flies. But by road it’s longer, around 1,400km, while to walk it off-road can be even further, some 1,900km, depending on the route chosen. The first recorded continuous walk was completed in 1871 by two brothers from Cheshire, who finished the route in just fifty-five days. Travellers choose their own paths by road, trail or cycleway. Some start in John o’Groats and head south to Land’s End (a journey known as JOGLE) while others go in the other direction (LEJOG). Going south to north is perhaps slightly easier, as the wind is usually behind you and the sun isn’t in your eyes.

LANDSCAPES

The route crosses an extraordinary range of landscapes. From John o’Groats, travellers journey through the misty Scottish Highlands. Once in Northern England, routes pass through the Lake District or the wet and windy Pennine Hills. Further south, in the Midlands, walkers cross gentler farmland areas and the Peak District National Park, known for its strange rock formations. From there, the route takes hikers through the Cotswolds, with its rolling hills, meadows and cottages. The final stretch moves through Devon’s moorlands and fossil cliffs before reaching the rocky coves of Cornwall. Those who complete the JOGLE or LEJOG on foot really have experienced the UK, step by step.  Places

THE DUTCHMAN 

The Scottish village of John o’ Groats is named after a Dutchman, Jan de Groot. In 1496, King James IV of Scotland gave de Groot permission to start a ferry service between the northernmost point of the Scottish mainland and the Orkney Islands. De Groot built himself an eight-sided house near the harbour on the mainland and lived there with his seven sons. The village that grew up around the house took Jan de Groot’s name — but pronounced with a Scottish accent.

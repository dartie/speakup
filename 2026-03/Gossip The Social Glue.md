Sometimes, [over time](## "col passare del tempo"), a word loses its original positive meaning and gradually takes on a negative one. How does this happen? A good example is the word ‘gossip’, which today often has a negative connotation.
‘Gossip’ comes from ‘godsibb’, an Old English term made up of ‘god’ and ‘sibb’ meaning ‘relationship’ or ‘affinity’. It referred to a relative connected, not by blood, but spiritually through God. In the Middle Ages, godsibs were the [godfathers and godmothers](## "padrini e madrine") present at a baptism. Their role was to support the biological family and they represented close friendship, [trust](## "fiducia") and care. At the time, these relationships were strong [bonds](## "legami"), socially significant and in some cases even [legally binding](## "legalmente vincolante").

## WOMEN’S SPEECH
In the late Middle Ages and during the Renaissance, the term began to refer to [trust](## "fiducia")ed female members of the community who assisted women during childbirth. They were close friends of the family and spent [a great deal](## "una grande quantità") of time with them, talking freely about everything and sharing news, stories, and secrets. Between the 14th and 15th centuries, the word ‘godsibb’ was shortened to ‘gossip’, and its meaning gradually [shifted](## "cambiare") from referring to a person to describing a type of behaviour, that is, instead of a [trust](## "fiducia")ed intimate person, it referred to informal conversation [engaged in](## "partecipare a") by a [talkative](## "loquace, chiacchierone") companion. 

## USELESS TALK
Between the 16th and 17th centuries, ‘gossip’ began to acquire a [derogatory](## "peggiorativo, dispregiativo") meaning and became associated with women’s domestic talk and engagement in conversations considered [pointless](## "futile") or trivial. Women’s words were increasingly devalued, especially in contrast to male-dominated written and institutional discourse. The opposition between oral and written communication became [sharper](## "più marcato") and reflected the [widening gap](## "divario crescente") between private and public spheres.

## GENDERED
For centuries, gossip was closely connected to gender and was often [dismissed](## "sminuire"). Its shift from a word about [trust](## "fiducia") and care to one about socially [reprehensible behaviour](## "comportamento riprovevole") isn’t just a linguistic change, but also reflects a broader social and cultural transformation. Women and the lower social classes were associated with ‘inferior’ oral, informal and non-institutional language. Such attitudes stigmatised and delegitimised certain groups, reflecting imbalances of power.

## SOCIAL GLUE 
Today, linguistics and sociology often re-evaluate gossip as social [glue](## "collante") through which relational [bonds](## "legami") are formed, moral values are shared and group identity reinforced. This function is often compared to social [grooming](## "toelettatura, pulizia sociale (etologia") in primates.

## GOSSIP, RUMOUR OR HEARSAY?
Gossip usually focuses on people, their behaviours and relationships. The word ‘rumour’, instead, refers primarily to events or facts whose truth is uncertain. ‘[Hearsay](## "voci di corridoio, testimonianza per sentito dire")’ is a more formal term, mainly used in legal contexts. It describes information learned through others, without direct evidence, and is often not accepted as evidence in a [court of law](## "tribunale").

## GOSSIP IN LITERATURE
Gossip plays an important role in literature, where it often becomes a narrative engine. In William Shakespeare, the word ‘gossip’ still means ‘godmother’ or ‘close female friend’, but it already suggests the idea of indiscreet talk. This shows a moment of transition in meaning. Shakespeare uses gossip in a [strikingly](## "sorprendentemente") modern way: the plot in Othello or [Much Ado About Nothing](## ""Molto rumore per nulla""), for example, develops around the circulation of gossip that anticipates what we would now call disinformation or an early form of fake news, with the [aim](## "obiettivo") of insinuating, suggesting and manipulating.
Jane Austen is also a master in the use of gossip to [move the plot](## "far avanzare la trama"). In novels such as Pride and Prejudice or Emma, the gossip circulating in small, closed social circles [shapes](## "modellare") reputations and strongly influences marriages or social exclusion. In Oscar Wilde, on the other hand, moral judgment is reversed: gossip is presented as entertainment and social performance, exposing the hypocrisy of Victorian society. Silence means invisibility, and visibility means power. This theme is [embodied](## "incarnare, personificare") in his novel The Picture of Dorian Gray, where the character Lord Henry Wotton [asserts](## "affermare, sostenere"): “The only thing worse than being talked about is not being talked about.”

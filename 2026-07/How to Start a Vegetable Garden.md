Maybe it’s because I’ve lived in cities my whole life, but I can’t think of anything more luxurious than [popping out](## "uscire un attimo") to your garden and eating a fresh tomato [straight from the vine](## "direttamente dalla pianta"). How [decadent](## "lussuoso") to enjoy its [crisp](## "croccante"), bright flavour and the [smug](## "compiaciuta") satisfaction that you [coaxed this food into being](## "creare con pazienza") with your own hands.
But what does becoming a modern-day [Demeter](## "Demetra (dea greca dell’agricoltura)") actually [entail](## "implicare")? What if you don’t have a [yard](## "giardino"), or even a balcony? And is it worth [growing](## "coltivare") your own food when supermarkets exist?
“A vegetable garden can be any size,” says Matt Mattus, senior director of horticulture at the American Horticultural Society. You don’t even need access to outside space, he says.
Yes, it’s more effort than popping to the store, but there are huge benefits to [growing](## "coltivare") your own veg, experts say. First, the taste and freshness. “There are no words that can describe the crunch and flavour of a fresh-from-the-garden carrot or potato,” says Mattus.
There are also health benefits, says Carole Connare, editor of the Old Farmer’s Almanac — not just because we’re eating fresh and local greens. “When we stick our hands in [dirt](## "terra"), it promotes wellbeing and calmness,” she says. Indeed, research has shown there are bacteria in [soil](## "suolo, terreno") that can increase serotonin, lower stress and strengthen our immune systems.
So, if you want to grow a vegetable garden, how do you start? What do you need?

## FIRST: GOOD SOIL
Consider the area available to you, because that will determine the kind of equipment you need.
As Mattus noted, even the smallest space can accommodate some form of vegetable garden. “Greens and [herbs](## "erbe aromatiche") grow beautifully indoors under LED lights,” he says. With just a [windowsill](## "davanzale") and some sort of [tray](## "vassoio, vaschetta") — a plastic or glass food storage container, for example — you can easily grow microgreens, which add flavour and nutrients to sandwiches or salads.
In small outdoor spaces, like a balcony, [pots](## "vasi") or [window boxes](## "fioriere") can form a garden. “Many vegetables grow very well in containers,” Mattus says. Tomatoes and peppers, for example, [thrive](## "prosperare, crescere") in large [pots](## "vasi") as long as they get at least five hours of sun.

When you have access to more outdoor space, like a [yard](## "giardino") or a community garden [plot](## "appezzamento, parcella"), you need to figure out the quality of the [soil](## "suolo, terreno") you’re working with.
“It’s a simple test,” says Connare: if you [dig up](## "dissotterrare") a bit of [soil](## "suolo, terreno") with a [trowel](## "paletta"), how does it look? If it’s dark and [crumbly](## "friabile") like a cake, then “that’s really good [soil](## "suolo, terreno")”, she says. But if the [soil](## "suolo, terreno") is light colored and feels like sand, [growing](## "coltivare") anything in it will likely be difficult.
Bad [soil](## "suolo, terreno") isn’t a big problem. The solution is to work with [raised beds](## "aiuole rialzate"), filled with high quality [soil](## "suolo, terreno") from a garden store.
“Many people start with a raised bed,” says Mattus. Usually, this measures about 4 feet by 8 feet [1.22m by 2.44m], and 12 inches to 14 inches [30cm to 36cm] deep. He suggests filling it with a combination that is half [loam](## "terriccio") (a mix of sand, [silt](## "limo") and [clay](## "argilla"), considered the ideal garden [soil](## "suolo, terreno")) and half composted leaves, mixed in with composted or aged [manure](## "letame").
Investing in good [soil](## "suolo, terreno") will likely be one of your bigger expenses. Besides that, Mattus says, a new vegetable gardener only really needs a [shovel](## "pala"), a [trowel](## "paletta"), and possibly some [stakes](## "pali") or [cages](## "gabbie"), depending on the [crop](## "coltura"). “A vegetable garden can be relatively inexpensive,” he says.

## GROW WHAT YOU LIKE
What to plant? Start by choosing a few vegetables you enjoy. “If you’re going to start a vegetable garden, you want to like the results,” says Connare.
Planting varieties of vegetables that you can’t find at your local market, or plants that taste better fresh than store-bought can be rewarding, says Mattus. The flavour of fresh, homegrown tomatoes and potatoes is “[unmatched](## "ineguagliabile")”, he says.
Some [crop](## "coltura")s are easier than others, Connare says: most greens, such as lettuce, kale and [arugula](## "rucola"), tend to grow quickly and easily; [starches](## "alimenti amidacei") such as [squash](## "zucca") are generally prolific and disease-resistant; and [radishes](## "ravanelli") are “a wonderful [crop](## "coltura") for not a lot of work.”
More difficult [crop](## "coltura")s, according to gardening [non-profit](## "organizzazione senza scopo di lucro") the Square Foot Gardening Foundation, include [celery](## "sedano"), melon, cauliflower, [eggplant](## "melanzana") and broccoli.
Plants in boxes and balconies can be positioned to make the most of the sunlight.
Check the [seed](## "seme") packet to find out if a certain plant will work in your garden. It will likely be printed with information about when to plant the [seed](## "seme")s, [how far apart](## "a che distanza") to place them, and whether they need direct or indirect sunlight.
Don’t forget to ask other enthusiasts for advice. “When you go to your local plant store, people there tend to be very [knowledgeable](## "competenti, informate") about what works in your area,” says Connare.

## COMMON MISTAKES
A very common mistake, says Connare, is that “people start too big.” Be realistic about how much time, energy and money you’re willing to commit to your garden. “Whenever I’m driving around, I look in people’s back[yard](## "giardino")s and I see [raised beds](## "aiuole rialzate") and even a little [greenhouse](## "serra"), and after a year or two, they don’t get used.”
There’s also no need to spend lots of money on [fancy](## "sofisticato, costoso") equipment [right away](## "subito"). “You’ll certainly find reasons to spend money later if you [get the bug](## "appassionarsi, prenderci gusto"),” she says.
People often buy plants that are too mature, like tomatoes that are already [in bloom](## "in fiore"), or [oversized](## "troppo grande") [parsley](## "prezzemolo"), says Mattus. “Larger plants exposed to cold temperatures can [bolt](## "andare a seme") prematurely once warm weather arrives,” he says, meaning the plant has been stressed, [goes to [seed](## "seme")](## "andare a seme") early, and produces vegetables that are bitter and [unpalatable](## "sgradevole").
Connare also suggests gardeners [stagger](## "scaglionare") their planting when possible. “It’s okay to not get it all in the ground on April 15,” she says.
Many flowers and vegetables perform better when planted later, agrees Mattus. “Gardening is not just a spring activity,” he says. “It’s a season-long process.”

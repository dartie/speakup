All Bets Are On: Prediction Markets [B2]

Se in passato le scommesse si concentravano soprattutto sugli eventi sportivi, oggi si può scommettere su qualsiasi cosa nei mercati di previsione, un’industria che vale miliardi di dollari.

Will Spain win the World Cup? Is there going to be a recession this year? Who will win the 2028 US presidential election? These are just some of the things you can bet on prediction markets. Popular since the early 2020s, prediction markets are online platforms where people can bet on real-world events; that is, on almost anything in politics, economics, sports, and popular culture. They have exploded in popularity since 2024, when many people used them to bet on who would win the US presidential election, and now register billions of dollars in transactions a week.

YES OR NO SHARES

So how do prediction markets function? People bet on the outcome of future events by buying shares. Most markets focus on ‘yes or no’ questions, for example, “Will 2026 be the hottest year on record?” Shares cost between \(0 and \)1. If a ‘yes’ share costs 65 cents, it means the market believes there is a 65 per cent probability that this will be the hottest year on record.  Kalshi is the first regulated prediction market in the US. US TV networks CNN and CNBC have partnered with Kalshi to integrate its probability data into their news stories. Participants buy and sell ‘yes’ or ‘no’ shares, based on what they believe, and prices fluctuate based on supply and demand. If 2026 is the hottest year on record, those who bet ‘yes’ will win $1 per share; those who bet ‘no’ will lose their investment.

PROBLEMS

However, prediction markets are not always accurate because of inaccurate or incomplete information, market manipulation, biases… And they are not always fair, or perhaps even legal. Although federally legal in the US, many states consider them a form of unregulated gambling and are trying to ban them. They are also susceptible to illegal insider trading, where people with access to confidential information use it for financial gain. This year, Kalshi, one of the most popular prediction markets, allowed users to bet on whether Iran’s Supreme Leader, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, would be removed from power by a predetermined date. After Khamenei was killed in February 2026, it refused to pay out approximately $54 million in winnings to those who had bet ‘yes’, because, it said, Khamenei’s death didn’t qualify as being removed from power and because it prohibits betting directly on death.

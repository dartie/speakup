Ian Mortimer: “The Time Traveller’s Guide to Medieval England” [C1]

Ci sono molti modi di guardare e raccontare il passato. Lo storico Ian Mortimer riesce a rendere il Medioevo sorprendentemente vivo, facendo sentire il lettore parte di quell’epoca.

Dr. Ian Mortimer is the author of thirteen history books, including four medieval biographies and four fictional novels. A fellow of the Royal Historical Society and the Society of Antiquaries, Dr. Mortimer’s books have sold more than 1.3 million copies worldwide. Innovative in his work, the historian defines his approach as reflecting his interest “in what it means to be alive in any time… Once you concentrate on a period, it lives in your mind.” His aim, he says, is to immerse his readers in history, as if they were travelling “on a sensory ride through times past.” 

TIME TRAVELLING GUIDES

Central to Dr. Mortimer’s work are four Time Traveller’s Guides: to Medieval England, Elizabethan England, Restoration Britain and Regency Britain. Highly readable, they work both as history books and tourist guides. Dr. Mortimer’s most popular book to date is The Time Traveller’s Guide to Medieval England: A Handbook for Visitors to the Fourteenth Century. To find out more, Speak Up contacted Dr. Mortimer. We began by asking him about his approach, which combines the rigour of academic history with the excitement of historical fiction.  Ian Mortimer (English accent): My emphasis… I would really stress to people, there are many, many ways of doing history. Yes, we have our traditional political histories, with kings, wars, et cetera. We have our traditional, now traditional social histories, looking at the experience of the common people, and I think that’s equally valid. But both of these are really geared around academic study. Whichever one you’re talking about, they are about taking a body of evidence, comparing it with what we know of history up to now and then seeing how this changes our picture of the past. This is a very static way of looking at the past; it is very sophisticated, it is very interesting, but it’s like reducing everything to one dimension. We are moving in time, the past was moving in time. We are now able to understand them because we too are human. And because of these things, there are many different ways of looking at the past. PERSPECTIVES OF THE PAST

Dr. Mortimer’s books are witty and wild. Rather than concentrate on the opulent grandeur of royalty and court intrigues, he submerges his historical stories in normal people’s everyday lives, their routines and chores, little joys and miseries. His visceral approach in the Time Traveller’s Guides has earned the praise of the media: from the BBC’s History Magazine reviewer, who writes “To read the book is to be transported to 14th-century England in the company of a knowledgeable and witty guide.”, to The Guardian, which compares Dr. Mortimer to Geoffrey Chaucer: “After The Canterbury Tales, this has to be the most entertaining book ever written about the Middle Ages.” Ian Mortimer: We can play around with all sorts of different perspectives on the past, and our traditional ones, that almost God’s eye view of looking down on mankind and its wars and its kings and its social upheavals… that was nobody’s perspective. No one ever was able to look down like that. But if you look at the past from the idea of being there, as in The Time Traveller’s Guide to Medieval England, you can actually begin to appreciate what it must have been like for people on the streets. You can start to appreciate what the wars of the time meant for people living here when you think about history as it impacted on people living in this house. There are so many different ways of doing history, and I want to make people feel alive to how human the past is, not objective and distant, but as meaningful as being alive today.

THE MORTIMER METHOD 

While seeking to entertain, Dr. Mortimer wears his PhD with pride. He sticks to the facts, he says, but informs his reader of what we actually want to know. Ian Mortimer: What is different about my approach is, it puts the question [of]what you want to know first, whereas an academic will look at the document and see what it says regardless of what you want to know. And that whole process means I ask different questions with the same documents. So, for example, building records. If an academic is looking at accounts for building a cathedral, he may well want to know how much people were paid, how the cathedral afforded this great big building. I will look at it and think: “What were they using for scaffolding? What materials were they getting from where, and how much was somebody able to earn in a day, so that could he afford to feed a family if he was a normal worker?” So it’s the different questions. And this is quite an important point because we’ve got loads and loads of documents, and we tend to use them in very traditional ways. We can look at all our documents again, and get lots more out of them because, if we ask different questions, they become almost like different documents. They tell us new things. People

IN THE ARCHIVES

Dr. Mortimer uses a variety of historical documents to write his immersive histories. His foucs, he says, is on asking the question that typical historian don’t ask. We asked him to give us an example. Ian Mortimer:  Different ways of reading literature, different ways of reading accounts, different ways even of reading things like royal writs — you know, an order from a king to do something or other. Well, if you’re imagining being there at the time, it loses its traditional, political significance and it becomes something about travel. How fast could you travel? For me, I’m interested in how fast people could get around. What the actual writ said doesn’t matter to me. What I’m interested in is the fact that the king gave the order on, say, the sixth of June, and it was received seventy miles away the next day. So that’s the sort of thing that interests me from the point of view of being there in the past, imagining being there.

SHAKESPEARE’S HALF TRUTHS

During this process, Dr. Mortimer has stumbled on details that have confused him. For example, at the end of the 16th century mortality rates soared in England. This, Dr. Mortimer discovered, was due to a hike in food prices, which meant that even mild diseases and infections could spread fatally among the malnourished population. The Elizabethan Poor Laws of 1598 and 1601 introduced a radical change. From then on, when food became too expensive, local parishes were forced to give food or money away. We asked Dr. Mortimer how history could have hidden such a surprising fact. Ian Mortimer:When I was writing the 17th century book, so this is concentrating on the years 1660 to 1700, I was cataloguing, as I always do, and working out the most likely causes of death. And about 6 per cent of the population, 6 per cent of everybody, died from problems to do with their teeth. That really surprised me! I mean, I just was amazed by that. What other things? The hierarchies, the absolute misery of people who are at the bottom of society in Elizabethan England in the 1590s. The 1590s, when Shakespeare is writing in London, people are starving to death around the country. I mean, you don’t get that from Shakespeare, but there’s this real disparity between the eloquence and the subtlety of Shakespeare’s work and, on the other hand, people simply starving to death as if they didn’t matter. And the 1590s solved that problem, and England became one of the first countries in Europe to be able to avoid famine as a result. That was a real surprise. TRULY EMPATHETIC

And, Dr. Mortimer says, we should not be judgemental about people of the past, as they found themselves in extremely complicated circumstances. Ian Mortimer:Traditionally, in history, we contrast objectivity, and we approve of objective decisions about people and objective judgments, and we contrast that with subjectivity. And we regard subjective decisions, as you know, meaningless in historical terms. But this is wrong, because objectivity and subjectivity when you’re looking at the past aren’t opposites. There’s a whole scale of how objective you can be, and it doesn’t come down to subjectiveness, it comes down to empathy. So if you think about this sliding scale of very distant objectivity judging a king for his decisions, down to understanding why that king made the decisions he did, empathising with him, you see the [that] scale exists between the very objective and the truly empathetic. And it doesn’t mean you have to be subjective. It doesn’t mean you have to introduce your own biases. And I want people to realise that we can take any position along that scale of objectivity that you want. We can look at people distantly if you want to, or we can look at them close up and examine them for why they made the decisions they made. What did they feel? How would we feel if we were in their situations? In the Middle Ages especially, people endured so much. How would we get by today? That’s a question in my mind all the time.

MYSTERIOUS MIDDLE AGES

Dr. Mortimer’s guides reveal the importance of adding complexity to history. They also reveal how the past is connected to the present, as he explains.  Ian Mortimer: People don’t really understand the Middle Ages very well. It seems very distant, very strange, and they normally come at it through jokey things like Monty Python and ruined castles… But, whether we joke about how much violence there was and, we really don’t understand the Middle Ages as a people. So I’m trying to show them how sophisticated the Middle Ages was, how full of hope, how although it’s full of struggle, it was also full of improvements, and working towards a better world. The beginning of the 17th century is all full of witchcraft and superstition. By the end of it, we’ve had Isaac Newton, we have the beginning of science, we quantify things. The beginning of the modern world is really with that quantification and that massive change.

TECHNOLOGY

And, Dr. Mortimer concludes, the past places technology in context: while the word is new, the idea of changing the world through a gadget, artefact or concept can be found in medieval society.  Ian Mortimer:Our assumptions about change being based around technology constrast with the reality, which is most social change has nothing to do with technology whatsoever. Technology makes things bigger and faster, true, but what we are really develops over the Middle Ages. I don’t see the Middle Ages being dark at all. I see them as being underfunded, most of all. Struggling, all the time. Struggling, but capable of the most brilliant achievements.

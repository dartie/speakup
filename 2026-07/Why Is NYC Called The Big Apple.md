# Why Is NYC Called The Big Apple?   [B1]

New York City is known for many things: [pigeons](## "piccioni"), bagels, [crowds](## "folle"), the subway... the list [goes on and on](## "continuare all’infinito"). Not included in this list, however, is ‘apples’. While New York State has innumerable [orchards](## "meleti"), the city itself has never been particularly known for this fruit, in large part because it’s very difficult to plant apple trees in the middle of Times Square; and yet The Big Apple is by far its most famous [nickname](## "soprannome"). Why?

## ALL THE RAGE
To understand the etymology of this unusual [nickname](## "soprannome"), we have to go back a couple of hundred years. In 19th-century America, apples were a crucial food source, and [all the rage](## "di moda"). Big, bright, shiny apples were therefore an object of some importance, and the term ‘big apple’ came to mean anything that was equally big, bright, and shiny.
New York City is known for many things, including [pigeons](## "piccioni"), but certainly not for its apples.

## STORIES OF CITIES
In 1909, American journalist and editor Edward S. Martin wrote an introduction to The [Wayfarer](## "viaggiatore") in New York, a collection of stories about the city. Smaller cities, he wrote, were inclined “to think that the ‘big apple’ gets a disproportionate share of the national [sap](## "linfa"),” and as such were not too happy with the city getting so much attention.

## BIG AMBITION
In the 1920s, a sports journalist named John J. Fitz Gerald wrote a piece for the New York Morning Telegraph in which he described the hopes and dreams of horse racers and jockeys around the US. One of the highest ambitions they could aspire to — “the dream of every [lad](## "ragazzo, giovane") that ever threw a leg over a [thoroughbred](## "purosangue") and the goal of all horsemen” — was winning the “Big Apple”: the big, bright, shiny prize that came with winning horse races in New York City.
With Fitz Gerald’s article, the term really [caught on](## "prendere piede"), and The New York Times used it for the first time on 11 March 1928. Soon, jazz musicians started singing about “The Big Apple.”

## CHANGING TIMES
Fast forward a few decades, and New York’s reputation in the 1970s [plummeted](## "precipitare, crollare"). Rising [crime rates](## "tassi di criminalità") and [bankruptcy](## "bancarotta") had [lessened](## "ridurre") the [shine](## "splendore") on the Big Apple, and fewer and fewer people wanted to come and see it. Enter Charles Gillett, businessman and president of the New York Convention and Visitors [Bureau](## "ufficio"). Remembering the popularity of the phrase, he decided that the way to [boost](## "incentivare") tourism in the city was to [go all out](## "puntare tutto su") on Big Apple imagery. [Pins](## "spille") and [stickers](## "adesivi") were made, the term was used everywhere, and as tourism gradually increased, the Big Apple regained its [lustre](## "splendore").

## HOW DO YOU SAY...?
È la luce dei miei occhi.
She’s the apple of my eye.
Nowadays, the term “Big Apple” is losing popularity, and you’ll rarely hear it used seriously anymore, especially not by New Yorkers, who find the term a bit touristy. After all, it was always meant for visitors.

‘Like’: The Most Hated Word? [C1]

C’è una parola che proprio non sopporti? Per molti anglofoni, quella parola è ‘like’: quattro lettere ripetute continuamente (forse troppo), che finiscono per assumere i significati più svariati.

‘Like’ is, like many words, more than just a word. In fact, it’s something of a superword: a linguistic Swiss Army knife that can function as a noun, verb, adjective, adverb, preposition, conjunction, and more.

LONG HISTORY

The word ‘like’ has been part of English for more than a thousand years. Over time, it has developed a wide range of meanings and uses. In its role as a discourse marker or filler, it has been labelled by some as the most hated word in the English language. Many linguists link this negative perception to social and gender prejudices, particularly those directed at a cultural stereotype known as the Valley Girl. Valley Girls are often defined by a distinctive way of speaking: the use of uptalk and of certain discourse markers such as ‘like’ or ‘whatever’, and expressions such as ‘totally’, ‘Oh, my God’, and ‘seriously?’

VALLEY GIRLS

The concept of the Valley Girl emerged in the United States in the late 1970s and early 1980s. It refers to a particular type of young woman, generally white and upper-middle-class. Valley Girls are typically associated with Southern California, especially the San Fernando Valley and Los Angeles. Valley Girls are often defined not only by their fashion and lifestyle, but also by a distinctive way of speaking: the use of uptalk (a rising intonation at the end of sentences), and of certain discourse markers such as ‘like’ or ‘whatever’, and expressions such as ‘totally’, ‘Oh, my God’, and ‘seriously?’

CLUELESS

The Valley Girl stereotype has been criticised as both sexist and classist. Popularised in films such as Heathers (1989), Clueless”) (1995), and Mean Girls (2004), it has often been portrayed as superficial. These women are almost exclusively preoccupied with boys, beauty and image, celebrities and social status. Over time, however, the speech features associated with this stereotype, including the linguistic features, have spread widely and are no longer confined to young women or to California. 

EXPRESSIONS

“It’s, like, freezing in here.” “I was like, ‘What do you mean you’ve met someone else?’” These are just two examples of how people use the word ‘like’ in everyday speech either as a discourse marker or as a replacement for ‘said’. It is precisely these informal uses that are explored in Like: A History of the World’s Most Hated (and Misunderstood) Word, a book by Megan C. Reynolds. In her book, Reynolds traces the evolution of ‘like’ in its modern, informal functions, showing how speakers, herself included, use it to soften statements, manage tone, and maintain conversational flow despite the strong reactions it can provoke. To find out why a word so common has such a bad reputation, Speak Up contacted Reynolds, a contributor to major US publications including The New York Times and Elle. She began by explaining what motivated her to write the book. Megan C. Reynolds (American accent): I like the kind of books in, like, the Mary Roach vein, sort of a deep dive into one very specific subject. And the thing about ‘like’ is that I think I’ve always had sort of a passing interest in etymology. The one question I always ask and the one question I always need to know the answer to, is ‘why?’ I think it’s, frankly, the most important question for anything. And I have noticed that I do say ‘like’ a lot. And I’ve never found a problem with it. No one has ever found a problem with it, but I do know that lots of people have an issue with it.

IN PERSPECTIVE

But does Reynolds really think it is the most hated word in the English language, or is the title a bit of an exaggeration? Megan C. Reynolds: The title is a little dramatic, if I may. So am I. So that tracks. There are obviously other words that are way worse, that are much more hated, that have books written about them as well. But I do think the sort of vitriol that’s attached to the word is attached to specifically the messengers, like, the people who are saying it the most. And the people who are saying it the most are women. And so that seems like something to call out. That seems important to me, at least. And misunderstood, I think it is misunderstood because of (a) because of how useful it is; and (b) because being really mad about something that young women are saying should cause you to examine what your motivations are. Like, why are you actually upset about this? Language

GENDER DIFFERENCES

In her book, Reynolds points to a possible sociolinguistic explanation as to why women use ‘like’ as a filler or ‘discourse softener’ in colloquial speech so much more than men. Megan C. Reynolds: There isn’t like a very specific reason, but one of the more general reasons that I’ve found is that women’s speech is naturally more accommodating. It’s generally coded as less direct. You’re making sure that the other person in the conversation is being taken care of. I think that’s why women use it more, and it’s because it can serve as kind of a hedge, and kind of a softener. So if you want to make a very direct statement, feel slightly, like… just not toothless entirely, but, like, maybe missing one to two teeth, you can throw a ‘like’ in there and it doesn’t sound amiss.

NUANCE

The use of ‘like’ can be surprisingly nuanced. It doesn’t just tell us something about the speaker, but also about how they feel about the situation they are describing, and even about their relationship with the person they’re talking to. Megan C. Reynolds: One of my favourite uses, and one of the uses that I think gets a lot of flack, is the quotative. I guess the better example is like, “My boss was, like, ‘You’re terrible at your job!’” One assumes that your boss did not say that outright. What I would infer from that statement is that whatever happened in that conversation made you feel bad about your job or whatever else, but likely that was not exactly what was said. And what that word is doing there is, when you’re saying ‘like’ instead of ‘said’, what you’re actually doing is giving your conversational partner a sort of a glimpse into, like, how you felt about the thing that was happening. It makes conversations much more interesting. It makes telling stories much more interesting. And it also fosters connection even just, like, in a teeny tiny way. One of my other personal favorite ways is using ‘like’ as an approximator for a quantity or as an intensifier. That’s me saying, “There were, like, one million things for me to do today.” Certainly not. I probably have, like, three things to do today, like, frankly. Using it as an intensifier is a way of showing a little bit about how you feel about the situation.

SELF-CENSORSHIP?

Reynolds herself uses ‘like’ a great deal — like, a lot! — but she says she hasn’t experienced the kind of criticism or pressure that many women face over their speech. Megan C. Reynolds: I am very lucky in that I was raised primarily by my father and he did not in any way police my language unless, I mean, I called him a ‘douchebag’ once and he was, like, “Really?” And I said, “Okay, you’re right. Sorry, I didn’t mean it.” But aside from that, he’s never policed my language or the way that I speak. You know, I hear stories from friends and even just from my two sisters who were raised by my stepfather that they were told, like, “Don’t say ‘like’ so much, it makes you sound stupid.” “Of all the things you could be upset about the way someone is speaking, if someone’s saying ‘like’ a lot grates on you, if that’s, like, the only thing that you hear when someone is talking, I’m sorry, that’s a you problem.”

READING THE ROOM

At the same time, she is fully aware of her own linguistic habits and believes that most people are capable of adjusting their tone to suit different situations. Not everyone, however, feels the need to do so. Megan C. Reynolds: The notion of professionalism has shifted, I think, a lot in the workplace. If you are someone who tends to use ‘like’ and a lot of other filler words, you will naturally not do it because you know you’re in a professional environment. You can read the room. That’s a lot of it; being able to sort of accurately take the temperature of the audience that you’re speaking to and how to say what you need to say so that it is received appropriately, which is why politicians will tend to change the way they may be addressing their constituents based on who’s in the room, what they’re trying to convey. Do they want to seem down to earth? Do they want to seem, like, normal? That also backfires. Donald Trump does not speak like a president, at least a president that I’ve known in my lifetime. You know, I think his supporters probably think that’s refreshing, and… it is not.

EVOLVING LANGUAGE

Reynolds believes that language should remain a living, evolving tool. She also has a clear message for those who criticise the use of words such as ‘like’ as a way of belittling certain groups. Megan C. Reynolds: There are nine million other things, truly, literally, to worry about. But grammatical purists, they usually seem very concerned about what’s right. But the thing to know, I guess, about what’s right is that it’s what’s right to them, and because they are older, that has changed. You know, a purist would point to a dictionary and say ‘like’ is only to be used in this way, that or the other. And even though the slang usage of ‘like’ is in the dictionary at this point, the thing about a dictionary is that it is essentially outdated by the time it is published, because language is moving much faster than the dictionary people have time to be able to assemble. So a dictionary is useful as, I guess, as a record and sort of a general guidebook. Like, words do have meaning, I’m not saying that they don’t. The prevalence of ‘like’ in the English language is not a sign that we’re heading to hell in a handbasket. There’s a bunch of other signs that point to that. So this is not it. This is not the fight. We should all be just a little more flexible in our thinking about what makes good language or what language should be. Of all the things you could be upset about the way someone is speaking, if someone’s saying ‘like’ a lot grates on you, if that’s, like, the only thing that you hear when someone is talking, I’m sorry, that’s a you problem.

WAY WORSE

In fact, there’s — literally — a word that Reynolds believes has an even worse reputation than ‘like’. Megan C. Reynolds: I think that a word that people get more upset about, I don’t think there’s a book about this yet, though, if someone wants me to write it, I will, is ‘literally’. I have no issue with it. I think at this point, I think it’s generally accepted as an intensifier. ‘Literally’ has just a much more literal and specific meaning that I think when words like that are used out of place, that knocks something in people’s brains a little bit more. But, yeah, there are words that arguably have more power in the bad way that we could be concerned about trying to eradicate from our speech or whatever else.

IDENTITY

As a final point, Reynolds argues that our way of speaking is a core part of our identity, and should be respected as such. Megan C. Reynolds: Everyone has a personality, and one of the great ways to convey your personality is how you speak. Speaking is the way we sort of form tribes based on… like affinity tribes, you know, like the teenagers all talk the same and act the same because they’re teenagers and it’s kind of freaky. So everybody’s on their own journey, as I like to say.

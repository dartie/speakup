“Gone With the Wind” by Margaret Mitchell [B2]

Audace e delicata, desiderata e rifiutata, dirompente e al tempo stesso legata alla famiglia e alla casa: Rossella O’Hara è un personaggio tanto controverso e complesso quanto il classico di cui è protagonista.

A classic of Southern American literature set primarily in Georgia during the 1860s and 1870s, Gone with the Wind follows the life of its protagonist, Scarlett O’Hara, a young woman born into a family of Southern slave owners who lives through the fall of the Confederate States of America. Her personal story unfolds against the backdrop of the Civil War and the Reconstruction era, as she fights relentlessly for the survival of her home, Tara, to which she repeatedly returns as a physical and emotional refuge.

SCARLETT’S JOURNEY

Scarlett’s journey is marked by her unrequited love for Ashley Wilkes, an idealistic and tradition-bound planter whose moral gentility she admires but never truly possesses. This obsession never fades, even as it remains unfulfilled. In stark contrast stands her tempestuous romance with Rhett Butler, a cynical, charismatic and socially defiant figure whom she marries only to be abandoned by him in the end. “Life’s under no obligation to give us what we expect. We take what we get and are thankful it’s no worse than it is.” “La vita non è obbligata a darci quello che desideriamo. Dobbiamo prendere quello che ci capita e ringraziare che non sia peggio.” Clark Gable as Rhett Butler and Vivien Leigh as Scarlett O’Hara in the 1939 film version of Gone With the Wind, adapted from the 1936 novel by Margaret Mitchell.

CONTROVERSIAL CLASSIC

The novel depicts a deeply racist, slave-owning society, sparking enduring debates about whether it condones, critiques or nostalgically romanticizes this world. A closer reading reveals layers of complexity, particularly in its protagonist. In a society ruled by the codes of late-19th-century White Southern gentility, with its rigid hierarchies and social fakeries, Scarlett stands out as raw, sharp, passionate and deeply contradictory. “As God is my witness, as God is my witness, the Yankees aren’t going to lick me. I’m going to live through this, and when it’s over, I’m never going to be hungry again. No, nor any of my folks. If I have to steal or kill — as God is my witness, I’m never going to be hungry again.” “Dio mi è testimone che gli yankees non mi abbatteranno. Supererò questo; e quando sarà passato, non soffrirò mai più la fame. Né io né i miei. Dovessi rubare o uccidere… Dio mi è testimone che non soffrirò la fame mai più.”

HEARTBREAK AND CHARM

Both sweet and shrewd, Scarlett simultaneously embodies heartbreak and charm, independence and belonging. This latter trait is most evident in her determined love for her home, Tara, which she singlehandedly restores and sustains in the aftermath of the Civil War, managing to keep it within her family despite all odds. Entrepreneurial and tireless, she fights to fulfil the oath she makes upon returning to the devastated Tara after the war, when she vows “never to be hungry again.” As her mother, and later her father, die when the war ends, Scarlett assumes control of the household, preserving both land and lineage. This fierce determination and personal agency were also manifest in the romantic context as she continues to pursue the illusion of love represented by Ashley Wilkes. “Her eyes met his, hers naked with pleading, his remote as mountain lakes under gray skies. She saw in them defeat of her wild dream, her mad desires.” “I loro occhi s’incontrarono: quelli di lei supplichevoli, quelli di lui remoti come laghetti montani sotto il cielo grigio. E Rossella vide in essi il naufragio dei suoi pazzi sogni e dei suoi desideri.” Classic Books

A STORY OF CONTRASTS 

Scarlett’s complexity makes her a groundbreaking female character, defying the gender norms of her time. This subversive femininity stands in sharp contrast to the figure of Melanie Hamilton, Ashley Wilkes’s wife, who embodies idealised Southern womanhood. Valuing selfsacrifice, moral rectitude, and decorum above all else, Melanie is almost a caricature of bourgeois femininity. Similarly, her husband Ashley represents gentility and honour, embodying the ultimately fragile and hypocritical ideal of the Confederate South. “I’m tired of everlastingly being unnatural and never doing anything I want to do. I’m tired of acting like I don’t eat more than a bird, and walking when I want to run and saying I feel faint after a waltz, when I could dance for two days and never get tired. I’m tired of saying, ‘How wonderful you are!’ to fool men who haven’t got one-half the sense I’ve got, and I’m tired of pretending I don’t know anything, so men can tell me things and feel important while they’re doing it.” “Sono stanca di dover sempre essere innaturale e di non fare mai quello che mi piace. Sono stanca di fingere di mangiare come un uccello e di passeggiare quando ho voglia di correre, di dire che mi gira la testa dopo un valzer, mentre ballerei due giorni di seguito senza stancarmi. Sono stufa di dire “siete straordinario!” a degli imbecilli che non hanno la metà dell’intelligenza che ho io e di far finta di non saper nulla perché gli uomini possano dirmi delle sciocchezze credendo d’insegnarmi chi sa che cosa…”

DARKNESS

Scarlett and her other love interest, Rhett Butler, a wealthy, worldweary libertine who openly rejects Southern social norms, and whom Scarlett only truly recognises as her great love once he emotionally withdraws, form a couple diametrically opposed to the Wilkeses. Far removed from any pretence of saintliness, they emerge as darker, more lucid figures, unafraid to face reality, and their tumultuous selves pour through any façade of conformity. “I wonder if anyone but me realizes what goes on in that head back of your deceptively sweet face.” “Mi chiedo se qualcuno oltre me si renda conto di cosa ci sia dietro quel tuo viso dolcemente ingannevole.”

TOMORROW IS ANOTHER DAY

Scarlett’s relentless hope and determination culminate in the novel’s famous final line. Yet the ending is also the result of how she has repeatedly delayed confronting her feelings, constantly telling herself she will “think of it all tomorrow.” Even in this final moment, and despite all the tragedy Scarlett has endured and the defeat weighing on her shoulders, she once again refuses to surrender to despair. Scarlett envisages tomorrow as a possibility to finally turn her gaze inwards with the same clarity and courage she has always directed outwards. “I’ll think of it all tomorrow, at Tara. I can stand it then. Tomorrow, I’ll think of some way to get him back. After all, tomorrow is another day.”  “Penserò a tutto questo domani, a Tara. Sarò più forte, allora. Domani penserò al modo di riconquistarlo. Dopo tutto, domani è un altro giorno.”

CLASSIC FILM

A bestseller when it was published in 1936, Gone with the Wind became a cultural phenomenon. Its success was further amplified by the 1939 film adaptation, directed by Victor Fleming and starring Vivien Leigh as Scarlett O’Hara and Clark Gable as Rhett Butler. The film won eight Academy Awards, including Best Picture, and remains one of the most influential works in the history of American popular culture.

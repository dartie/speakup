The Americans: 250 Years of a Nation [B2]

Il 4 luglio 2026 gli Stati Uniti celebrano 250 anni. Parliamo della storia del Paese, delle caratteristiche del suo popolo e delle pressioni che stanno mettendo a dura prova l’identità tradizionale.

Defining a national identity is not easy. In the case of the United States of America, the question is also an interesting one because American identity is mainly ideological and cultural rather than ethnic. Being American means accepting fundamental constitutional principles developed over the past 250 years, principles which have shaped the nation’s identity to a significant degree.

FOUNDING CREED

These principles, which became America’s founding creed, originated in three 18th-century documents: the Declaration of Independence (1776), the Constitution (1787) and the Bill of Rights (1791). The Declaration’s famous words, “all men are created equal… with certain unalienable Rights, that among these are Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness”, would both guide the country and help form the character of its people. The iconic opening phrase of the Constitution’s preamble, “We the People”, signified that government power in the new nation originated with its citizens. This concept is central to American democracy. Key figures who have contributed to the promotion of American identity: recording artist Elvis Presley; boxer Muhammad Ali; and pilot Amelia Earhart.

NATION OF IMMIGRANTS

Also fundamental to American identity is the vision of the US as a nation of immigrants, with people from all over the world journeying to the country in search of a better life. America has been built by immigrants. First were the group known as American Indians (or Native Americans), who crossed the Bering Strait to Alaska and migrated down the West Coast thousands of years ago. Two centuries after the 17th-century settlers from Britain, Germany and Holland arrived, waves of immigrants from across Europe followed. In the last sixty years people from Latin America and Asia have also settled in the country. Culture

DIVERSE POPULATION

As a result, the US has a racially and ethnically diverse population, which now numbers around 342 million. An approximate breakdown gives us: White: 59 per cent; Hispanic or Latino: 20 per cent; Black: 13 per cent; Asian: 6 per cent; and Native American: 1.5 per cent. This diversity is an essential part of the American identity. The fusion of cultures, usually described as a ‘melting pot’, has created a very distinct US culture. In recent years some commentators have begun referring to a ‘salad bowl’; a blend of American cultures and identities that remain distinct yet create a diverse national identity.

COMMON BELIEFS

Freedom, equality and democracy are common beliefs in this ‘melting pot’ or ‘salad bowl’. The First Amendment (part of the Bill of Rights) guarantees freedom of speech, religion, press, assembly, and petition. Americans value the idea of a political community in which people have reciprocal responsibilities. They have a strong belief in the idea of America and participating in democratic processes at every level. In these common beliefs, however, lie the worst stain on America’s past. Slavery, often called “America’s original sin”, lasted approximately 246 years and is the foundational moral failing of the country. Americans’ historical murderous treatment of the Indigenous Peoples is also a source of shame for many. THE AMERICAN DREAM

Another concept fundamental to the American identity is an idea famous all over the world: the ‘American Dream’, which originally represented the idealism of the American experiment, as the creation of the United States of America is often called, has over time come to be associated with upward mobility. This is the idea that, through hard work and determination, anyone can succeed, regardless of their background or circumstances.

THE AMERICAN CHARACTER

The American identity inevitably includes aspects of personality. But what exactly is the American character? Generalising in this minefield of potential clichés, we can say that it consists of a number of traits. Individualism — personal freedom and self-reliance — is fundamental, supported by optimism, hard work, and an entrepreneurial spirit. Americans are passionate about their country’s potential and have a strong sense of patriotism. American flags are ubiquitous, adorning homes, gardens, buildings and even clothing. Consumerism is equally important, a key focus of American life. Consumerism reaches its peak every year on Black Friday, with record online spending last year of $11.8 billion.

A LOVE OF SPORTS

Another binding element is a passionate love of sports, especially baseball, basketball and American football (or just ‘football’ for them.) One influential social commentator recently described American football as “the most important single thing” in the US, acting as a unifying idea of the country and what it stands for. Ninety-three of the one hundred most-watched TV programmes in 2023 were football games. The most-watched game had 57.2 million viewers, about 16 per cent of the population!

WORLD POLICE

There is also a geopolitical element to being American: for eighty years, since the end of the Second World War in 1945, America has acted as the dominant global power, leading the rules-based international order and often behaving as the world’s policeman, intervening militarily and politically to (in theory) maintain order and promote democracy.

A RADICAL MOMENT

As they celebrate 250 years as a country, Americans’ idea of themselves is evolving at breakneck speed as the nation experiences its most radical moment since the Civil War. Americans’ core beliefs are under real pressure for the first time in history because of a brutal push for change coming from the Government. Added to this, political polarisation, which began in the 1970s, is now becoming more and more bitter. The country is also very unbalanced economically: the bosses of major companies are now earning up to three hundred times the salary of a typical worker, an enormous jump from the 1960s’ figure of about 21-to-1. America’s famous social mobility is also in trouble. In 2020, the US was only 27th in the Global Social Mobility Index. Belief in the fabled American Dream is also decreasing. In a January 2024 poll, only 27 per cent believed it was still true (down from 50 per cent in 2010), with 69 per cent disagreeing. Finally, immigration, a key part of America’s history, has now become a cause of violent disagreement, with thousands of people being arrested and deported in chains. Being American is now, sadly, a source of both pride and pain.

In Malheur National Forest in Oregon in the US, lives the world’s largest living organism: a parasitic fungus that covers almost four square miles, weighs up to 35,000 tonnes, and is thousands of years old. This organism, known as the Humongous Fungus, exists mostly underground as a vast network of [thread-like](## "filiformi") filaments called mycelia, which spread through [soil](## "suolo, terreno") and tree roots. It acts as a parasite on conifer trees, attacking their roots and absorbing their nutrients, and produces honey-coloured mushrooms at the base of the trees it infects.

## NEITHER ANIMAL NOR PLANT
This is one of the many fascinating and largely unknown facts about fungi, a distinct kingdom of eukaryotic organisms that includes [yeasts](## "lieviti"), [moulds](## "muffe") and mushrooms. Neither animal nor plant, fungi are essential to our ecosystem and play a vital role in human health; hundreds of mushroom species have medicinal properties, and fungi are used in the production of many medicines, including penicillin.

## EDUCATING THE PUBLIC
Mycology is the scientific study of fungi, and Bruch Reed is the [COO](## "direttore operativo ('Chief Operating Officer')") of the North American Mycological Association (NAMA), a [non-profit](## "senza scopo di lucro") organisation of professional and amateur mycologists with over ninety affiliated mycological societies across the US, Canada, and Mexico. The organisation is dedicated to promoting scientific and educational activities related to fungi, and to informing the public about which mushrooms are safe to eat... and which ones could kill you.
Amanita phalloides, a poisonous mushroom better known a [death cap](## "amanita falloide, tignosa verdognola").

## RECOVERING A LOST HERITAGE
NAMA has been organising annual [forays](## "escursioni, spedizioni") since 1960, bringing together hundreds of mushroom enthusiasts, scientists and [foragers](## "raccoglitori") in a different region of North America each year to [survey](## "studiare, censire") fungal biodiversity. The [gatherings](## "incontri") combine fieldwork with [lectures](## "conferenze"), identification workshops and a range of mushroom-inspired activities, from cooking and cultivation to artistic [pursuits](## "attività") such as natural [dyeing](## "tintura") and paper-making using fungal materials. Participants also discuss medicinal uses, foraging practices, and newly-discovered species.
To find out more about the fascinating world of mycology, Speak Up contacted Reed. He began by arguing that knowledge of fungi is part of a cultural [heritage](## "patrimonio") that, in many places, has been [neglected](## "trascurare") or lost [altogether](## "del tutto").

**Bruch Reed (American accent):** Historically, it’s a connection to the [land](## "atterrare") and the natural world that requires consistent generational [passing on](## "trasmissione") of the knowledge. And in the United States and Canada, we broke that in a couple different ways. One, by being a nation of immigrants who came from someplace else. There are some of the same mushrooms in the places that immigrant people come from, and we have other ones that are different. But also, we, as a culture, oppressed native people and [to an extent](## "fino a un certo punto") [stripped](## "privare di") their connection to their history with the [land](## "atterrare"), and that’s a great loss of knowledge. There’s very little record of Indigenous peoples’ use of mushrooms, in particular in North America. We have a number of initiatives trying to connect with [Indigenous folks](## "popoli indigeni") in their communities to rebuild this knowledge.

## GREAT BIODIVERSITY
Fungi remain one of the least understood groups of organisms on the planet. New species are discovered every year, and advances in DNA analysis continue to reshape scientists’ understanding of how fungi are related. Reed argues that the field is so vast that despite decades of study, the word ‘expert’ cannot be applied to anyone. Not even to him.
**Bruch Reed:** Fungi are arguably the least historically studied kingdom of life. They’re difficult to study. They’re challenging to understand. Their diversity and their mode of conducting their lifestyle is [humblingly](## "che rende umili") vast. I don’t know of anyone in mycology who doesn’t [cringe](## "rabbrividire") a bit when we hear the term ‘expert’ or get called ‘expert’, because I might know a lot more about mushrooms than most people, but to be an expert I think would take twenty-two lifetimes. I would never call myself an ‘expert’. I am also very proud of and [adamant](## "irremovibile") to call myself an ‘amateur mycologist’, because an amateur is one who loves what he or she or they pursue, and I love them, and I pursue them and I am constantly learning new things about mushrooms. At every single group event that I participate in, there will be a species that does not have a name. And DNA is [unraveling](## "svelare") those mysteries of identity and relationship, but we’re still discovering new species, even in places that are heavily mycologized. [I’m unlikely to](## "è improbabile che io") be able to go out and find a new snake species or even a plant, but fungi are so [humblingly](## "che rende umili") vast that there is infinite amount of work to be done just to know them, [let alone](## "figuriamoci") understand the myriad ways in which they fit into the environment and control the environment and connect the environment and permit life itself to exist. 

## INTEGRAL TO LIFE
Although mushrooms are the most visible part of the fungal world, they represent only a small fraction of fungi’s in[flu](## "influenza")ence on life on earth. Many plants depend on fungal partnerships to obtain nutrients, while some species cannot even reproduce without them. Reed knows this as he also grows orchids as a hobby and the two passions turn out to be deeply connected.
**Bruch Reed:** I just got back from studying these orchids in the wild in La Chinatla, in Mexico. It’s a secret love. Like, I have four hundred orchids in this apartment that I cultivate. Every time we see a wild orchid — and really most times we’re seeing any plant —, we are also seeing fungi that we don’t actually see. But without fungi, the orchid [seed](## "seme") can’t germinate. The [seed](## "seme")ling cannot synthesise its own food and sugars. Orchid [seed](## "seme")s are like [dust](## "polvere"), they’ve got nothing but DNA in them. There’s two million in every [pod](## "baccello") or so, but those [seed](## "seme")s must [land](## "atterrare") in a precise environment to connect with the fungus or a fungal spore to generate the plant that we [are in awe of](## "restare meravigliati da"). The connectivity of fungi that’s integral to life itself will never cease to amaze me. 
The horn of plenty, also known as the trumpet of the dead (Craterellus cornucopioides), is a highly prized [edible](## "commestibile") mushroom.

## DELICIOUS BUT DEADLY
[And yet](## "eppure"), as is universally known, fungi can also be deadly. Some species produce toxins capable of causing fatal poisoning if consumed. Recently, California has seen an [unprecedented surge](## "aumento senza precedenti") in poisonous mushrooms, specifically of the [death cap](## "amanita falloide, tignosa verdognola") (Amanita phalloides) and western destroying angel (Amanita ocreata) varieties. Since late last year, at least four people have died from eating these mushrooms, while over forty others have been hospitalised and three have required [liver](## "fegato") transplants.
As [COO](## "direttore operativo ('Chief Operating Officer')") of NAMA, Reed helps coordinate mushroom identification efforts for poison-control centres and works extensively to educate the public about both the benefits and risks of fungi. He believes greater fungal [literacy](## "conoscenza") could save lives.
**Bruch Reed:** We have to rebuild what became a [tenuous grip](## "conoscenza limitata") on this knowledge, because [the flip side](## "l’altra faccia della medaglia") is, of course, that mushrooms can be wonderful medicine. There are mushrooms growing [within walking distance](## "a pochi passi") of where I’m sitting, each of which could kill three adults. The [morel](## "morchella, spugnola") poisonings, it’s from people eating them undercooked. A [raw](## "cruda") [morel](## "morchella, spugnola") can kill people. Killed three people in 2023 in this country from [raw](## "cruda") [morel](## "morchella, spugnola") — the Dave’s sushi incident [sickened](## "causare malessere, far ammalare") fifty-some people, two of them died of necrotizing gastritis. Mushrooms are beautiful and wonderful and delicious and healthful, but they have to be taken seriously.

## DECEPTIVELY ORDINARY
[It is worth noting](## "è opportuno sottolineare") that the most dangerous mushrooms can be [deceptively](## "ingannevolmente") ordinary in appearance. The [death cap](## "amanita falloide, tignosa verdognola") and the destroying angel are responsible for the majority of fatal mushroom poisonings worldwide. Their toxins attack the [liver](## "fegato") and other organs, and symptoms often do not appear until it is too late to prevent serious damage, as Reed explains.
**Bruch Reed:** A couple [bites](## "morsi, bocconi") of an Amanita mushroom, cooked or not, you get sick about six to eight hours. You think you have the [flu](## "influenza"), you’re [achy](## "indolenzito"), you might be [throwing up](## "vomitare"), probably diarrhea. A couple days of that, you get better. And then one, two, three days... catastrophic [organ failure](## "insufficienza d’organo") that’s very difficult to treat and results in death too often. Destroying angels, they’re a [whitish](## "biancastro") to [off-white](## "bianco avorio"), greenish tinted with a [sac-like base](## "base a forma di sacco"), white spores, and they taste good. Amanita is the Alice in Wonder[land](## "atterrare") mushroom and the destroying angel. It’s a big [genus](## "genere"), but there’s an Amanita that’s a delicious [edible](## "commestibile") in the group of what we call Caesar amanitas. That’s Amanita vernicoccora. And that’s a wonderful, delicious Caesar mushroom. Caesars are a [renowned](## "famosa") millennia-old delicacy all over the world. But superficially, the mushroom can [resemble](## "assomigliare a") each other.  And one of those destroying angels can kill three adult humans.

# How to Recognise (and Handle) a Psychopath   [C1]

La psicologa canadese Leanne ten Brinke studia da decenni le personalità tossiche. Quali segnali d’allarme dovremmo imparare a riconoscere in colleghi di lavoro, politici e potenziali partner?

Coming face to face with a probable psychopath was enough to make Dr. Leanne ten Brinke rethink her career choices. Early in her 20s, while studying forensic psychology in Halifax, in the Canadian province of Nova Scotia, Ten Brinke was volunteering at a [parole office](## "ufficio di sorveglianza"), which would hold weekly group meetings for [released](## "rilasciare") [sex offenders](## "criminali sessuali"). “Most of the men showed [contrition](## "pentimento"),” says Ten Brinke. “They really seemed to recognise the damage that they had done.” Except for one. The treatment programme seemed “like a game to him”, she says. One week, in a discussion about the impact their crimes had on victims, this [rapist](## "stupratore") stared at Ten Brinke and, smiling slightly, started to say how much his victim looked like her, “and how I was ‘his type’. Clearly he was trying to scare me, and he did.”
It [put her off](## "scoraggiare, dissuadere") a career working with convicted criminals, but she remained fascinated with “dark personalities” — psychopathy, mainly, but also narcissism, machiavellianism (manipulating and exploiting others), and sadism. From politics to business to the media, it wasn’t as if there was a [shortage](## "carenza") of people to study. There were [selfish](## "egoisti"), [callous](## "spietati"), impulsive and manipulative people everywhere, often presenting as gregarious and [c[harm](## "danno")ing](## "affascinanti"). “It started to occur to me that these [traits](## "caratteristiche") aren’t just confined to an [underworld](## "mondo criminale"). These [traits](## "caratteristiche") appear in all aspects of our lives,” she says.
Now associate professor of psychology at the University of British Columbia, Canada, Ten Brinke says these people could be in our families, or living next door. They’re the trolls online. They’re at work, at school, leading our institutions and our countries.
In her book Poisonous People, Leanne ten Brinke offers a perspective on dark personalities, bringing together personal stories and research. She says such [traits](## "caratteristiche") are quite common.
Instead of being specific conditions that one either has or hasn’t, psychopathy and other personality disorders are now thought to exist on a continuum, says Ten Brinke. It is estimated that 1 per cent of the general population have clinical levels of psychopathy (scoring highly on the [PCL-R](## "strumento per la valutazione della psicopatia ('Psychopathy Checklist—Revised')"), the psychopathy [checklist](## "lista di verifica") [assessment](## "valutazione") commonly used for diagnosis). Other studies have suggested that up to 18 per cent have elevated levels — what we may call “dark territory”, as Ten Brinke puts it in her book, Poisonous People: How to Resist Them and Improve Your Life. 
Although the vast majority of people do not have clinical levels of psychopathy, people with elevated dark [traits](## "caratteristiche") cause an [outsize](## "enorme") [harm](## "danno") to society, whether that’s in the criminal or political arena, or in the corporations they run. “The research suggests that [nature and nurture](## "natura e cultura") both play a role,” Ten Brinke says. This is particularly true for children with “[callous](## "spietati") unemotional” [traits](## "caratteristiche") and persistent aggressive behaviour. This can be measured, says Ten Brinke, “as early as two or three years old, and getting these kids into treatment as early as possible is important because there we can see changes to [traits](## "caratteristiche") over time.”

With adults, treatment can only really change behaviour, not personality. “The best research that we have on treatment for adults is coming out of [criminal justice settings](## "contesti del sistema di giustizia penale") — these are people who have shown very high clinical levels of these [traits](## "caratteristiche"), and have committed some crime, often violent in nature. The goal of those treatment programmes is usually to reduce the [likelihood](## "probabilità") of [recidivism](## "recidiva"), and especially violent [recidivism](## "recidiva"), and the research shows that it can work. Individuals, even with high levels of psychopathy, who stay in treatment show lower levels of reoffence over time.”
Ten Brinke watched a documentary on psychopathy one day at school and was [hooked](## "catturare l’attenzione"). “I was just so intrigued by someone who had such a different experience of the world than I do.” She was fascinated by criminals. Her doctoral thesis was on whether it was possible, by watching the public [appeals](## "appelli") of parents whose children had [gone missing](## "scomparire"), to detect those who had, in reality, killed them. By the end of her [PhD](## "dottorato di ricerca ('Philosophiae Doctor')"), however, Ten Brinke was getting tired of murder. She went on to study business, “where my interest really [broadened](## "ampliare"), to study dark personality [traits](## "caratteristiche") across different contexts.”
There is, she says, a small but reasonable chance that your boss is a psychopath, given that people with dark personality [traits](## "caratteristiche") are over-represented in [senior management](## "alta dirigenza"). “We certainly know that dark personalities are really interested in power and status,” says Ten Brinke. “They also tend to [come across](## "dare l’impressione") as quite charismatic and extremely confident, and we tend to confuse confidence for competence. Also, they’re probably just [putting themselves forward](## "candidarsi") for these positions more than the average person.”
It’s the same reason we think extreme confidence makes for good leaders — again, our [willingness](## "volontà") to [conflate](## "confondere") confidence with competence. A strongman type tends to think of themselves as a great leader, and they tell the rest of us that they are. “We tend to believe them because we have a [truth bias](## "inclinazione a credere agli altri"). Research suggests that we tend to just believe the things we are told, unless we’re given some very specific reason to be suspicious.” There is also what Ten Brinke describes as a “dark spiral”. At times of uncertainty and conflict, “we look for a strong leader. Then, in position, they can create more chaos, more instability, which actually makes us more likely to vote, again, for someone with these [traits](## "caratteristiche"). We can end up going round and round.”
In her book, Ten Brinke acknowledges that she has no idea how Donald Trump would score on the [PCL-R](## "strumento per la valutazione della psicopatia ('Psychopathy Checklist—Revised')") [checklist](## "lista di verifica"), but she suggests he has elevated [traits](## "caratteristiche"), giving the example of his tens of thousands of lies, [lack](## "mancanza") of empathy (such as when he [mocked](## "prendere in giro") a [disabled](## "con disablità") reporter), impulsiveness, and deviation from social norms. However, in one of her studies of more than one hundred US senators, dark personality [traits](## "caratteristiche") had a negative impact on their influence. What worked in getting [bills](## "progetti di legge") [passed](## "approvare") was cooperation, “people who are more [humble](## "umile"), who are courageous but are also willing to see the other side and admit if they make mistakes, admit if they change their mind.” Recognising and valuing these [traits](## "caratteristiche") may help us choose leaders differently.
Recognising dark personalities can help us make more informed choices in many areas of life, says Ten Brinke. “It can be either a ‘detect and [avoid](## "evitare")’ or a ‘detect and manage’ type of situation, depending on the circumstances.” Research has found that job adverts that look for people who are “results-oriented” or who “think outside the box” attract more narcissists than adverts whose language highlights a desire for team players. The same could be said for [dating](## "appuntamenti") profiles. How can someone [avoid](## "evitare") attracting a dark personality, or identify one from their profile? “They might use euphemisms to [put a positive spin](## "dare una connotazione positiva") on some of these [traits](## "caratteristiche"),” says Ten Brinke. “‘[Thrill-seeking](## "in cerca di emozioni forti")’ might indicate some impulsivity.” Power can be attractive, but “you might want to be [discerning](## "perspicace") about whether that is achieved by earning people’s respect or by putting people in a fearful, submissive position”.
 
It’s important to remember that someone’s personality is a [pattern](## "modello ricorrente"), she says — “first impressions will only [get you so far](## "servire fino a un certo punto")” — but there are broad behaviours to [watch out for](## "fare attenzione a"). “If someone interrupts often, but gets really angry if they are interrupted; if they ignore and [push personal and professional boundaries](## "superare i limiti").” They can be charismatic, and [c[harm](## "danno")ing](## "affascinanti"). In romantic relationships, love bombing at the start is often a feature. “I think that’s a good case for taking things relatively slowly. You need time and context to get a real sense of someone.”
People with dark personalities can be [skilled](## "abili") at deception, but we tend to be quite bad at detecting this, says Ten Brinke. Contrary to many people’s beliefs, you can’t [reliably](## "in modo affidabile") detect a lie by reading body language. Instead, you need to pay attention to [verbal cues](## "indizi verbali"), such as inconsistencies, but this is [tiring](## "estenuante") and probably not something you want to be [running in the background](## "eseguire in background") in every interaction. “But in some situations where I’m like, okay, this is a person who has a personality that is likely to manipulate, I might pay more attention to the words they’re saying. Ask good questions, ask [follow-up questions](## "domande di approfondimento").”
Those who [lean towards](## "essere incline a") narcissism can [display](## "mostrare") “classic things such as [showing off](## "vantarsi") and talking about themselves a lot. They are always the expert on every topic of conversation.” Someone with psychopathic [traits](## "caratteristiche") may be impulsive. “We also see emotional differences in the sense that they might be weirdly calm, showing no emotion.” Or their emotions might [flare up](## "esplodere") but be [short-lived](## "di breve durata"), particularly in the case of anger. We can all experience moments of schadenfreude at others’ [misfortune](## "sfortuna"), but if someone routinely [puts others down](## "umiliare") or seems to take pleasure in someone else’s failure or pain, “those can be important red flags. You start to gain evidence for a [lack](## "mancanza") of compassion or empathy for others.”
It’s unrealistic, Ten Brinke adds, to [avoid](## "evitare") everyone with dark personality [traits](## "caratteristiche"). Instead, for those with lower levels of psychopathy and other negative [traits](## "caratteristiche"), you can usually manage them. “We’re not changing someone’s personality, but we can approach our interactions with a [[harm](## "danno")-reduction mindset](## "approccio orientato alla riduzione del danno"). One is having clear boundaries.”
When I took the [assessment](## "valutazione") Ten Brinke includes in her book, [aimed at](## "mirata a") detecting low levels of dark [traits](## "caratteristiche"), I was alarmed to find I scored higher than I’d like. If someone is worried they’re a psychopath, what should they do? That worry is a good sign, she says. The usual problem for people with psychopathy is that “it can be quite difficult to motivate them to want to change.” Research shows that our personalities are not [chiselled in stone](## "scolpite nella pietra"). They can [shift](## "cambiare").” It may be [wise](## "saggio") to look at ourselves — the world does not need more people with psychopathic and narcissistic [traits](## "caratteristiche"). “I do think that recognising that we can all become a little bit less manipulative, a little bit more caring, and that all of these things will add up across people and across time, is a hopeful message.”

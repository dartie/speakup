The Amazing Kingdom of Fungi: The Mushroom Hunter [C1]

I funghi sono ovunque. Alcuni sono deliziosi, altri benefici, altri ancora letali, ma tutti svolgono un ruolo fondamentale negli ecosistemi terrestri. Abbiamo parlato con il micologo Bruch Reed di questo affascinante e ancora poco conosciuto regno della natura.

In Malheur National Forest in Oregon in the US, lives the world’s largest living organism: a parasitic fungus that covers almost four square miles, weighs up to 35,000 tonnes, and is thousands of years old. This organism, known as the Humongous Fungus, exists mostly underground as a vast network of thread-like filaments called mycelia, which spread through soil and tree roots. It acts as a parasite on conifer trees, attacking their roots and absorbing their nutrients, and produces honey-coloured mushrooms at the base of the trees it infects.

NEITHER ANIMAL NOR PLANT

This is one of the many fascinating and largely unknown facts about fungi, a distinct kingdom of eukaryotic organisms that includes yeasts, moulds and mushrooms. Neither animal nor plant, fungi are essential to our ecosystem and play a vital role in human health; hundreds of mushroom species have medicinal properties, and fungi are used in the production of many medicines, including penicillin.

EDUCATING THE PUBLIC

Mycology is the scientific study of fungi, and Bruch Reed is the COO of the North American Mycological Association (NAMA), a non-profit organisation of professional and amateur mycologists with over ninety affiliated mycological societies across the US, Canada, and Mexico. The organisation is dedicated to promoting scientific and educational activities related to fungi, and to informing the public about which mushrooms are safe to eat… and which ones could kill you. Amanita phalloides, a poisonous mushroom better known a death cap.

RECOVERING A LOST HERITAGE

NAMA has been organising annual forays since 1960, bringing together hundreds of mushroom enthusiasts, scientists and foragers in a different region of North America each year to survey fungal biodiversity. The gatherings combine fieldwork with lectures, identification workshops and a range of mushroom-inspired activities, from cooking and cultivation to artistic pursuits such as natural dyeing and paper-making using fungal materials. Participants also discuss medicinal uses, foraging practices, and newly-discovered species. To find out more about the fascinating world of mycology, Speak Up contacted Reed. He began by arguing that knowledge of fungi is part of a cultural heritage that, in many places, has been neglected or lost altogether. Culture

And in the United States and Canada, we broke that in a couple different ways. One, by being a nation of immigrants who came from someplace else. There are some of the same mushrooms in the places that immigrant people come from, and we have other ones that are different. But also, we, as a culture, oppressed native people and to an extent stripped their connection to their history with the land, and that’s a great loss of knowledge. There’s very little record of Indigenous peoples’ use of mushrooms, in particular in North America. We have a number of initiatives trying to connect with Indigenous folks in their communities to rebuild this knowledge.

GREAT BIODIVERSITY

Fungi remain one of the least understood groups of organisms on the planet. New species are discovered every year, and advances in DNA analysis continue to reshape scientists’ understanding of how fungi are related. Reed argues that the field is so vast that despite decades of study, the word ‘expert’ cannot be applied to anyone. Not even to him. Bruch Reed: Fungi are arguably the least historically studied kingdom of life. They’re difficult to study. They’re challenging to understand. Their diversity and their mode of conducting their lifestyle is humblingly vast. I don’t know of anyone in mycology who doesn’t cringe a bit when we hear the term ‘expert’ or get called ‘expert’, because I might know a lot more about mushrooms than most people, but to be an expert I think would take twenty-two lifetimes. I would never call myself an ‘expert’. I am also very proud of and adamant to call myself an ‘amateur mycologist’, because an amateur is one who loves what he or she or they pursue, and I love them, and I pursue them and I am constantly learning new things about mushrooms. At every single group event that I participate in, there will be a species that does not have a name. And DNA is unraveling those mysteries of identity and relationship, but we’re still discovering new species, even in places that are heavily mycologized. I’m unlikely to be able to go out and find a new snake species or even a plant, but fungi are so humblingly vast that there is infinite amount of work to be done just to know them, let alone understand the myriad ways in which they fit into the environment and control the environment and connect the environment and permit life itself to exist. 

INTEGRAL TO LIFE

Although mushrooms are the most visible part of the fungal world, they represent only a small fraction of fungi’s influence on life on earth. Many plants depend on fungal partnerships to obtain nutrients, while some species cannot even reproduce without them. Reed knows this as he also grows orchids as a hobby and the two passions turn out to be deeply connected. Bruch Reed: I just got back from studying these orchids in the wild in La Chinatla, in Mexico. It’s a secret love. Like, I have four hundred orchids in this apartment that I cultivate. Every time we see a wild orchid — and really most times we’re seeing any plant —, we are also seeing fungi that we don’t actually see. But without fungi, the orchid seed can’t germinate. The seedling cannot synthesise its own food and sugars. Orchid seeds are like dust, they’ve got nothing but DNA in them. There’s two million in every pod or so, but those seeds must land in a precise environment to connect with the fungus or a fungal spore to generate the plant that we are in awe of. The connectivity of fungi that’s integral to life itself will never cease to amaze me.  The horn of plenty, also known as the trumpet of the dead (Craterellus cornucopioides), is a highly prized edible mushroom.

DELICIOUS BUT DEADLY

And yet, as is universally known, fungi can also be deadly. Some species produce toxins capable of causing fatal poisoning if consumed. Recently, California has seen an unprecedented surge in poisonous mushrooms, specifically of the death cap (Amanita phalloides) and western destroying angel (Amanita ocreata) varieties. Since late last year, at least four people have died from eating these mushrooms, while over forty others have been hospitalised and three have required liver transplants. As COO of NAMA, Reed helps coordinate mushroom identification efforts for poison-control centres and works extensively to educate the public about both the benefits and risks of fungi. He believes greater fungal literacy could save lives. Bruch Reed: We have to rebuild what became a tenuous grip on this knowledge, because the flip side is, of course, that mushrooms can be wonderful medicine. There are mushrooms growing within walking distance of where I’m sitting, each of which could kill three adults. The morel poisonings, it’s from people eating them undercooked. A raw morel can kill people. Killed three people in 2023 in this country from raw morel — the Dave’s sushi incident sickened fifty-some people, two of them died of necrotizing gastritis. Mushrooms are beautiful and wonderful and delicious and healthful, but they have to be taken seriously.

DECEPTIVELY ORDINARY

It is worth noting that the most dangerous mushrooms can be deceptively ordinary in appearance. The death cap and the destroying angel are responsible for the majority of fatal mushroom poisonings worldwide. Their toxins attack the liver and other organs, and symptoms often do not appear until it is too late to prevent serious damage, as Reed explains. Bruch Reed: A couple bites of an Amanita mushroom, cooked or not, you get sick about six to eight hours. You think you have the flu, you’re achy, you might be throwing up, probably diarrhea. A couple days of that, you get better. And then one, two, three days… catastrophic organ failure that’s very difficult to treat and results in death too often. Destroying angels, they’re a whitish to off-white, greenish tinted with a sac-like base, white spores, and they taste good. Amanita is the Alice in Wonderland mushroom and the destroying angel. It’s a big genus, but there’s an Amanita that’s a delicious edible in the group of what we call Caesar amanitas. That’s Amanita vernicoccora. And that’s a wonderful, delicious Caesar mushroom. Caesars are a renowned millennia-old delicacy all over the world. But superficially, the mushroom can resemble each other.  And one of those destroying angels can kill three adult humans.   www.namyco.org If you want to know more about this topic, read the article Fungi: Nature’s Unknown Kingdom.

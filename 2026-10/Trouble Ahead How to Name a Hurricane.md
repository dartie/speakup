People like naming things. We name our children, of course, but also pets, lakes, mountains, plants, summer houses. Giving a name to something gives it an importance that anonymity usually [lacks](## "essere privo di"). If something has a name, it becomes more [relatable](## "vicino, riconoscibile"). The cat who lives on your street is just a cat; Miles the cat who lives on your street is a close friend. In some cases, though, giving a name can have unintended consequences that cause more [harm](## "danno") than good. 

## HUMAN NAMES
This is the case for hurricanes, which are commonly given ‘human’ names. The names for Atlantic hurricanes are chosen from a predetermined list in alphabetical order that rotates every six years (so after a while, you might hear the same name reused for minor tropical storms.) Names are sometimes retired if they were used for particularly devastating hurricanes; for example, the name Katrina has not been used since 2005.
The consequences of a tropical storm can be destructive and even deadly.

## WARNING!
Until the mid-1900s, hurricanes were [tracked](## "monitorare, registrare") using year and order of appearance. As you can imagine, this naming system wasn’t the [catchiest](## "facile da ricordare"). Researchers decided that giving them a [proper](## "adeguato") name would facilitate communication, an important consideration when talking about potentially devastating natural phenomena. If a warning came out telling people to be careful of Hurricane 1934-3, it remains an abstract concept. If the same warning is sent out about Hurricane Katrina, it suddenly becomes much more personal and urgent, and it’s easier to succinctly tell the public what they need to know.

## RISKY STRATEGY
But despite the usefulness of giving hurricanes names, this strategy comes at a risk. As we know all too well, the consequences of a tropical storm can be destructive and even deadly. [Flooding](## "inondazione") and strong winds damage buildings and landscapes, and anyone caught in the middle of the storm can be at high risk. The term ‘Hurricane Katrina’ still brings back memories of destruction and inadequate [aid](## "aiuto, assistenza") more than twenty years later.

Something so serious and devastating therefore becomes equated with a name. Which means that anyone with the name Katrina, for example, suddenly shares a characteristic with an [infamously](## "tristemente nota") negative thing. As a result, bullying based on a person’s name is common. In addition, if the shared name is one associated with a cultural or ethnic minority, it can increase the stigma already linked to a name marked as ‘foreign’.

## EFFECTIVE COMMUNICATION
A similar problem exists in the medical community when naming new diseases. When the coronavirus pandemic began, for example, it was briefly called “the Chinese virus.” The name not only led to discrimination against Chinese citizens and people of Chinese [heritage](## "origini"), but also provided a false sense of security in those who thought that if this was only a “Chinese virus”, anyone who wasn’t in China was safe.
Names are useful, and sometimes they make crisis communication more effective. But we need to be careful about how and why we name negative phenomena like hurricanes, because the unintended consequences can be serious.

A Short Story: The Deed to Be Done [C1]

Dopo un colpo in testa, il protagonista di questo racconto non riesce a capire la situazione in cui si trova. È in pericolo? Qualcuno vuole ucciderlo? La verità, però, è molto diversa.

Woof! Woof! Woof!” When I open my eyes, the pain explodes like a silent flash. A cold, deep wave of agony spreads from my left temple and washes over my body. It takes me a moment to realise that my head is resting on a hard place. The side of my face is dripping blood in the rain. I see red all around. Then black again. “Woof! Woof!” It takes me a few seconds to figure out where the sound is coming from. Oh, the pain… I try to focus my eyes. It’s a dog — a black and white shepherd of some kind. It seems to be hovering over me in the dark. Of course, it is not. It is me who is underground, lying in a muddy ditch. My head is throbbing, as if something inside were pounding against the sides trying to get out of my skull. I feel numb and weak, but somehow I force myself to sit up, with the black dog still barking non-stop. I turn around and my foot hits something that scrapes the rock with the sound of rusty iron — a shovel. I grab it by its wooden handle and wave it to scare the dog away. He barely retreats, but at least he stops barking, a puzzled look in his eyes. In the dim light of the waxing moon I take a moment to try to make out where I’m standing. The ditch is a rough rectangle, about six feet deep and another six feet long, maybe a couple of feet wide. It is — I realise with horror — a grave. I wave the shovel at the dog again. I need to get out of here before my gravedigger comes to finish the job. Running across the wet grass in rubber boots and a large plastic poncho that flaps in the cold night wind, I keep tripping and falling. The dog chases me, but still doesn’t try to bite me. It is as if he’s running alongside me rather than hunting me. It is barking non-stop, however, as though he’s trying to say something. Fiction

A Short Story: Best Friends Forever Certe amicizie durano in eterno. Ma c’è qualcosa che non quadra in quella di queste ragazze: è decisamente troppo lunga. Talitha Linehan , Molly Malcolm I run towards the only light I see, a small wooden house at the end of a narrow path. Drowning in pain, I push the front door open. The dog stays outside, barking no more. Somehow I find the latch instinctively — muscle-reflex-like — and lock the door. The place is silent except for the sound of the rain on the wooden roof. I go straight into the bathroom. In the mirror I see a face that the pain renders unrecognizable, covered in a pink hue. It has to be me. Who else? There’s a gaping wound of some five inches above my left eyebrow; the throbbing in my head is the loudest sound within the stillness of the house. Blood, rain, and mud — that face could be anyone’s. I could be anyone. I find some sort of bandage in the bathroom cabinet and a few aspirins, which I swallow in one gulp. What next? The numbing pain clouds my thinking. Is this my gravedigger’s house? Is this his dog? Who digs a grave in his own yard? For whom? I lock every door and window and try to stay awake. The pain helps with that. From a window I see the dog lying on the porch, a strange tranquillity in his rhythmic breathing. Despite the throbbing in my head I try to listen for steps approaching. Surely my executioner must be looking for me. But why? Why should anyone want to kill me? I try to find an answer in my mind but there’s no recollection that could explain these events. I mustn’t let the anguish take control of me, I tell myself. I must stay alert until dawn breaks, when I can get out of this house and find help. I will figure out the chain of events afterwards. Dawn breaks and I can see the dog sleeping peacefully on the porch. All around the house, the grass is wet with morning dew. There is no one in sight, so I wait no longer and open the door a few inches. Instantly, the dog sits up on his hind legs, wagging his tail as if happily awaiting my next command. On the path leading to and from the house, still wet and muddy, I can see the footprints of my rubber boots and the dog’s paw prints. Oddly, they come and go in both directions. On the path up to where my grave was dug, I see a continuous track, deeper than the footprints and roughly two feet wide. I cannot recall walking up that path, but I feel the imperative to follow the track, the dog diligently escorting me, as though that is the thread I must pull. It leads me to my intended grave. Oh, I understand now… I don’t recall anything, yet I understand. Oh, the horror…! Knowing is worse than living in fear and ignorance. Better to expect a terrible end than to realise one’s true duty — the task at hand that, once commenced, cannot be abandoned. There it is, wrapped in plastic at the end of the track, on the edge of the grave. The executioner’s job. The last part of the gravedigger’s business. The deed yet to be done.

It’s Halloween season, a time of ghosts, ghouls, vampires, and… zombies. At this time of year, people all over the world participate in zombie walks, by dressing and acting like zombies as they walk through cities, sometimes frequenting local pubs and raising money for a good cause. 

MINDLESS AND HUNGRY

The myth of the zombie as a reanimated being deprived of its will originated in Haitian folklore, in which a zombie is a corpse brought back to life, often through the magical practices of voodoo, and made into a mindless servant. Unlike their modern counterparts, these figures were not flesh-eating monsters but rather living or reanimated individuals stripped of will and autonomy, a concept often interpreted as reflecting the historical trauma of slavery and among communities of African descent. IN POPULAR CULTURE

The contemporary image of the zombie, however, owes much to George A. Romero’s 1968 horror film Night of the Living Dead, widely credited with introducing the modern idea of zombies as ravenous ghouls that feed on human flesh. The film, centered on a group of people who take refuge from a horde of cannibalistic monsters in an abandoned house, was inspired in part by Richard Matheson’s 1954 post-apocalyptic horror novel I Am Legend. In recent decades, the zombie genre has experienced a resurgence, thanks to films such as 28 Days Later (2002), Dawn of the Dead (2004), and World War Z (2013), as well as television series like The Walking Dead.

A ZOMBIE CONVENTION

People don’t just want to watch zombies in films and TV shows, however. At this time of the year in particular, they want to be (or at least pretend to be) zombies by participating in a zombie walk. The first organized zombie walk was in Toronto, Canada, in 2003, and the event soon became popular all over the world. The ‘zombies’ normally meet in a city centre and then walk through the streets and public spaces, or visit local pubs. Many events have live music and street food, and raise money for food banks or other good causes.

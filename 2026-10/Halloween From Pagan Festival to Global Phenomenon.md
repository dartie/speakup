Trick or treat? Halloween has become synonymous with a yearly ritual celebrated by children throughout Europe and the US; terrifying the neighbourhood with an implicit social contract: [hand over](## "consegnare") the [treats](## "dolcetti") or face the consequences!
Inside and out, houses in the UK and the US are [dressed to the nines](## "vestite di tutto punto") in [cobwebs](## "ragnatele"), skeletons and enough fake blood to alarm the neighbours. Jack-o’-lanterns — [pumpkins](## "zucche") [hollowed out](## "svuotare") and [carved](## "intagliare") into [leering](## "ghignanti"), [gap-toothed faces](## "facce sdentate") — are placed on [doorsteps](## "soglie di casa") as a signal to passing trick-or-treaters that they are welcome to [knock](## "bussare"). Adults, not to be [outdone](## "superare, fare meglio di") by children, have also [claimed](## "rivendicare") the holiday for themselves, dressing up to attend the most [bone-chilling](## "da brividi") of celebrations.

## CELTIC FOLKLORE
Halloween has become a global phenomenon, a billion-dollar industry, and the one night of the year when it is entirely acceptable to answer the door… dressed as a vampire.

Long before all the [money-spinning](## "lucrativo"), Halloween was the product of a meeting between two very different worlds. The ancient Celtic festival of Samhain, which marked the Celtic New Year and the night when the boundary between the living and the dead [grew thin](## "diventare sottile"), and the Christian feast of All Saints’ Day were combined. All Saints’ Day was moved strategically to 1 Nov[ember](## "brace") by the Catholic Church, almost certainly to [ease](## "facilitare") the conversion of those same Celts who rather liked their existing celebrations.

## SAMHAIN 
To [unravel](## "svelare") the real story behind the world’s [spookiest](## "inquietante") celebration, Speak Up met with Lisa Morton, horror expert and author of Trick or Treat: A History of Halloween.
**Lisa Morton (American accent):** Halloween definitely gets a macabre side from that old Celtic holiday of Samhain. It was their New Year celebration. It was that time of the year when they were moving out of summer and into winter. We’re talking mainly the Irish Celts, by the way. We don’t know a lot about the other Celts because they didn’t write their own history down. But we do know about the Irish Celts celebrating Samhain, and it was a very cold time of year as a period between seasons and between years, it was a liminal time for them, and they thought it was that one night a year when those borders might be a little bit thinner. And they believed in very spooky things that were called the sídh, which is spelled S-I-D-H. And the sídhe [plural] could cross over on that one night, and they might do all kinds of terrible things. There are stories of them burning down the Celtic palaces or bringing a [hanged](## "impiccato") corpse back to life. So they were definitely a little bit afraid of that night, and they would tell these stories around their big celebrations at Samhain. 
Trick or Treat: A History of Halloween, Lisa Morton.

## PAGAN ASSIMILATION 
For all its [ghoulish trappings](## "orpelli macabri"), Halloween owes its survival to a remarkably pragmatic decision made by the Catholic Church. Rather than attempting to eradicate the Celtic festival of Samhain — a battle that history suggests rarely goes well for the eradicators — the Church chose a different approach: assimilation.
**Lisa Morton:** The Catholic Church was very [smart](## "intelligente") on these things. They had a doctrine in which they did not believe in [stamping out](## "sradicare") these existing pagan celebrations, but in assimilating them. So they, as we see in the case of Halloween, actually moved one of their existing celebrations to help [co-opt](## "coinvolgere") Samhain. And these practices were very successful. And we can see this, by the way, also in Mexico and Central America when their missionaries move in there and start converting the Aztecs and the Mayans. And they bring the same holiday with them, but when it combines with those traditions, we get Día de los Muertos, which is very different from Halloween. 

## AMERICAN POPULARITY
For a long time, Halloween remained a distinctly regional affair. It was celebrated primarily in Ireland and Scotland, where its Celtic roots [ran deepest](## "essere profondamente radicato"). It took one of history’s great human [upheavals](## "sconvolgimenti") to carry it across the Atlantic. When large numbers of Irish and Scottish migrants crossed the Atlantic in the 1840s, many [fleeing](## "fuggire") [famine](## "carestia") and economic [hardship](## "difficoltà"), they brought their traditions with them, and America, it turned out, was ready to embrace them.
**Lisa Morton:** It was celebrated pretty extensively only in Ireland, in Scotland, for centuries. But then when the Irish and Scottish immigrated to America in the 1840s to escape [famine](## "carestia"), they brought that holiday with them. And it really [caught on](## "prendere piede") in the United States. And one of the reasons it [caught on](## "prendere piede") in the 19th century here was magazines, [of all things](## "incredibilmente"). Print technologies were just [coming into play](## "entrare in gioco"). Everybody loved to read magazines and the magazines would write about this [quaint](## "pittoresca") holiday that the Scottish and Irish immigrants were bringing with them. And American mothers and kids absolutely loved it. And by the end of the 19th century, it was very, very popular.
Children carve a Halloween pumpkin in this photograph from the 1930s. The tradition of creating jack-o’-lanterns remains one of the most enduring symbols of Halloween celebrations.

## BUY OFF THE KIDS
Halloween may feel like a [timeless](## "senza tempo") tradition, but some of its most iconic elements are surprisingly recent inventions. Trick-or-treat, that [cornerstone](## "caposaldo") of modern Halloween, is [barely](## "a malapena") a century old. It was born not out of ancient ritual, but out of a very practical desire to stop children destroying things. For much of its early American life, Halloween was less a night of innocent fun and more a night of escalating [mayhem](## "caos"), with gangs of young boys causing enough damage by the 1920s that several cities seriously considered [banning](## "vietare") the holiday [altogether](## "del tutto"). In the end, the solution was simple: [bribe](## "corrompere") them with sweets.
**Lisa Morton:** It really wasn’t until the middle of the 19th century that children became such an important part of the holiday. Before that, if we look, for example, at how it was celebrated in the 18th century in Scotland and Ireland, it was a night mainly for young adults. And they would have parties, and they would [tell fortunes](## "predire il futuro") at the parties. They would play these games that they hoped would tell them who they were going to end up marrying, because that was such an important part of their lives. And it really isn’t until it comes into America and gets spread around the middle class that it becomes a thing for children. And then, of course, by the time we move into the 20th century, just before trick-or-treat, the festival was known mainly for [prank-playing](## "fare scherzi")... mainly young boys around America would play these pranks. And sometimes the pranks were very destructive. And by about the 1920s, it reached the point where a lot of cities in America were considering trying to ban the holiday. Because these destructive pranks were costing them so much money. But fortunately, [a few cooler heads prevailed](## "ha prevalso il buon senso") and said, “Hey, you know, maybe we would have more success [buying these kids off](## "corrompere").” And by the mid-1930s, we see the spread of trick or treat, and that is indeed very successful in [putting a halt](## "porre fine a") to the [prank-playing](## "fare scherzi"). 

## NEITHER HEAVEN NOR HELL
No Halloween image is more iconic than the [grinning](## "sogghignante"), [candlelit](## "illuminata da candele") pumpkin. But the jack-o’-lantern has a surprisingly dark origin. It takes its name from [Stingy](## "tirchio") Jack, a folk legend anti-hero so [thoroughly](## "completamente") unpleasant that neither heaven nor hell would accept him after death. Condemned to [wander](## "vagare") the earth forever, he lit his way with a burning [ember](## "brace") inside a hollowed [gourd](## "zucca ornamentale"). 
**Lisa Morton:** The Jack-o’-Lantern is based on a very old legend of Jack the [Trickster](## "imbroglione"). He’s sometimes called [Stingy](## "tirchio") Jack. And the legend was a guy who was really [mean](## "cattivo") and really bad, and he [tricks the devil three times out of](## "ingannare qualcuno per portargli via qualcosa") taking his [soul](## "anima"). But finally, Jack dies. He tries to get into heaven. Of course, heaven doesn’t want this guy. And when he tries to go to hell, the devil says, “I don’t want you either, but I’ll give you this burning hell [ember](## "brace"), and you can use that to light your way.” So Jack puts it in this [carved](## "intagliare")-out [gourd](## "zucca ornamentale"). And he uses that to light his way as his spirit [wander](## "vagare")s the earth forever. And of course, in Ireland and in Scotland, where they were first carving out these [gourd](## "zucca ornamentale")s, they didn’t have [pumpkins](## "zucche") because those are native to the New World. So when they came here in the 1840s and brought this idea with them and they found our beautiful big orange [pumpkins](## "zucche"), those were great for making jack-o’-lanterns. And by the time you get to about 1900, you can look at the merchandising for Halloween and just see that pumpkin is everywhere now and had just become the king of Halloween.
Actors dressed in traditional Celtic-inspired costumes walk through central London during a Tourism Ireland campaign highlighting Halloween’s origins in the ancient Irish festival of Samhain

## DARK LEGENDS
For a holiday built on myth, Halloween has accumulated some spectacular myths of its own. Perhaps the most persistent is the idea that Samhain was a Celtic festival dedicated to a lord of the dead, a notion that sounds ancient and sinister but can be [traced to](## "risalire a") a single, spectacularly-[unreliable source](## "fonte inaffidabile"). Charles Valancy, an 18th century [surveyor](## "agrimensore") who [moonlighted](## "svolgere un secondo lavoro") as an amateur historian with more enthusiasm than [accuracy](## "accuratezza"), decided that Samhain, long-established as simply [mean](## "cattivo")ing ‘summer’s end’, was somehow connected through a tortuous etymological chain to an Indian deity associated with death. His six-volume collection of Celtic folklore [found its way into](## "finire per arrivare a") libraries around the world, and the myth [took root](## "mettere radici"). 
Then there is the poisoned Halloween candy [scare](## "paura, timore"). Few Halloween fears have proved more durable than the spectre of anonymous strangers [slipping](## "introdurre di nascosto") poison into sweets or [concealing](## "nascondere") [blades](## "lamette") in fruit, despite the fact that real cases have been very rare. 

## REAL FEARS 
The holiday, it seems, is as good at generating its own horror stories as it is at celebrating them. Which raises a rather interesting question: why do we enjoy being frightened in the first place? Halloween and the horror genre have grown up together, feeding each other’s appetite for the macabre, and neither shows any sign of losing its [grip](## "presa, influenza") on our imagination.
**Lisa Morton:** One of the things that I think is so interesting about Halloween is that it is a celebration of darkness, of things that we might be afraid of, but it makes those things playful and fun. We like, of course, this sort of visceral reaction, which is your heart is beating faster, you’re [involved](## "coinvolto"). And then after the scary moment passes, you get that [rel[ease](## "facilitare")](## "liberazione"), you laugh, you survived, it was fun. And I think that does help us in some ways to deal with our real fears. It serves the same purpose as a great horror movie or a great horror novel in that it helps us recognize these fears, and then maybe we can even learn to deal with them a little bit. And of course, every day the world is becoming a much more [fearful](## "spaventoso") place, which I think is one of the reasons that we are seeing an incr[ease](## "facilitare") in the popularity of both horror and Halloween. And with Halloween, you have the extra [layer](## "strato") of things like [costuming](## "travestimenti"), where you get to become somebody else for a night, and that can be very empowering, especially for kids.

## GUARDIANS OF THE OTHERWORLD
The sídh are the supernatural beings at the heart of Irish [fairy folklore](## "folklore delle fate"), and they are [far removed](## "molto lontane") from the [sparkly](## "luccicanti"), benevolent creatures of popular imagination. In Irish tradition, the word originally referred to the ancient mounds and fairy forts [scattered](## "sparsi") across the Irish landscape, which were believed to be entrances to the [Otherworld](## "Altromondo"), a parallel [realm](## "regno") of extraordinary beauty and danger. Over time, the term came to describe the beings themselves.
The sídh are not [straightforwardly](## "chiaramente") good or evil, but they are unpredictable and are best treated with extreme caution. They [encompass](## "includere") a wide range of beings, from the banshee, whose [wail](## "lamento") [foretells](## "predire, presagire") a death in a family, to [shapeshifting](## "che mutano forma") [mermaids](## "sirene") and púcas, bringers of both good and bad fortune. They are especially dangerous at liminal times — midnight, sunrise, sunset, and the seasonal festivals of Samhain and Bealtaine — when the boundaries between worlds grow dangerously thin.

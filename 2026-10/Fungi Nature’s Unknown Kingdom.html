Fungi: Nature’s Unknown Kingdom [C1]

Il regno dei funghi è immenso e, per la maggior parte, ancora da scoprire. Eppure ne sappiamo davvero poco. Da secoli, però, i funghi vengono utilizzati sia in medicina che in cucina.

Fungi — a kingdom that includes yeasts, moulds, and mushrooms — are vast, strange, and endlessly versatile. More importantly, they are essential to life on earth and yet they remain one of the great unknowns of the natural world.

UNDISCOVERED

For centuries, fungi were classified as plants. It was only in 1969 that scientists finally gave them a kingdom of their own. In fact, fungi are genetically closer to animals than to plants. Like animals, they cannot produce their own food through photosynthesis and must absorb nutrients from their surroundings. More than 150,000 species have been identified so far, but researchers believe millions more may still be undiscovered.

MEDICINAL

Almost all plants depend on fungi living around their roots to help them absorb water and nutrients from the soil. Together, they form underground networks so vast and interconnected that scientists have coined the term ‘wood wide web’ to describe the way they link trees and plants through webs of microscopic threads. Fungi are also nature’s great recyclers. By breaking down dead wood, leaves, and other organic matter, they return carbon and nutrients to the soil, making new life possible. Without them, dead plant material would simply accumulate around us. As COO of the North American Mycological Association, Bruch Reed has spent decades immersed in this world. We spoke with him to find out what makes fungi so fascinating. One area that is attracting growing interest is their potential use in medicine. Reed says that some of the most familiar mushrooms are also among the most promising. Lingzhi mushroom cultivation. This mushroom has been used in traditional Chinese medicine for over 2,000 years.  Bruch Reed: Mushrooms can be medicinal across a number of ways. Edible mushrooms are nutritious and delicious and contain protein, may or may not contain various substances that help our gut health, help brain health. There are mushrooms that have been used for millennia in TCM — traditional Chinese or Asian medicine —, such as the Phellinus genus, reishi mushroom, which is Ganoderma, various others. Lion’s mane, for example, is a culinary mushroom that’s delicious and amazing and versatile and super nutritious and has been shown to be neuroregenerative. So it stimulates the growth of new neurological cells in adult mammals. And it’s being investigated for treatments for Parkinson’s disease, et cetera. Culture

PSYCHOACTIVE

Psychoactive mushrooms containing psilocybin have been used for centuries by Indigenous cultures and are now being studied for their potential role in treating conditions such as depression and PTSD. While the subject remains controversial, Reed believes it deserves serious scientific discussion. Bruch Reed: Psychoactive mushrooms are native to North America. They are abundant, particularly along the coasts. And while my organization will never take an advocacy position one way or another on those, we do acknowledge and study and document those. They are being extensively used to treat PTSD, depression. There are investigations into neurological healing from those mushrooms because they can really be good for people. So while we’re never going to advocate for them, we are definitely documenting the science about those. And it needs to be discussed. It’s reality.

DELICACY

Some of the world’s most celebrated foods — including truffles, porcini, chanterelles, and morels — are fungi prized for their distinctive flavours and aromas. According to Reed, mushrooms can evoke an astonishing variety of tastes and textures, ranging from meat and chicken to bread, flowers, and even sweets. Bruch Reed: As a kid, I only liked morels. I wouldn’t eat anything else. I always hated the cream of mushroom soup. Yuck! And I didn’t like store-bought mushrooms. And I came to find out there are mushrooms that have the taste and texture of chicken that can freak out vegans when you serve it [them] to them. There are mushrooms that taste like candy. There are mushrooms that taste like meat, like absolutely like a meat dish, just naturally on their own. There are mushrooms that taste like flowers and bread and cucumber. There’s this wide variety of flavour profiles and texture profiles that you can experience while enjoying something that provides health benefits and is nutritious. I love eating them. I love it! And most mushrooms are edible. Are they good? Maybe not, but most are edible. Some are toxic. Some of the toxic ones will absolutely end your life. So that’s why education is just so important. The most important component of mycology is propagating the knowledge.

EAT THEM!

The mushrooms found in supermarkets offer only a glimpse of the fungal kingdom’s culinary diversity. Reed points out that flavour can vary dramatically depending on where a mushroom grows. He encourages people to explore new varieties while learning from experienced foragers and experts. Bruch Reed: Some of the reasons I didn’t like the mushroom soup is that those mushrooms are just boiled. They have a narrow flavour profile. Now, those are Agaricus genus mushrooms. Wild Agaricus are a world of difference apart. One of the ones that tastes so delicious like meat is the state mushroom of Colorado, Agaricus julius. And that thing smells like almonds and umami and tastes like sweet meat. It’s so delicious. And that’s an Agaricus mushroom. The same species that are grown in sterilized manure on farms, you can find that same mushroom growing wild, and the flavour profile will be incredibly different from the terroir and the conditions. I will say, I’ve been pleased to see that over the last twenty years, there are increasing varieties of mushrooms, cultivated and wild, that are offered in a lot of grocery stores. They’re very good. The flavour profiles and textures may vary from wild. You can find lion’s mane and hen of the woods and chanterelles and winter chanterelles and shiitakes. Oh, shiitake mushrooms are so good! So getting those from the store, great. What a great mushroom to eat. So I encourage people to try them, but I also encourage people to join their mushroom club, learn from their local experts. You can expand your repertoire of mushrooms that you eat on your own. It is important that you always remember only to eat a mushroom if you personally can identify it or it’s identified by someone who is knowledgeable. It’s so much fun to walk out into a forest and pick a luxury food that you probably couldn’t afford. I mean, morels are fifty bucks a pound. I’ve never bought a morel in my life, and I wouldn’t. There’s a lot of adventuring to be done safely in the mushroom world, and if you’re a foodie, you should avail yourself of it. Your life will change if someone properly cooks for you fresh black trumpets. The scent is intoxicating. The flavour is this elusive but delicious umami, sweet forest mushroom essence. They’re sacramental in terms of a culinary delight, honestly. Fresh porcini, oh! Fresh oysters, what a delight! What a delight it is to eat woodland fresh oysters. Eat it all you want!

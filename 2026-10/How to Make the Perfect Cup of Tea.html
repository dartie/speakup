How to Make the Perfect Cup of Tea [B2]

Henrietta Lovell, intenditrice di tè e fondatrice della Rare Tea Company, ci parla della passione tutta britannica per questa bevanda e ci offre alcuni consigli per prepararla alla perfezione.

Nothing defines the British more than their love of tea. While afternoon tea is most well-known around the world, tea is welcome at any time. Around a million cups are consumed a day in the UK! A good, strong ‘cuppa’ lifts the spirits and is the cure for all ills, from tiredness to a broken heart. Despite the many varieties, the usual tea enjoyed by the average British household is black and comes in a tea bag. Discovering good-quality tea, however, is like “realising there is more to coffee than instant granules.” So says Henrietta Lovell in her new book Infused: Adventures in Tea. 

THE RARE TEA LADY

As the founder of The Rare Tea Company, she imports loose-leaf tea directly from the growers. She spoke to Speak Up about the British obsession with tea which, she says, does not equate to a great tea culture.  Henrietta Lovell (English accent): People believe that English people grow up with a great tea culture, but that’s not true. We just have a great love of tea. So people drink a lot of tea, but that doesn’t mean they drink a lot of good tea. And like most British people and most people across the world, I grew up with an industrial tea bag, made by vast industrial machinery for a low price, and I got it from the supermarket in a box and I had no idea of where it came from or that there was another world beyond.   Infused: Adventures in Tea, Henrietta Lovell. Lovell, founder of the Rare Tea Company, has dedicated her career to sourcing single-estate loose leaves directly from small, sustainable farms worldwide.

A CULTURAL PHENOMENON

Even without a strong ‘tea culture’, there is no doubt the British love their tea. This stereotype has appeared in books and films for generations. We asked when and why a whole nation became so obsessed with it. Henrietta Lovell: There’s no real reason why we became so obsessed with tea. Just a cultural phenomenon. It started many hundreds of years ago and became more and more prevalent in British society. It started very much with the aristocracy and the rich traders who could afford it. And then as tea became more affordable, less taxes, it spread around the world… it spread through society. But the change really happened in the way we drank it in the Second World War. Because before that, like wine, we would go to the grocer and choose a little of what we could afford. You know, “Oh, my mother-in-law is coming. I need to buy the really lovely Lapsang Souchong from the Wuyishan [region] in China that she loves,” or “The priest is coming and he really loves the Keemun or Indian tea,” and you [would] choose what you could afford in the situation. EGALITARIAN TEA BAGS

Everything changed with the Second World War: tea was rationed, and the government supplied the cheapest kind, which soon became the norm. After the war, supermarkets reinforced this trend. They pushed out small grocers and put pressure on producers. Lovell explains that wartime rationing and supermarkets together led to the cheap tea Britons drink today. Henrietta Lovell: These two factors hitting together made us very used to drinking cheap, industrial tea and forgetting the other part. And partly also the wonderful egalitarianism of the war, where we all worked together. In Britain we had a National Health Service afterwards, free health care, all those wonderful things, free education, that came with this understanding that we are all level. And no more ‘upstairs, downstairs’… no more Lord and Lady and chambermaids, let’s all be equal, we’ll drink the same tea. And so it felt like maybe a betrayal of our egalitarianism. But actually, it’s a betrayal of our brothers and sisters in Africa and India, when we close our eyes to their suffering, so we can drink cheap tea.

NOT SNOBBERY

So, cheap, everyday tea has a long socio-economic history behind it. Persuading people to explore more expensive, loose-leaf varieties is more than a question of taste. Deeper feelings and cultural sensitivities need to be considered, too. Henrietta Lovell: It’s an emotional attachment and to say something is wrong can make people very unhappy. They can be… upset, like, “Don’t tell me I’m wrong, this is what I like.” So, I would rather like them to embrace more than the industrial, because there’s more out there. So, you might drink a very affordable bottle of wine from the supermarket, it just says ‘red wine’ and maybe it doesn’t tell you the story of where it comes from. But you know that there is another world if you really want to celebrate or to share with friends… you choose a little bit more carefully and you find out what the region is, you know some story of the maker, the grapes… You know, you get excited about it. You look for these incredible flavours rather than just, like plonk to go with your stew or maybe to cook with. There are different levels and we all understand that… it’s not snobbery, trying to be super sophisticated. It’s just good and we pay sometimes a bit more for something a bit better. THE PERFECT BREW

Despite the wide variety, all tea is made from a single plant: Camellia sinensis. The way it is processed determines its flavour. Lovell points out that tea bags are made through industrial processes, using bleaches and glues. The solvents and chemicals used to make the bag will end up in your tea. Given that loose-leaf tea is the better option, how do you make the perfect cup? Henrietta Lovell: There are three things you can change: the water temperature; the amount of tea to water; and the time you infuse it. And the key things to know are, if you want stronger tea, use more tea, not longer time. Longer time will extract more bitter flavours, so, the more bitter and drying you want it, leave it the longest time. But really, in the first sixty to ninety seconds, you’ll get most of the beautiful flavours from it. And if you want it sweeter, drop the temperature down a bit, because those bitter elements dissolve at higher temperatures. So, if you drop a green tea down to like seventy degrees [Celsius] instead of a hundred, it will be sweeter. And play around with those variables, how much leaf to water you use, what temperature you infuse that and how long, and you’ll be able to make this whole world of different variation, like the recipe in a cake.

TEA IS GOOD FOR YOU

What about milk? Lovell says you need strong flavours to balance a silky drop of milk. Boiling water and a longer extraction time will help to draw out the tannins; otherwise, the milk will overpower the tea. She always adds milk after brewing. Once you know how strong it is, you can adjust the amount accordingly. However you like to drink it, tea is generally seen as healthy. Claims that some types help with weight loss or detoxification are more questionable. We asked Lovell if tea really is good for you. Henrietta Lovell: For thousands of years it’s been thought of as a medicine. So in China, it really is always considered like that. It is full of antioxidants and polyphenols that are very beneficial for the body. It also contains L-theanine, which is a nice sort of mood-enhancing molecule. If caffeine is a problem for you, there are lots of beautiful herbs in the world that grow locally that you can use, you know, lemon verbena, for instance, or rooibos has no caffeine… these are herbs. But if you’re okay with caffeine, it will taste delicious, it will make you feel good, it will give you a gentle lift, it will help with your antioxidants and all those wonderful properties that have been enjoyed for thousands of years.

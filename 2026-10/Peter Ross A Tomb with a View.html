Peter Ross: “A Tomb with a View” [C1]

Nel suo pluripremiato libro, l’autore fa rivivere le straordinarie storie dei cimiteri di Regno Unito e Irlanda attraverso le vicende delle tante persone, vive e non, le cui esistenze sono profondamente legate a questi luoghi.

A Scottish woman accused of being a witch, an unmarked grave for babies, an Irish guide who took his own life in a famous Irish cemetery… These are the themes of just a few of the extraordinary stories told in the award-winning book A Tomb With a View: The Stories and Glories of Graveyards by Peter Ross. The book explores the graveyards of the UK and Ireland through some of the people who are buried in them, as well as those who help preserve their history and their grounds.

LIFE TO DEATH

When Ross began working on A Tomb With A View, he wanted to write a book about life and the living. But, he told Speak Up, it evolved into a book about love, specifically “the love we feel for the people that we lose,” as expressed in life and in death. This theme resonated with critics and readers, and the book went on to be named the non-fiction book of the year by Scotland’s National Book Awards, as well as a Financial Times Book of the Year and a Radio 4 Book of the Week. St. Kessog’s Graveyard, an old graveyard situated in Callander, Scotland, is located within the Loch Lomond and Trossachs National Park.

PREVIOUS BOOKS

Ross is an author and journalist based in Glasgow, Scotland. Previous books include Steeple Chasing: Around Britain by Church, a portrait of Britain told through the stories held within its churches, and Upon a White Horse: Journeys in Ancient Britain and Ireland, a journey through the prehistoric sites of the British Isles, spanning from the Stone Age to the era of the Vikings. He described the three books as “an informal trilogy”. Together they span human history, from the Stone Age to Victorian Times, and document, he says, “a sort of expression of our humanity in stone, whether that’s gravestones or churches or stone circles.” We spoke to Peter Ross to learn more about his fascination with graveyards, and what these spaces reveal not only about the dead but also about the living. Places

Glasgow Necropolis: The City Of The Dead La necropoli di Glasgow è un vero e proprio museo a cielo aperto. Tra statue, lapidi e antiche iscrizioni, in questo immenso giardino vittoriano emerge la complessa realtà della società scozzese del XIX secolo. Julian Earwaker, Justin Ratcliffe Peter Ross (Scottish Accent): First of all, I’ve got a long interest in graveyards going back to my childhood. When I was a little boy and used to spend a lot of time with my grandparents who lived in Stirling, which is in central Scotland, I used to go and hang out, spend most of the day in the old graveyards, which wasn’t too far from their house, just up the hill near Stirling Castle. And some of the graves there are hundreds of years old and they had very interesting medieval carvings on them, so, skulls and crossbones and angels and hourglasses with wings and things like that. And I used to find the look of those things really fascinating. I used to love to touch those old stones. But also some of the more modern stones, some of the Victorian stones, the names on those stones were always intriguing to me. Like, I always used to wonder who those people were and what their relationships were with each other, and how they lived and how they died. There was also a very specific thing, which is that when I moved to the house that I’m in now in Glasgow, in the south of Glasgow, it’s very close to a Victorian cemetery called Cathcart Cemetery. And when we first moved here and I was walking in the area trying to get to know the new place where we lived, I went into the cemetery, which is right behind our house, it’s on a hill behind the house. And there was a particular grave on which was written: “Mark Sheridan, Comedian”, and that he had died during the First World War. And I was just really intrigued by that, I’d never seen the word ‘comedian’ on a grave before. So I looked into who he was. And it turned out that he was this music hall star from the northeast of England who had very tragically taken his own life while he was on tour in Glasgow, and had been buried here because that’s where he died. It made me think that there must be stories like that everywhere, really.

PSYCHOGEOGRAPHY

Across the UK and Ireland, there are thousands of graveyards with remarkable architecture, mausoleums, and statuary. Ross, however, was searching for something more: a deeper connection between place and people capable of shaping emotions, memory, and behaviour. Peter Ross: With all of my work, whatever I’m writing about, whether it’s graveyards or churches or anything else, it isn’t really enough for the place itself to be amazing. I’m always looking for the present tense human story that can go with that place. So it was a case of trying to find amazing places, but where I could tell a story about people now and why those places are important to people now. So for example, there’s a cemetery in Dublin called Glasnevin Cemetery, which is this incredible-looking, vast cemetery in Dublin. A lot of famous Irish people from across the centuries is [are] buried there. In the centre of it, it’s got this great white tower with a kind of conical top that looks like something from a fairy tale… you know, it looks like the tower from which Rapunzel let down her hair. So I really wanted to write about that place, but what allowed me to do so was the fact that an individual called Shane MacThomais, who was the tour guide in Glasnevin, and very well known in Dublin for giving tours of that cemetery, he died actually in tragic circumstances in the cemetery itself. And so I kind of wanted to try to tell the story of the place and the person and the way that both shaped the other. So that was what I was looking for, that connection between a historic place that’s amazing and a contemporary person that’s also amazing and kind of trying to bring them together. St. Mary’s Church and its historic hilltop graveyard in Whitby, North Yorkshire, overlook the North Sea. Reached by the town’s famous 199 steps, this centuries-old cemetery inspired scenes in Bram Stoker’s Dracula and remains one of Britain’s most iconic coastal burial grounds.

MANAGING DEATH

Wandering through cemeteries may seem morbid, yet many people feel drawn to these places and to the chance they offer to approach death in a calmer, less dramatic way, argues Ross. Peter Ross: In terms of the human response to cemeteries and graveyards, for some people they’re completely repellent and frightening places, and consider it morbid to spend any time in there. And others are somehow drawn to them. And I think that the reason that we are drawn to them is perhaps a mixture of things to do with an interest in the history that these places tell us; graveyards are libraries of the dead. You can learn things about people and about the past from cemeteries. So there’s that aspect of it. But I think also there’s something to do with being places that allow you to maybe confront death in quite a sort of safe and manageable way. Similarly to the way that some ghost stories function — we read ghost stories with a certain degree of pleasure and they’re a way of processing our feelings about the dead and about death.

WHISPERED WORDS

For Ross, acknowledging the presence of death is also a way of celebrating life. Peter Ross: It’s more than just the fact that you know that the dead are physically there and present. There’s a sense of them being a watchful and living presence around as well, which I don’t think is necessarily a religious feeling, it’s maybe an older feeling than Christianity, the idea that the dead are still with us and present in such places. And that to me didn’t feel frightening or troubling, but rather kind of neighbourly. You could feel the arm around your shoulder and the whispered word in your ear that everything is going to be okay. The grave of Thomas Sayers, Britain’s last bare-knuckle boxing champion, in Highgate Cemetery in London. The marble monument features a relief portrait of the boxer and is famously guarded by a life-sized statue of his loyal mastiff dog Lion.

ON MORTALITY

Mausoleums and statues raised to the dead are, for the living, declarations of love, says Ross. However, he gives little thought to his own death, nor does he wish for any grand gesture to commemorate him. Peter Ross: I don’t know if it really made me confront my mortality. I think perhaps that’s something that I’m super aware of anyway, which is perhaps one of the reasons why I’m interested in these places. In the UK, I think two thirds of people are now cremated rather than buried. But I think there is something tremendously enticing about those grand Victorian tombs, isn’t there? I mean, they’re so kind of flamboyant. I loved visiting some of the big Victorian cemeteries like Highgate or Kensal Green in London. Some of the tombs, they were so extroverted. These people were demonstrating three things, I think. They’re demonstrating the depth of their grief for the person they’ve lost, the depth of their faith, and also the depth of their pockets. They’re showing off how rich they are. But I’d like to think I’d maybe go for a slightly more humble statement for myself rather than some kind of great narcissistic edifice. Especially because I’m a writer and don’t have any money.

TECHNOLOGY

Ross’s work has taken him through dozens of graveyards filled with old headstones and ornate mausoleums. But can technology also change the way we will one day be laid to rest? Peter Ross: That is happening already. I believe there’s a photographer in Paris who has built his own tomb, while he’s still alive. And I think it’s got a QR code on it, so you can look at his work. But I suppose the danger with that is, how is technology going to move on? You know, will QR codes become an obsolete technology that nobody can use anymore, and we’ve moved on to something else? Whereas if you’ve got chisel and hammer and stone, you can pretty much guarantee that that’s going to last.

A BOOK ABOUT LIFE

Ross’ book features around twenty-five graveyards across the UK and Ireland, yet the conclusion he reaches is far from sombre. Peter Ross: I always meant for it to be a book about life, you know. That was always my intention. I always wanted to tell stories about the living, not just it be a list of the famous dead. But what I found as I worked on the book is that it’s really a book about love. That’s the great theme of A Tomb With a View, which is about love, it’s about the love we feel for the people that we lose, the love that we express towards them in life, and then also after their passing.

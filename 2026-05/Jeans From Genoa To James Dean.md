Everything begins with the great textile tradition of Genoa on the northwestern coast of Italy. As early as the Middle Ages, the people of Genoa produced a strong [fabric](## "tessuto, stoffa") made of [cotton](## "cotone") imported from the eastern Mediterranean. It was used to make clothes for [sailors](## "marinai") and port workers, as well as sacks, [sails](## "vele") and covers for [goods](## "merci"). The first ambassadors of this [garment](## "capo d’abbigliamento") were [therefore](## "quindi") Genoese [sailors](## "marinai") who wore it on their journeys to Mediterranean ports in France and Spain, to England and around the world.

## A DURABLE GARMENT
At that time, it was common for imported [goods](## "merci") to take their name from the place they came from. For this reason, the French identified this [fabric](## "tessuto, stoffa") as gênes (that is, Genoa in French.) In English pronunciation, this term was transformed into ‘jeans’. The first written record of the word [dates back to](## "risalire a") 1567.
The [spread](## "diffusione") of jeans increased greatly with textile industrialisation in the 18th and 19th centuries. In the 19th century, this [garment](## "capo d’abbigliamento") arrived in America th[rough](## "grezzi") immigration and transatlantic [trade](## "commercio").

## MR. LEVI STRAUSS
A German-born American [fabric](## "tessuto, stoffa") merchant and entrepreneur named Levi Strauss immediately understood the potential of this almost indestructible [garment](## "capo d’abbigliamento"). It seemed perfect for [gold prospectors](## "cercatori d’oro"), miners, railway workers, and cowboys.
The idea of adding the famous [copper](## "rame") rivets to [strengthen](## "rafforzare") points of stress and [wear](## "usura"), such as the pockets, came from Jacob Davis, an American [tailor](## "sarto") of [Latvian](## "lettone") origin. However, he did not have the sixty-eight dollars needed to register the patent, so he contacted Levi Strauss, who supplied the [fabric](## "tessuto, stoffa"). They became partners and in 1873 they [filed](## "depositare un brevetto") the patent together.

## BLUE JEANS
The [fabric](## "tessuto, stoffa") made by the Genoese was probably not the typical blue of modern jeans. It was mostly a natural [hue](## "tonalità"), that is, brownish, beige, or other [rough](## "grezzi") tones that were resistant to [dirt](## "sporco"). The blue colour we associate with jeans today arrived later, in the 19th century, with American industrial production and the use of indigo [dye](## "tinta, colorante"), which was relatively cheap, [long-lasting](## "resistente") and easy to find. Most importantly, it was very effective at hiding [dirt](## "sporco"). 

## YOUTH ICON
Until World War Two, jeans were still only a [humble](## "umile") work uniform, and no one could imagine their future fame. It was only in the 1950s that they conquered young people and then the world. The decisive moment came th[rough](## "grezzi") American cinema: when Marlon Brando and James Dean wore them in The Wild One and Rebel Without a Cause, respectively, jeans immediately became popular with youth culture.

## EGALITARIAN GARMENT
From there, it was a short step to becoming a symbol of counterculture and rebellion among teenagers in the 1960s. Jeans turned into a kind of uniform that expressed rejection of conventions and formal clothing, based on a simple and powerful egalitarian idea: a [garment](## "capo d’abbigliamento") equally worn by men and women, by the rich and the poor. And soon this symbol of Western culture would [break down geographical barriers](## "abbattere barriere") and become the most wide[spread](## "diffusione") item of clothing in the world.

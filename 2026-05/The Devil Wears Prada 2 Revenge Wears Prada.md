The Devil Wears Prada was Lauren Weisberger’s debut novel. Published in 2003 when the author was twenty-six, it fictionalised her ten-month experience as assistant to Anna Wintour, the powerful — and many would say [notorious](## "famigerata") — [editor-in-chief](## "caporedattrice") of Vogue. As one might imagine, Weisberger’s [scathing](## "tagliente") critique of the fashion industry elite didn’t [go down too well](## "essere ben accolto") with her [former](## "ex, precedente") employer. But the [backlash](## "reazione negativa, contraccolpo") did not prevent Weisberger from writing, and she has gone on to publish seven more books so far, including two sequels to her bestselling debut: Revenge Wears Prada (2013), When Life Gives You Lululemons (2018).

## ANOTHER CHAPTER
Weisberger’s journey to publishing a sequel to the hit-story was not easy. In a promotional interview in 2013, she talked about her desire to carry on telling the story of Andy, Miranda, and the other characters in The Devil Wears Prada:
**Lauren Weisberger (American accent):** I’ve always, always, always wanted to revisit these characters. I think the first book, it was primarily a fish-out-of-water story. Andy was working at Runway. She had no idea what was going on. She was confused and [overwhelmed](## "sopraffatta") and sort of terrified and yet seduced by the whole thing, and she really had to [figure that out](## "capirlo, venirne a capo") for herself. And part of what was so much fun about picking up was we have a different Andy now. She’s, of course, fundamentally the same person, but she’s older, she’s [confident](## "sicura di sé"). And things have changed, too, in Miranda’s life. She’s been through dozens, if not hundreds, of assistants since then. And I thought it was kind of fun to see how has she changed or has she changed [at all](## "per nulla")?
Anna Wintour and Anne Hathaway during the live 98th Oscars at the Dolby Theatre in Los Angeles in March 2026.

## FLASHY AND FASHIONABLE
For Weisberger, the experience of working at Vogue under Anna Wintour was a [stark](## "netto") contrast to everything that she had known before. Manhattan was [flashy](## "vistosa, appariscente") and fashionable, the powerful people there were extremely [demanding](## "esigenti") and she was young and inexperienced in this elite world. While Weisberger only worked as Wintour’s assistant for ten months between 1999 and 2000, [it’s fair to say](## "si può affermare che") the experience left an indelible mark on her — and changed the course of her career.

**Lauren Weisberger:** It was such a crazy place for me. I grew up in a very small town in Pennsylvania, and I went to a school in upstate New York with a big campus, and we wore huge [downcoats](## "piumini") and lots of [fleece](## "pile"), and there was no such thing as fashion [in any way, shape, or form](## "di nessun tipo"). I had no concept of it. And then to have my first job after university be this crazy world! It was shocking, the whole thing of it. And I just feel so lucky. I mean, how many twenty-two-year-olds get to have this [bird’s eye view](## "visione d’insieme, dall’alto") of how this incredibly powerful and very talented woman runs this industry [from the top down](## "dai vertici"), and just see everything she did, and all the [calls](## "decisioni, scelte") she made, and all the decisions she made. You know, her fingerprint is on every page of that magazine. And it was really, really amazing to see.

## GLAMOUR AND PAIN
[Enduring](## "sopportare") terrible bosses and precarious conditions was something that [resonated a lot with](## "risuonare, colpire") readers. While glamorous industries, such as fashion, entertainment and media, might have a reputation for having questionable practices, people often [put up with](## "sopportare, tollerare") more than they should. And this truth [struck a chord](## "colpire nel segno") with people from other industries, too. This fundamental truth could be the reason for the book’s success — the experience of working for a difficult, often irrational boss felt familiar to many.
**Lauren Weisberger:** One of the things that sort of surprised me with the reception of that book and in travelling around and meeting readers is how many people have had equally as horrible or sometimes even worse experiences. It’s just terrifying to think about. I’ve heard some of the most incredible stories from young women who have worked for just these [hideous](## "orribili"), [hideous](## "orribili") people. There’s still something, whether it’s fashion, media, entertainment, these sort of industries, they’re just sexy. They’re sexy and they’re fun. They are. And it’s why you can work a hundred hours a week and get paid nothing, and still want to do it. The whole sort of ambiance of the place is crazy and wild and fun.

The Sunday Roast: The Great British Roast-Off [B1]

Se c’è una cosa a cui i britannici non possono rinunciare (e di cui vanno molto orgogliosi) è l’arrosto della domenica. Una gustosa tradizione che risale addirittura ai tempi di Enrico VII.

According to a 2012 poll, the Sunday roast is the second thing Brits love the most about their country (the first is bacon sandwiches!) This is hardly surprising, given their love of nice meals and tradition. A more modest version of the Christmas dinner, the Sunday roast is the perfect excuse to indulge in some yummy food with family or friends, at home or in the pub.

BEEFEATING NATION

The most widespread version of the origins of the Sunday roast takes us back to the 15th century. It is believed that the royal guards of King Henry VII would enjoy a little feast of roasted beef every Sunday after church. This is why they came to be known as ‘beefeaters’. In the following centuries, the tradition spread to all the households that could afford it, and it became a Sunday staple. Eating a large meal after the Sunday service was common all over Europe, and the roast variant prevailed in the British Isles.  A Yeoman Warder (or Beefeater), one of an elite cohort of ceremonial guardians at the Tower of London. All Beefeaters are retired officers from the British Armed Forces with at least twenty-two years of service.

GOT A SPARE OVEN?

Traditionally, families prepared the meal before the Sunday service. The meat would be left to roast for hours, and it would be ready by the time they went back home after mass. Then, vegetables such as potatoes and turnips would be added. The juices from the meat and vegetables would be used to make a thick gravy to pour over them. Not all households owned a fireplace to make their Sunday dinner, however, so some people took their ingredients to a local bakery to roast them there, while they attended church. Afterwards, they would pick their food up and take it home, just like an early version of a takeaway! Since it was a big meal, leftovers would be used to make stews and pies in the following days. THE MODERN SUNDAY ROAST 

Although meat is made in the oven nowadays and not roasted by the fire, the Sunday roast remains a powerful tradition. Many UK pubs offer it even on weekdays!  The modern Sunday roast consists of a large piece of meat — it’s often roast beef, but it can also be lamb, pork, or chicken —, with potatoes and vegetables, and of course some delicious gravy. The most common side dishes for the roast include Yorkshire puddings, sausages, and a variety of sauces, like apple or cranberry sauce.

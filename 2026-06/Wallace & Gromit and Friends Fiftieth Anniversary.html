Wallace & Gromit and Friends: Fiftieth Anniversary [B2]

L’Aardman Animations di Bristol ha dato vita a personaggi memorabili. Ma dietro a questi pupazzi di plastilina si nasconde un complesso lavoro di pazienza, creatività e maestria.

For fifty years, Aardman Animations Limited has been creating some of the most recognisable characters in British animation. From Morph, to Wallace and his faithful dog, Gromit, to Shaun the Sheep, Aardman’s films are known for their warmth, humour and distinctive hand-made style. The stories are easy to follow, often relying on visual comedy rather than dialogue, but behind their apparent simplicity lies an extraordinary amount of skill and patience.

FROM THE KITCHEN TABLE

Aardman was founded in 1976 by Peter Lord and David Sproxton, who met as children at school and began experimenting with animation at home. Their earliest films were made on a kitchen table using everyday materials. This modest beginning remains central to Aardman’s identity, showing that animation can start with curiosity and imagination rather than expensive equipment. A scene from A Close Shave (1995), an animated film in the Wallace & Gromit franchise that introduced Shaun the Sheep.

A HAND-MADE STYLE

One of the most striking features of Aardman’s films is their hand-made quality. Characters are traditionally made from plasticine, sets are built by hand, and small imperfections remain visible on screen. At Aardman, this quality is often referred to as ‘thumbiness’: the visible marks left by the animator’s hands. Although Aardman’s films feel playful and spontaneous, a great deal of work happens before any filming begins. To find out more, Speak Up spoke to Tanaya Basu De Sarkar, co-curator of Inside Aardman: Wallace & Gromit and Friends, a new exhibition at the Young V&A about the studio’s celebrated body of work and its appeal to all kinds of audiences. She began by describing the long, meticulous process involved from the first idea to a finished scene. Tanaya Basu De Sarkar (English accent): There’s a lot of work that goes into making a stop-motion animation before you have the puppets on set. So even before then, you will have the script written, it’ll be storyboarded, which means each moment will be illustrated, a bit like in a comic strip. That will then be turned into a sort of very basic hand-drawn animation based on that same storyboard. And then, if there’s any audio to be recorded, that will be recorded. And all of this is done before you get to the point of filming anything. Then when it comes to filming, there’s one bit which is quite a fun thing, which is that directors and animators often act out the scenes themselves that they’re about to film. So this helps them get an idea of timing and the characterisation they want to put into that scene. So they’ll record that, they’ll look at that, sort of study from that a bit, and then, we get into the actual animating and that’s when animators, who are the people who move the models, the puppets, they will move an object a tiny bit, they’ll take a picture, then they’ll move it a tiny bit more, take another picture, and so on and so forth. And when you play this all back, all these slight movements, it tricks the eye into thinking that they’re moving. Places

LABOUR OF LOVE

The stop-motion technique, Basu De Sarkar explains, demands extraordinary dedication and time. Tanaya Basu De Sarkar: Stop-motion does take a lot of time. It’s a labour of love as an animation form or as any filmmaking form, really. Animators tend to produce about five seconds of footage per day. When you think about the fact that there are feature-length films to be made, you can see why it takes such a long time, and also why they work in teams. So there’ll be multiple animators working on the film at the same time because if it was just one person making five seconds of footage a day, you might not get a film released.

STORIES WITHOUT WORDS

Many of Aardman’s most famous characters communicate without speaking at all. This reliance on visual humour has helped the films reach audiences across the world. We asked why these stories travel so well across languages and cultures. Tanaya Basu De Sarkar: I think Aardman’s films are loved all over the world, including by people who don’t speak English because a lot of their main characters don’t speak at all. So, characters like Gromit or Shaun the Sheep, who are some of their most popular characters, they don’t talk and that means in turn that the films really rely on visual humour, so really subtle expressions, strong visual humour, facial expressions, and at the same time also their storylines are really compelling, and I think audiences across the world have probably identified quite a sort of British sense of humour that goes into them. And that’s made them really popular as well. A Shaun the Sheep sketch.

MATERIALS AND CRAFTSMANSHIP

Although Aardman’s characters look simple, they are carefully engineered to allow very precise movement. We asked Basu De Sarkar about the materials used and how consistency is maintained during long productions. Tanaya Basu De Sarkar: Traditionally Aardman are best known for their plasticine characters, their clay characters. So Wallace and Gromit would’ve been made earlier on plasticine and they still are. And also inside of them is a metal, what’s called an ‘armature’, which is basically like a skeleton, and that’s what really means that you can move the characters around and do fine movements with each of their fingers, so that you’re in control of how you’re moving the puppets for each of those small motions that need to be photographed. Aardman have always been innovative in their use of material, so they have made puppets which incorporate silicone as well, or 3D printed elements. In terms of how they keep puppets consistent, there’s a few things. One, they batch-make the clay in a machine [that] it looks like a sausage maker or something. It’s a huge contraption and they’ll batch make all the colours at once. So that you’ve got a lot of the same perfectly mixed colours to work to. But I guess that the main thing that makes them consistent is that they use moulds to sculpt out the characters, so they’ve got master copies of the sculptures, they’ve got moulds which they’ll continue to work on.

DIGITAL TOOLS

Although Aardman is best known for its hand-made animation, digital technology also plays an important role in the film-making process. Basu De Sarkar explained how tools like CGI and virtual reality are used alongside traditional stop-motion techniques. Tanaya Basu De Sarkar: I think the use of CGI elements is different across the films, but they use it to sort of enhance the storytelling rather than, let’s say, replacing the main characters with anything CGI or something like that. It’s just part of how they can best tell their stories. So if it’s like a crowd scene on the film of Early Man, for example, which was about cavemen footballers. There were lots of crowd scenes where the spectators are watching football. In that they made characters and then they duplicated them in CGI for a crowd scene, So it’s sort of using the building blocks to elevate their storytelling. And they’ve also started recently using VR as part of their set building, their set planning process. So they’ll make VR models first and plan how you’d film a scene within that VR set model, let’s say if it’s a chicken coop for Chicken Run, and then they can go on and build it. So it’s using tools to support those same traditional stop motion making methods.

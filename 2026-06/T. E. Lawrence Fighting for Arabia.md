T. E. Lawrence was an inspirational soldier who p[lay](## "giacere")ed an important role in the Arab Revolt against the Ottoman Empire, of which the Arab population was a part, during World War One. The Ottoman Empire then [encompassed](## "comprendere") many present-day [Middle Eastern](## "del Medio Oriente") countries such as Turkey, Syria, Iraq, Israel, Palestine, Jordan, and parts of Saudi Arabia. Lawrence’s legendary wartime activities in the Arab fight for independence, especially his unconventional guerrilla methods, which would influence [warfare](## "guerra, conflitto armato") in the future, made him famous around the world as Lawrence of Arabia.

## SYMPATHY FOR THE ARABS
Thomas Edward Lawrence was born in 1888, the illegitimate son of Sir Thomas Chapman, an Anglo-Irish [baronet](## "baronetto (titolo nobiliare britannico inferiore al barone)"), and Sarah Junner, a [nanny](## "bambinaia"). The couple never married, for legal reasons, adopting the surname Lawrence in an unsuccessful attempt to avoid scandal. They moved regularly before [settling](## "stabilirsi") in Oxford.


>All men dream, but not equally. The dreamers of the day are d[anger](## "rabbia")ous men, for they may act on their dreams, to make them possible. This I did.



After studying History at Oxford University, Lawrence worked as an archaeologist in Syria for the British Museum from 1910 to 1914. He learnt Arabic and developed a deep sympathy for the Arabs, who had lived under Ottoman [rule](## "dominio") for centuries and were now demanding independence.

## AN INTELLIGENCE OFFICER
When war began, with the Ottoman Empire deciding to support Germany, Lawrence was sent to work in the British Army intelligence unit in Cairo. The Arab Revolt began on 10 June 1916, when Sharif Hussein bin Ali, Emir of Mecca, declared independence, hoping to create a unified Arab nation. Britain supported the revolt, trying to destabilise the Ottoman front. Lawrence was sent to [liaise](## "fungere da tramite") with the forces of Faisal I of Iraq, Sharif Hussein’s son.

## GUERRILLA CAMPAIGN
In fact, Lawrence very quickly became involved in planning and leading a guerrilla campaign against the Ottoman forces, using [hit-and-run tactics](## "tattiche mordi e fuggi") to [mine](## "piazzare mine") railway lines and bridges, and disrupt communications and [supply routes](## "rotte di approvvigionamento"). He hoped Arab military success would lead to post-war self-government. His small but effective irregular forces [tied down](## "immobilizzare") thousands of Turkish troops, preventing them from fighting against the regular Allied forces.
Lawrence participated in several spectacular actions and was [wounded](## "ferire") numerous times. In July 1917, he led a 965-kilometre march across the desert to launch a surprise [inland](## "nell’entroterra") attack against the strategic town of Aqaba on the Red Sea. This famous victory made him a central figure in the Arab Revolt and dramatically increased his reputation in the British military. Then, when Damascus fell in October 1918, he was [instrumental](## "decisivo") in establishing an Arab government in the city under Faisal.


>I’m very [WARY](## "cauto, diffidente") of being stared at and discussed and [PRAISED](## "elogiare"). What can I do to be forgotten? After I’m dead, they’ll [RATTLE MY BONES ABOUT](## "smuovere, agitare") in their curiosity.




## A ROMANTIC HERO
In 1919, Lawrence attended the post-war Paris Peace Conference to [lobby](## "fare pressioni") for Arab independence. To his shock, he discovered that the British and French governments had already decided, in the secret Sykes-Picot Agreement, on the future of Turkey’s Arab territories, creating British-controlled and French-controlled zones. He felt the British had broken an assurance that Hussein would lead a new Arab kingdom.
At the same time, he was becoming a celebrity. The American journalist Lowell Thomas had visited the Middle East in 1918. He then created a sensationalised stage presentation of the Arab Revolt, which opened in New York and then London in 1919. Thomas [portrayed](## "ritrarre") Lawrence as a great romantic hero. The photos of Colonel Lawrence dressed as a Bedouin captivated the public. He [became a household name](## "diventare famoso"), a “matinée idol”, in his own words.
Soon, however, the public attention was too much. Looking for anonymity, he joined the Royal Air Force in 1922. His identity quickly [exposed](## "svelare"), he left to enter the Royal Tank Corps, before rejoining the RAF in 1925. One year later, he published his autobiography, Seven Pillars of [Wisdom](## "saggezza"), which would cement his place in popular culture.

## LAWRENCE’S LEGACY
Lawrence finally left the RAF in February 1935. Just three months later, riding his motorbike in a Dorset [country lane](## "stradina secondaria"), he lost control, crashed, and fractured his [skull](## "cranio"). He died six days later on 19 May, aged forty-six.
T.E. Lawrence was one of the most extraordinary figures of the 20th century, the most famous participant in the First World War. His actions helped to deter[mine](## "piazzare mine") the [outcome](## "risultato") of the war, while his influential role in the Arab Revolt strengthened the nascent feelings of independence which would eventually completely [reshape](## "rimodellare") the Middle East.

## A COMPLEX CHARACTER
Some biographers consider that [shame](## "vergogna") and a sense of being an outsider — the result of being illegitimate — marked Lawrence’s character for life. An introverted [loner](## "solitario") at university, he tested himself by going for long periods without food, drink or sleep. He also walked and cycled enormous distances, researching castles in France and Syria. 
When the war started, he quickly became a legendary figure, a born leader who [criss-crossed](## "percorrere in lungo e in largo") deserts and dressed and lived like a local fighter. Known as ‘al-Lorans’, he won the hearts of many Arabs, who often competed to accompany him on his missions. His intense [sense of duty](## "senso del dovere") and loyalty was combined with [self-doubt](## "insicurezza") and [guilt](## "senso di colpa") over the horrors of war.
He contributed to his own myth by inflating numbers in his life story: he [claimed](## "affermare") to have read fifty thousand books at university, of having [blown up](## "far esplodere") seventy-nine bridges and of having suffered sixty or more injuries. But he did receive British and French medals recognising his bravery. He was also offered a [knighthood](## "titolo di cavaliere") but rejected it, as he thought Britain had [betrayed](## "tradire") the Arabs. In fact, this [anger](## "rabbia") made him change his name, in 1927, to T. E. Shaw, looking for anonymity. 
Lawrence finally left the RAF in February 1935, alternating between optimism about future writing projects and a sense of emptiness. Death resolved this conflict. He died in a tragic accident, [flung over](## "essere scaraventato") the [handlebars](## "manubrio") of his beloved motorbike trying to avoid two young boys on their bicycles. His death so young [enhanced](## "aumentare") his mythical character even further.
The Lawrence of Arabia effigy by Eric Kennington at St. Martin’s Church, Wareham, Dorset.

## A MEDICAL LEGACY
In his fatal accident, Lawrence was not wearing a [helmet](## "casco"). One of the doctors attending him, while he [lay](## "giacere") in a coma, was one of Britain’s first neurosurgeons, the Australian Hugh Cairns. At the time he was researching head injuries suffered by motorcyclists. In October 1941, now an army doctor, he published his results on the importance of crash [helmet](## "casco")s in the British Medical Journal. At that time, the army was losing two motorcycle [despatch riders](## "messaggeri militari") a week in accidents. In November, the army made [helmet](## "casco")s obligatory. The number of deaths fell dramatically. Thirty-two years later, Parliament made [helmet](## "casco")s [compulsory](## "obbligatorio") for all motorbike riders. Sadly, Cairns had died by then, aged just fifty-six, in 1952, but his incredibly valuable work has saved [countless](## "innumerevoli") lives around the world.

Absolutely spectacular, surreal… [otherworldly](## "ultraterreno").” This is how the astronauts on Artemis II described what they saw when they became the first humans since Apollo 8 in 1968 to see [the far side of the Moon](## "la faccia nascosta della Luna") in its entirety. The four astronauts, Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover, Christina Koch and Jeremy Hansen, made history this year when they embarked on the adventure of a lifetime, [blasting off](## "decollare") from Florida to travel further into space than any human had ever gone before.
After flying almost 250,000 miles through the cosmos, they began their historic flight around [the far side of the Moon](## "la faccia nascosta della Luna"), [all the while](## "per tutto il tempo") communicating their observations in poetic terms to NASA’s Mission Control Center in Houston, Texas. “[Copy](## "ricevuto"), [Moon joy](## "gioia lunare"),” responded the Mission Control communicator Jacki Mahaffey, so [coining](## "coniare") a term to describe the emotional wonder of deep-space exploration. Reflecting on his experience, Wiseman said, “I’m not really a religious person, but there was just no other avenue for me to explain anything... I just [broke down in tears](## "scoppiare in lacrime"). It’s very hard to fully [grasp](## "comprendere") what we just went through.”
NASA astronaut and Artemis II mission specialist Christina Koch.
While orbiting [the far side of the Moon](## "la faccia nascosta della Luna"), the four astronauts experienced the indescribable wonder of not only seeing parts of the Moon never seen before, but also seeing a total solar eclipse, and our own planet [setting](## "nascondersi, tramontare") and [rising](## "sorgere, elevarsi") behind the lunar surface. “Humans probably have not evolved to see what we’re seeing,” said Glover. Wiseman agreed with this sentiment. “No matter how long we look at this, our brains are not processing this image in front of us,” he said.

## THE OVERVIEW EFFECT
The astronauts also reported experiencing a [shift](## "cambiamento") in consciousness, a phenomenon termed in 1987 by space philosopher Frank White as the [Overview Effect](## "effetto della veduta d’insieme"). Seeing our planet from space changes the way astronauts think about Earth and life. As Koch said, “You don’t see borders, you don’t see religious lines, you don’t see political boundaries. All you see is Earth and you see that we are [way more alike](## "molto più simili") than we are different.”

The Artemis II mission cost billions of dollars, involved highly advanced technology, and had an important scientific purpose. While on their ten-day journey through space, the astronauts had to perform important tasks to prepare for future missions. But it was their vivid and emotional descriptions that captivated people on Earth, demonstrating that despite the money, technology, and science involved, space exploration remains a fundamentally human experience.

## LUNAR FLYBY
From 1 April, when Artemis II blasted off from Earth, the astronauts began communicating through video and audio the very human side of space travel — the difficulty of living in a [cramped](## "angusto") spacecraft, in microgravity and with [limited supplies](## "scorte limitate"), while dealing with a malfunctioning toilet. But it was on Day 6, when they did the [lunar flyby](## "sorvolo lunare"), that things became highly emotional — and not just because of the incredible things they saw.

## ENJOY THE VIEW
That morning, the astronauts woke up to the song Good Morning, by Mandisa and TobyMac. And then they heard a special message, from Jim Lovell, one of the first three humans to orbit the Moon, on Apollo 8 in 1968. He recorded the message months before he died last August, aged ninety-seven. “Welcome to my old neighbourhood!” he said. “It’s a historic day, and I know how busy you’ll be. But don’t forget to enjoy the view.”
Science Officers in Mission Control.

## CARROLL CRATER
Later that day, the astronauts [wiped away](## "asciugare") tears as they made a special request to NASA. They asked that a lunar crater be named Carroll, in honour of Wiseman’s [late](## "defunta") wife and the mother of his two children, Carroll Taylor Wiseman, who died from cancer in 2020. The astronauts then embraced, and mission control observed a moment of silence. “It’s a [bright spot](## "punto luminoso") on the Moon, and we would like to call it Carroll,” Hansen said. The astronauts had also asked that another crater be named Integrity, in honor of the name they had given their spacecraft. “Integrity and Carroll crater, loud and clear,” said Mission Control communicator Jenni Gibbons in response.

## BRING THE WORLD TOGETHER
After returning to Earth on 10 April, Wiseman said the request to name a crater after his [late](## "defunta") wife was the “pinnacle moment of the mission”, when the astronauts were “the most [forged](## "forgiare"), the most [bonded](## "legare, unire").” All the astronauts agreed they were [bonded](## "legare, unire") forever by their experience in space. But the mission didn’t just bring them closer together, said Wiseman; it also brought the world closer together. “When we came home, we were shocked at the global [outpouring](## "ondata") of support, of pride, of [ownership](## "senso di appartenenza") of this mission. That’s what the four of us wanted. We wanted to go out and try to do something that would bring the world together.” And that, they surely did.

T. E. Lawrence: Fighting for Arabia [B2]

Lawrence d’Arabia fu uno dei soldati più famosi del XX secolo, tra i leader della lotta per l’indipendenza araba dall’Impero ottomano. Contribuì a plasmare la mappa del Medio Oriente così come la conosciamo oggi.

T. E. Lawrence was an inspirational soldier who played an important role in the Arab Revolt against the Ottoman Empire, of which the Arab population was a part, during World War One. The Ottoman Empire then encompassed many present-day Middle Eastern countries such as Turkey, Syria, Iraq, Israel, Palestine, Jordan, and parts of Saudi Arabia. Lawrence’s legendary wartime activities in the Arab fight for independence, especially his unconventional guerrilla methods, which would influence warfare in the future, made him famous around the world as Lawrence of Arabia.

SYMPATHY FOR THE ARABS

Thomas Edward Lawrence was born in 1888, the illegitimate son of Sir Thomas Chapman, an Anglo-Irish baronet, and Sarah Junner, a nanny. The couple never married, for legal reasons, adopting the surname Lawrence in an unsuccessful attempt to avoid scandal. They moved regularly before settling in Oxford.

All men dream, but not equally. The dreamers of the day are dangerous men, for they may act on their dreams, to make them possible. This I did.

After studying History at Oxford University, Lawrence worked as an archaeologist in Syria for the British Museum from 1910 to 1914. He learnt Arabic and developed a deep sympathy for the Arabs, who had lived under Ottoman rule for centuries and were now demanding independence. AN INTELLIGENCE OFFICER

When war began, with the Ottoman Empire deciding to support Germany, Lawrence was sent to work in the British Army intelligence unit in Cairo. The Arab Revolt began on 10 June 1916, when Sharif Hussein bin Ali, Emir of Mecca, declared independence, hoping to create a unified Arab nation. Britain supported the revolt, trying to destabilise the Ottoman front. Lawrence was sent to liaise with the forces of Faisal I of Iraq, Sharif Hussein’s son. People

GUERRILLA CAMPAIGN

In fact, Lawrence very quickly became involved in planning and leading a guerrilla campaign against the Ottoman forces, using hit-and-run tactics to mine railway lines and bridges, and disrupt communications and supply routes. He hoped Arab military success would lead to post-war self-government. His small but effective irregular forces tied down thousands of Turkish troops, preventing them from fighting against the regular Allied forces. Lawrence participated in several spectacular actions and was wounded numerous times. In July 1917, he led a 965-kilometre march across the desert to launch a surprise inland attack against the strategic town of Aqaba on the Red Sea. This famous victory made him a central figure in the Arab Revolt and dramatically increased his reputation in the British military. Then, when Damascus fell in October 1918, he was instrumental in establishing an Arab government in the city under Faisal.

A ROMANTIC HERO

In 1919, Lawrence attended the post-war Paris Peace Conference to lobby for Arab independence. To his shock, he discovered that the British and French governments had already decided, in the secret Sykes-Picot Agreement, on the future of Turkey’s Arab territories, creating British-controlled and French-controlled zones. He felt the British had broken an assurance that Hussein would lead a new Arab kingdom. At the same time, he was becoming a celebrity. The American journalist Lowell Thomas had visited the Middle East in 1918. He then created a sensationalised stage presentation of the Arab Revolt, which opened in New York and then London in 1919. Thomas portrayed Lawrence as a great romantic hero. The photos of Colonel Lawrence dressed as a Bedouin captivated the public. He became a household name, a “matinée idol”, in his own words. Soon, however, the public attention was too much. Looking for anonymity, he joined the Royal Air Force in 1922. His identity quickly exposed, he left to enter the Royal Tank Corps, before rejoining the RAF in 1925. One year later, he published his autobiography, Seven Pillars of Wisdom, which would cement his place in popular culture.

LAWRENCE’S LEGACY

Lawrence finally left the RAF in February 1935. Just three months later, riding his motorbike in a Dorset country lane, he lost control, crashed, and fractured his skull. He died six days later on 19 May, aged forty-six. T.E. Lawrence was one of the most extraordinary figures of the 20th century, the most famous participant in the First World War. His actions helped to determine the outcome of the war, while his influential role in the Arab Revolt strengthened the nascent feelings of independence which would eventually completely reshape the Middle East.

A COMPLEX CHARACTER

Some biographers consider that shame and a sense of being an outsider — the result of being illegitimate — marked Lawrence’s character for life. An introverted loner at university, he tested himself by going for long periods without food, drink or sleep. He also walked and cycled enormous distances, researching castles in France and Syria.  When the war started, he quickly became a legendary figure, a born leader who criss-crossed deserts and dressed and lived like a local fighter. Known as ‘al-Lorans’, he won the hearts of many Arabs, who often competed to accompany him on his missions. His intense sense of duty and loyalty was combined with self-doubt and guilt over the horrors of war. He contributed to his own myth by inflating numbers in his life story: he claimed to have read fifty thousand books at university, of having blown up seventy-nine bridges and of having suffered sixty or more injuries. But he did receive British and French medals recognising his bravery. He was also offered a knighthood but rejected it, as he thought Britain had betrayed the Arabs. In fact, this anger made him change his name, in 1927, to T. E. Shaw, looking for anonymity.  Lawrence finally left the RAF in February 1935, alternating between optimism about future writing projects and a sense of emptiness. Death resolved this conflict. He died in a tragic accident, flung over the handlebars of his beloved motorbike trying to avoid two young boys on their bicycles. His death so young enhanced his mythical character even further. The Lawrence of Arabia effigy by Eric Kennington at St. Martin’s Church, Wareham, Dorset.

A MEDICAL LEGACY

In his fatal accident, Lawrence was not wearing a helmet. One of the doctors attending him, while he lay in a coma, was one of Britain’s first neurosurgeons, the Australian Hugh Cairns. At the time he was researching head injuries suffered by motorcyclists. In October 1941, now an army doctor, he published his results on the importance of crash helmets in the British Medical Journal. At that time, the army was losing two motorcycle despatch riders a week in accidents. In November, the army made helmets obligatory. The number of deaths fell dramatically. Thirty-two years later, Parliament made helmets compulsory for all motorbike riders. Sadly, Cairns had died by then, aged just fifty-six, in 1952, but his incredibly valuable work has saved countless lives around the world.

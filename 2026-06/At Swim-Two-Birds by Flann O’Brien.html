“At Swim-Two-Birds” by Flann O’Brien [B2]

Un romanzo ironico, caotico, frammentato e di carattere metaletterario, scritto da un cinico studente di Dublino che perde il controllo dei suoi stessi personaggi.

Brian O’Nolan was a civil service official, novelist, playwright and satirist who wrote under a number of pen names, including that of Flann O’Brien. Born in Strabane, County Tyrone in 1911, O’Nolan — or Ó’Nualláin in the Irish language — became an influential figure in Ireland’s literary capital, Dublin. 

A COMIC MASTERPIECE

Originally published in 1939, O’Nolan’s best-known book is named after a ford on the river Shannon, the longest river in Ireland. Now considered a comic masterpiece, at the time At Swim-Two-Birds struggled to sell. Unsold copies languished in a London publisher’s warehouse until 1940, when a German air raid destroyed the warehouse and the five million books it contained.  O’Brien blamed the commercial failure of his novel on the raid and characteristically quipped that Hitler had hated his work so much he had started the Second World War to prevent it reaching a mass audience.

A COMPLICATED PLOT

In line with the Irish story-telling tradition, At Swim-Two-Birds is intentionally meandering and convoluted, as O’Nolan warns in the first paragraph: “One beginning and one ending for a book was a thing I did not agree with. A good book may have three openings entirely dissimilar and inter-related only in the presence of the author, or for that matter one hundred times as many endings.”   “L’idea che un libro dovesse avere un solo inizio e una sola fine, non mi convinceva. Un buon libro poteva avere tre inizi completamente diversi, collegati tra di loro soltanto nella prescienza dell’autore, e finire, se necessario, in trecento maniere diverse.” Limerick in southwestern Ireland, situated on the Shannon River in the province of Munster. King John’s Castle, located on King’s Island, dates to the 13th century. The novel’s title is a literal translation of Snámh Dá Éan, an historic ford on the Shannon River.

UNRELIABLE NARRATOR

Its unnamed narrator is an Irish literature student who lives with his uncle and spends most of his time drinking and juggling stories in his head. One of these involves a writer called Dermot Trellis, who is himself writing a book. The book thus creates stories within stories, combining autobiography, fantasy, farce, and linguistic observations. There is no doubt that O’Brien’s confusing narrative is deliberate. To help his readers keep up, the author/narrator includes a number of interludes explaining what just happened. “Synopsis, being a summary of what has gone before, FOR THE BENEFIT OF NEW READERS.” “Sinossi, ossia riassunto delle puntate precedenti, PER I NUOVI LETTORI.”

REBEL CHARACTERS

Readers can join the story wherever they like, as if they were starting a series from a central episode. There are regular descriptions of each character, with the narrator providing page numbers that refer back to summaries. The characters in the book rebel against their student author, drugging him to sleep so that they can have agency over their own fictional lives. All this is punctuated by visits from two cowboys, as well as the mythical Irish hero Finn McCool, and the Pooka (a magical human devil called Fergus MacPhellimey.) The characters frequently launch into long poems or songs. Classic Books

“To The Lighthouse” by Virginia Woolf Una semplice gita in barca viene ostacolata dal maltempo, dal destino e dalla guerra. Ma la trama è solo il pretesto per parlare delle emozioni, dei ricordi e dei pensieri più intimi dei protagonisti. Julian Earwaker, Molly Malcolm, Sarah Davison

POETIC TO PROSAIC

Some of the most comic moments occur when the narrator is describing his uncle. Detailed descriptions of domestic life can suddenly switch from poetic to prosaic. On one occasion the uncle invites a friend from his choir, Mr. Corcoran, home with him to listen to some opera music on a gramophone. At first, the two men sing along with the music, then suddenly… “Mr. Corcoran […] smiled preliminarily but when about to speak, his smile was transfixed in his features and his entire body assumed a stiff attitude. Suddenly he sneezed, spattering his clothing with a mucous discharge from his nostrils.” “Mr. Corcoran […] fece un sorriso preliminare; ma quando si accingeva già a parlare, il sorriso si trasfigurò sul suo volto e tutto il suo corpo prese un atteggiamento rigido. A un tratto starnutì, schizzando sui suoi vestiti una scarica di muco uscita dalle sue narici.”

UNIVERSAL PRAISE

Readers who manage to keep track of the intertwined plot threads are rewarded with many comic twists, and a strangely poignant end.  Prominent writers of the time, including Graham Greene and Anthony Burgess, all praised O’Nolan’s book. James Joyce found it in “true comic spirit!” Another admirer was Argentine author Jorge Luis Borges, no stranger to highly-imaginative plots of his own. On reading it, he declared, “I have enumerated many verbal labyrinths, but none so complex as the recent book by Flann O’Brien, At Swim-Two-Birds.”

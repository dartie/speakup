# Save the Microbes   [C1]

La biologia della conservazione sta affrontando la sua sfida più ardua: proteggere la diversità dei batteri e degli altri microbi presenti sulla Terra.

Hundreds of scientists have joined together to save a group of species from extinction, a group that might not seem like it needs saving: microbes.
Microbes need protection for many reasons, researchers say, including the fact that other species — indeed, entire ecosystems — depend on their [well-being](## "benessere"). “We need them in order to help conserve the pandas and the [rainforests](## "foreste pluviali") and the whales and the oceans and [everything else in between](## "tutto il resto"),” said Jack Gilbert, a microbiologist at the University of California, San Diego.
We are living through an extinction crisis, but nearly all of the extinctions conservation biologists have definitively documented so far have involved animals or plants. This month, the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN), which publishes a list of [endangered](## "in pericolo di estinzione") and vulnerable species, announced the newest additions, including the [slender-billed curlew](## "slender-billed curlew"), a migratory [shorebird](## "uccello costiero") and an [ebony-like](## "simile all’ebano") tree called Diospyros angulata.
Humans are driving these species to extinction by hunting them, destroying [wetlands](## "zone umide") and other habitats, cutting down forests and heating the planet. But as powerful as these pressures are, extinction is not inevitable. The IUCN also announced this month that the [green sea turtle](## "tartaruga verde") had [rebounded](## "riprendersi"), thanks to measures such as protecting the eggs the animals lay on beaches. The organization has officially changed the status of the species from [endangered](## "in pericolo di estinzione") to [least concern](## "preoccupazione minore").
But animals and plants represent only a fraction of the biological diversity on the planet. [Fungi](## "funghi") — which are a separate lineage from animals and plants — include millions, or perhaps even tens of millions, of species. But IUCN scientists started [assessing](## "valutare") their status only a decade ago, and so far they’ve evaluated just 1,300 species. That’s enough species to reveal that fungi are also gravely threatened. One-third of those 1,300 species are at risk of extinction.
A  Zoanthus polyps colony aqua-cultured in a coral reef aquarium tank
Now IUCN researchers are [casting an even wider net](## "ampliare il raggio d’azione"): they’re [setting out](## "proporsi") to protect the world’s diversity of bacteria and other microbes.
This may be the most [daunting](## "impegnativo") effort in the history of conservation. Microbes make up most of the genetic diversity on Earth, but microbiologists have [barely](## "a malapena") begun cataloguing them. [All told](## "in totale"), there may be one hundred billion species of microbes.
Making the task even harder, microbes are largely invisible to the naked eye; even under a microscope, two distinct species may look identical. And microbes live just about everywhere on Earth, in clouds miles overhead and rocks miles underground.
Even without documenting every microbe species, biologists can see that microbial diversity is under threat. [Soil](## "suolo") may contain half of all microbial species, for instance, but as forests are cut down and [grasslands](## "praterie") are converted to farm fields, much of that soil is destroyed, and some of its diversity of microbes is lost.
The Microbial Conservation Specialist Group, as the newly-formed group is known, plans to map [hot spots](## "zone chiave") for microbial conservation around the world, including [bare stretches](## "distese spoglie") of [rocky earth](## "suolo sassoso") in Antarctica and the insides of animals and plants. Samples will be collected, placed in [vaults](## "camere blindate") safe from extinction, and studied.

The specialist group will also help make plans to save the ecosystems where these microbes live. Conserving the microbes in [coral reefs](## "barriere coralline"), for example, could greatly benefit the people who live near them. Healthy [coral reefs](## "barriere coralline") break ocean waves, protecting coasts from flooding. They also serve as [nurseries](## "zone di riproduzione") for fish. Coral reefs, which [harbour](## "accogliere") one-third of all marine species, are considered the [rainforests](## "foreste pluviali") of the ocean.
But the coral animals that build the reefs can’t survive on their own. They depend on a coral microbiome, a collection of species that help them [break down](## "scomporre") their food and [fight off](## "combattere") pathogens.
Preserving microbes in other habitats could also enable them to provide us with their own services. Deserts and arid lands are [topped](## "coprire") with fragile microbial [crusts](## "croste") that pull carbon dioxide out of the air, for example. The more microbes that can be saved, the more work they can do to slow climate change.

June is peak season for roses. This much‑loved flower is especially dear to the English, so much so that it has become a national symbol. And it all began with a civil war that lasted thirty years.

THE WARS OF THE ROSES

The Wars of the Roses were a series of civil wars (1455-1485) in which the House of Lancaster — whose emblem was a red rose — and the House of York — whose emblem was a white rose — fought for the succession to the English throne. Henry Tudor, leader of the Lancasters, defeated King Richard III at the Battle of Bosworth, became king, and married Elizabeth of York. To symbolise this new peace between the two rival houses, Henry VII adopted the Tudor Rose as an emblem of harmony. Culture

CELEBRATIONS

As spring turns to summer, gardens across England burst into life with the colours and scents of roses. This is the perfect time to step outdoors — perhaps by visiting a botanical garden — and admire the many varieties of this iconic flower. June is packed with rose‑themed events. One of the highlights of the month is the Festival of Roses at RHS Garden Rosemoor, organised by the Royal Horticultural Society in the south‑west of England, near Great Torrington in North Devon. Here, visitors can enjoy roses both in bud and in full bloom, in two stunning gardens dedicated entirely to roses. At Borde Hill Garden in Haywards Heath, West Sussex, visitors can admire Jay Robin’s Rose Garden, home to over 750 David Austin English roses. These roses, a type of rose developed in England in the late 20th century by rose breeder David Austin — who combined old garden roses with modern varieties — are especially prized for their strong fragrance, soft colours and traditional shape. More than one hundred different varieties can be seen here, making it a must-visit for rose lovers. Meanwhile, Hever Castle in Kent hosts its annual Hever in Bloom event. It’s the perfect opportunity to explore the castle and gardens once owned by Anne Boleyn’s family. The event includes a tour of the Rose Garden, where more than five thousand roses create a dazzling display. And if you’re in London, don’t miss a stroll through Kew Gardens, where the stunning Rose Garden is home to around 170 different species and varieties of roses. ALEXANDRA ROSE DAY

For the English, the rose isn’t just a beautiful flower: it’s also a symbol of charity and social commitment. Alexandra Rose Day is a long‑standing British tradition, a fundraising event established in 1912 by Queen Alexandra to celebrate the 50th anniversary of her arrival in the UK for her marriage to the future King Edward VII. The Queen wanted to raise funds for London hospitals by selling silk and paper roses. In its first year, the event raised an astonishing £32,000 — a huge sum at the time. Today, the charity raises over £1.5 million a year, helping low-income families buy fresh fruit and vegetables.

THE TUDOR ROSE

The Tudor Rose emblem is made up of two roses, a red one and a white one, with five petals each, a golden centre and green leaves. Over the centuries, it has appeared on jewellery, coins, and royal badges, and it can be seen carved into historic buildings like Westminster Abbey.

Near Wall Street, at the southern end of Manhattan’s Financial District and just a short distance from the New York Stock Exchange, stands one of the most famous symbols of global finance, the Charging Bull, by the Italian artist Arturo Di Modica. Cast in bronze and weighing approximately 3,200kg, it is one of the most photographed statues in New York City. Since its installation in 1989, investors and visitors alike often touch it, believing this action to bring good luck. The statue has become a visual icon of the energy and drive of the stock market.

AND BEARS

In finance, a ‘bull’ is an investor who believes that the stock market, or a specific stock, is likely to rise in value, and buys with the expectation of selling later at a higher price. The opposite of a ‘bull’ is a ‘bear’: an investor who believes that the value of a specific security or industry is likely to decline in the future. For this reason, a ‘bull market’ is a term used to describe a situation in which markets are rising or expected to rise, signalling confidence and growth, while a ‘bear market’ describes the opposite condition, marked by falling prices, negative sentiment, and expectations of further decline. Charging Bull by Arturo Di Modica. Born in Sicily in 1941, Di Modica arrived in the US in 1970. The 3200-kilogram sculpture cost him $360,000 to make.

ORIGINS?

These expressions are rather informal and do not belong to strictly technical financial language; however, they are very common and refer not only to the stock market, but also to other markets, such as the real estate and currency markets. The male bovine and the ursid carnivore became visual representations of how financial markets behave in reference to the two different ways of attacking used by these two animals. A bull strikes upward with its horns, and is therefore associated with prices that go up. A bear, on the other hand, attacks with a downward swipe, which is why it is linked to prices that go down. According to some, there may also be a connection with another behaviour of the bear: its habit of hibernating, referring to the fact that during periods of falling prices investors often slow down their activity. CAN WE MEASURE THEM?

The expressions ‘bull market’ and ‘bear market’ are documented as early as the 18th century in the first London financial markets, although their origin is not entirely clear. They are not scientifically measurable; however, we generally speak of a ‘bull market’ when there is about a 20 per cent increase, and of a ‘bear market’ when there is about a 20 per cent decline. Bull and bear markets alternate in a continuous cycle, with some extreme cases. For instance, a clear example of a bull market was the economic boom after World War Two, while examples of bear markets include the Great Depression of 1929 and the 2008 financial crisis following the collapse of financial services firm Lehman Brothers.

SYMBOL OF RESILIENCE

Arturo Di Modica installed his sculpture secretly overnight as a gift to the city after the 1987 market crash, to symbolise the resilience of Americans and their ability to recover from a crisis. The police removed it, but it had become so popular that it was reinstalled shortly afterwards.

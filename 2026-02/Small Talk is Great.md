# Small Talk is Great   [C1]

Alla macchinetta del caffè o con il passeggero che ci è seduto accanto in aereo: una chiacchiera, seppur superficiale, aiuta a creare un’intesa.

People love to [complain](## "lamentarsi") about [small talk](## "chiacchiere, parlare del più e del meno"). Some say it’s “painful”, “dishonest” and “a [chore](## "seccatura, incombenza").” Some of my own friends have called it “boring” and “exhausting.” A 2016 Wired magazine article titled “Small talk should be [banned](## "vietare")” argued that [idle](## "inutile") [chit-chat](## "chiacchierata") “does not build relationships and does not make us happier”, but persists because “we actively seek the lowest common denominator.” Instead, the authors suggest deeper conversation topics, such as: “What is your relationship with God?” or “What is something you fear in life?”
I’m here to say, respectfully: all of this is wrong.
First, I find the anti-[small talk](## "chiacchiere, parlare del più e del meno") position arrogant, marked by a sense that the [holder](## "sostenitore") is more [thoughtfully](## "sapientemente, in modo riflessivo") and deeply [engaged](## "impegnato") with life than everyone else. When I was in [middle school](## "scuola media"), I knew a kid who only listened to inaccessible goth rock and [dismissed](## "ignorare, respingere") anyone who listened to the Top 40 as [shallow](## "superficiale") and stupid. That’s how [small talk](## "chiacchiere, parlare del più e del meno") haters sound to me: “You want to talk about the weather? Uh, ever heard of mortality?!”
Sure, [small talk](## "chiacchiere, parlare del più e del meno") sucks sometimes. I once got trapped talking to someone for thirty minutes about [pencil lead](## "mina della matita"). But you can’t always expect everyone to meet you in the [mucky](## "sudicie") depths of human existence. Maybe the person you’re talking to had a long day and still has to go home and make dinner and put their kids to sleep. You’re going to demand they reflect on what happens after we die? Everyone’s just trying to [get through the day](## "arrivare a fine giornata").
“I find [small talk](## "chiacchiere, parlare del più e del meno") incredibly useful,” says Lizzie Post, co-president of the Emily Post Institute, which provides etiquette advice and training. She describes [small talk](## "chiacchiere, parlare del più e del meno") as a “safety zone” for people to be in together. Religion, sex and politics are all excellent conversation topics, she says, but they may not feel accessible or comfortable to everyone all the time. Chatting about last night’s football game, the food at an event or someone’s hobbies is a much easier way to build initial [rapport](## "intesa, sintonia"). Then, if the other person seems open, you can [dip your toe](## "tastare il terreno") into deeper topics.
A couple of years ago, for example, a man in his late sixties sat next to me on a plane and we started chatting. We said hello and exchanged [niceties](## "convenevoli, carinerie") about the weather (hot) and why we were travelling (to see family.) We talked about work and recent vacations, and by the end of the two-hour flight, I knew all about his various [career pivots](## "cambi di carriera"), and how psilocybin had transformed his life and saved his marriage. Interesting! But we only got there because we started with [small talk](## "chiacchiere, parlare del più e del meno"). Had I opened with, “What is your experience with psychedelics and how have they affected your relationships?” that might have been [off-putting](## "sgradevole").

Post suspects some people don’t like [small talk](## "chiacchiere, parlare del più e del meno") because it doesn’t always allow them to be the star of the show — if you ask someone about their hobbies, then you have to listen to what they say. This can be challenging for those who prefer to be the talker. “Some people want to be seen in a certain way, and don’t feel like [small talk](## "chiacchiere, parlare del più e del meno") gets them to be seen in that interesting way,” she says.
And if you think [small talk](## "chiacchiere, parlare del più e del meno") is boring, that may be a skill issue. It requires a real [back-and-forth](## "botta e risposta"), even if it doesn’t [plumb](## "scandagliare") the depths of someone’s soul. Listening attentively can significantly improve the quality of your [chit-chat](## "chiacchierata"), Post says. “Have your radar up,” she explains. “Pay attention to whether the other person seems interested, and if they’ve contributed anything to the conversation. If you’ve been talking for a long time and the other person hasn’t said anything on the topic, they’re probably bored. Maybe their eyes [have glazed over](## "offuscare") a little bit,” Post says. “I would take it as a sign to change the topic, and ask them something about themselves.” She suggests a question like: “What’s keeping you interested these days?”
Yes, [small talk](## "chiacchiere, parlare del più e del meno") can be [shallow](## "superficiale"). But consider this: sitting in a [kiddie pool](## "piscina per bambini") and [sunning yourself](## "prendere il sole") while [sipping](## "sorseggiare") a soda is far more relaxing than [shivering](## "rabbrividire") in the open ocean. Shallow can be [delightful](## "delizioso"). So [go forth](## "andare avanti, procedere"), and [gab](## "chiacchierare") about public transit and a new restaurant you heard was pretty good. But if someone brings up [pencil lead](## "mina della matita"), just pretend you have to take a phone call.
